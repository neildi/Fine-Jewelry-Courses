# Role-Play Libraries and Scenario Banks

A trainer who has read Module 2 knows to put practice into the huddle instead of a classroom. But "practice" without a bank of ready scenarios means every manager improvises a different role-play, at a different difficulty, covering a different objection, every single week. Some associates get real practice; others get whatever the manager thought of on the spot. A scenario bank turns practice from a random event into a repeatable system.

## Why This Matters

The underlying skill this module organizes already has a name and a home in this catalog: C2's sales-conversation course names "the eight most common objections (price, 'just looking,' 'I'll think about it,' online comparison, lab-grown, financing hesitation, partner approval, competitor quote)" as the fixed set every associate needs to handle, and closes with "role-play scripts and a weekly practice cadence." INSTORE's own trade guidance for sales-floor fundamentals likewise builds in "a weekly roleplay cadence" as a named discipline, not an occasional extra. A scenario bank is the tool that makes that named cadence actually deliverable, week after week, without a manager reinventing the objection list from memory.

## What a Scenario Bank Actually Contains

A scenario bank is not a script to memorize word-for-word — it is a structured list of situations, each with enough detail that any manager can run it cold, and enough flexibility that no two role-plays feel identical. At minimum, each entry needs four things: the client's opening line, the underlying objection or dynamic it represents, what a strong response demonstrates, and a scoring note for the observing manager.

| Scenario | Client opener | Underlying dynamic | What a strong response demonstrates |
|---|---|---|---|
| Price objection | "It's beautiful, but that's a lot for a ring." | Price vs. value framing | Names the value before addressing price, doesn't discount reflexively |
| "Just looking" | "We're just browsing today." | Early-stage discovery resistance | Opens a low-pressure discovery question instead of retreating |
| Partner approval | "I need to check with my husband first." | Two-decision-maker dynamic | Offers a concrete next step (hold, photos, follow-up) instead of pressuring |
| Competitor quote | "I saw this cheaper somewhere else." | Price-matching pressure | Reframes on service, sourcing, or warranty rather than matching blindly |

This table draws directly on the eight-objection set already defined in C2 — a trainer building a scenario bank for a jewelry sales floor should start by turning each of those eight named objections into one row like this, rather than inventing a new objection taxonomy from scratch.

## Building Difficulty Levels Into the Bank

A scenario bank that only contains easy, textbook versions of each objection stops being useful once associates get comfortable. Each scenario should exist in at least two difficulty tiers: a clean version for new hires still learning the basic response, and a complicated version (an impatient client, a genuine budget constraint, a return customer with history) for associates ready to handle real-floor messiness.

**Trainer (building tier two of the price-objection scenario):** "Tier one: the client says the price is a lot, plain and simple. Tier two: the client says it's a lot, mentions they just bought a car, and seems ready to walk. The tier-two response has to hold the value conversation under real pressure, not just recite the same line calmly."

Without this tiering, a scenario bank trains associates to handle objections only in their easiest form — exactly the form they're least likely to encounter once they're experienced enough to be trusted with high-value clients.

## Running the Weekly Cadence From the Bank

The weekly practice cadence C2 calls for and INSTORE documents as trade practice needs a simple rotation rule so the same scenario doesn't get stale and the whole bank gets used across a quarter: one scenario per week, rotating through the full bank before repeating, with tier selection based on who's practicing.

**Manager (opening a weekly practice rotation):** "This week's scenario is the competitor-quote objection, tier one for our newer associates and tier two for anyone who's been here more than six months. Same format as always: thirty seconds each way, I'm watching for whether you reframe on value instead of just matching the price."

A rotation this simple, run from a pre-built bank, takes a manager under a minute to set up — the bank did the design work in advance so the weekly practice session doesn't require fresh preparation every time.

## Objections, Mistakes and Edge Cases

**Writing scenarios as scripts to be memorized.** A role-play that becomes a word-for-word script trains associates to sound rehearsed, not responsive. Write scenarios around the dynamic and the objection, and let the associate's own language handle the delivery.

**Only building scenarios for sales objections.** A scenario bank can and should cover other observable behaviors this catalog defines — clienteling data capture, repair-intake conversations, financing presentations — not just the eight sales objections, if those are also targeted training outcomes.

**Letting the bank go stale.** A scenario bank built once and never revisited eventually stops matching real client behavior on the floor (new competitor tactics, new financing questions, a shift toward lab-grown inquiries). Review and refresh the bank on the same cadence used for the curriculum roadmap in Module 7 of this course.

**Skipping the scoring note.** A role-play without a stated observation point turns into entertainment rather than assessment — every scenario needs the one-sentence "what a strong response demonstrates" line so the manager knows specifically what to watch for, not just whether the exchange felt natural.

## On the Floor: Applying It This Week

1. Build the first four rows of your scenario bank using C2's eight named objections as the starting list, with opener, dynamic, and success indicator for each.
2. Write a tier-two (harder) version for at least two of your highest-priority scenarios.
3. Set a simple rotation: one scenario per week, cycling through the full bank before repeating.
4. Add a one-sentence scoring note to every scenario so managers know exactly what to listen for during the role-play.
5. Schedule a quarterly review of the bank against what associates are actually hearing on the floor, and retire or rewrite any scenario that's stopped matching reality.
