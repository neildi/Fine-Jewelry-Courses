# Microlearning and Huddle Content

Module 2 of this course established that the huddle is where training practice belongs. This module is about the content itself: what a piece of training material has to look like to actually fit inside a five-to-ten-minute huddle slot, rather than being a full module awkwardly compressed to fit a short window.

## Why This Matters

The industry's own free-resource providers have already converged on short-format training as the standard for sales-floor delivery. The Natural Diamond Council's Diamond Learning Center delivers its content as "30 short videos" and "4 self-paced modules," with individual videos running "2-5 min" each. The De Beers Group Institute of Diamonds free retailer app is built entirely around "self-paced micro-learning" — videos, podcasts, games, and "fast facts" designed for exactly the kind of short, interruption-tolerant format a jewelry sales floor requires. When the two largest diamond-industry education providers both build their flagship free retailer training in short-form units, that is a strong signal about what format actually survives contact with the floor, not just a stylistic preference.

## What Makes Content Huddle-Ready

Huddle-ready content has three properties that most training material doesn't start with: it teaches exactly one idea, it can be delivered verbally or with a single visual in under two minutes, and it ends with something the listener does immediately rather than something they remember later. A 45-minute deck about the 4Cs is not huddle-ready. "Today's fact: fluorescence isn't a flaw, it's a trait some clients specifically look for — the next time someone asks about a blue glow under UV light, say that" is.

| Not huddle-ready | Huddle-ready |
|---|---|
| "Here's everything about diamond clarity" (15+ minutes) | "One clarity fact and one line to say about it" (90 seconds) |
| A slide deck requiring a screen | A single sentence a manager can read aloud from a card |
| Ends with "any questions?" | Ends with "say this the next time it comes up today" |
| Covers a whole topic | Covers one fact, one script line, or one behavior cue |

## The Huddle Card as the Native Format

This project's own production system already has a purpose-built format for this: the huddle card, defined in the Prompt Kit as "a one-page huddle card for a store manager to read aloud in a 5-minute morning huddle: 1 headline, 3 key points, 1 script to practice today, 1 question to ask the team." Every module in this catalog already produces one of these as a required production artifact — which means a trainer building microlearning content for the huddle doesn't need to invent a new format. The huddle card already is the microlearning unit; the job is to make sure every module's huddle card genuinely fits the one-fact, one-script, one-question standard, rather than being a miniature version of the full article crammed onto one page.

**Trainer (reviewing a huddle card draft for huddle-readiness):** "This has four key points and two scripts. Cut it down to the single most important point and the one script that matters most this week — the rest can wait for a future huddle."

A huddle card that tries to compress an entire module into one page usually ends up too dense to actually read aloud in five minutes; the discipline is choosing the single most important piece, not summarizing everything.

## Sequencing Microlearning Across a Week

A single huddle card delivers one idea once. The five-day sequence built in Module 2 of this course is what turns a series of individual huddle cards into a week of reinforced learning — introduce a fact or script on day one, have it practiced on day two, applied on day three, reported back on day four, and reinforced on day five. Each day's card should be genuinely short on its own; the depth comes from the sequence repeating and building, not from any single card trying to cover everything at once.

## Objections, Mistakes and Edge Cases

**Writing a huddle card that's secretly a full article.** If a manager can't read it aloud comfortably in under two minutes, it has too much in it — cut content rather than asking managers to read faster or skip sections.

**Treating microlearning as lower-quality training.** Short format is not the same as low rigor — the NDC and De Beers examples show major industry education providers building their primary retailer training as short-form content, not as a fallback for when longer training isn't available.

**Using microlearning for content that genuinely needs depth.** A completely new grading standard or a significant compliance change may need the longer-format explanation described in Module 2's objections section — microlearning works for reinforcement and single-fact delivery, not for introducing genuinely complex material for the first time.

**Forgetting the "say this today" line.** A huddle card without a specific script or action to practice immediately is trivia, not training — the one-line call to action is what separates a fun fact from a behavior change attempt.

## On the Floor: Applying It This Week

1. Pull three of your existing huddle cards and time how long it takes to read each one aloud — flag any that run over two minutes.
2. Rewrite one overloaded huddle card down to a single headline, three key points, one script, and one question, cutting everything else.
3. Check that every huddle card you've built ends with a specific, immediately actionable script line, not just a fact to remember.
4. Build a five-card sequence for one topic, following the introduce-practice-apply-report-reinforce structure from Module 2.
5. Compare your card format against the NDC and De Beers short-video model — if your typical delivery time is closer to fifteen minutes than two, look for what to cut.
