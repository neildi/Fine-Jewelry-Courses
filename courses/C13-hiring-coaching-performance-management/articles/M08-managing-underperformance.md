# Managing Underperformance

## Why This Module Matters

Module 7 built the evidence-based documentation habit that runs continuously through every associate's performance. This module covers what happens when that evidence starts showing a genuine, sustained pattern of underperformance rather than a normal, isolated slip — the specific, structured process a store leader follows to give an associate a real chance to improve while protecting the store, its team, and its legal position if the situation eventually requires a difficult decision.

## Progressive Discipline: A Structured Sequence, Not a Single Jump to Termination

The standard framework across HR and employment-law guidance is progressive discipline: a structured sequence of increasingly formal steps, typically four, that a manager works through in order rather than jumping straight to termination the first time a real performance problem surfaces. The most commonly cited sequence: a verbal warning (a direct, documented conversation naming the specific issue and what needs to change), a written warning (a formal document if the issue persists or is serious enough to warrant skipping straight past the verbal stage), a performance improvement plan or final written warning (a more structured, time-bound document representing a final attempt to resolve the issue), and termination if the pattern continues despite this process. The core principle behind this sequence, cited consistently across multiple sources: act early, before poor performance becomes a habit, and give the associate a genuine, fair opportunity to improve before the process escalates further.

## What a Performance Improvement Plan Actually Contains

A performance improvement plan (PIP), when the process reaches that stage, is a formal written document with five specific components that appear consistently across multiple independent HR sources: a clear statement of company expectations and the specific performance standard the role requires, a factual overview of the actual performance issue (using exactly the SBI structure and dated evidence-log entries this course's Module 7 already builds), specific, measurable goals the associate needs to hit (written as SMART goals — specific, measurable, achievable, relevant, and time-bound), a defined follow-up schedule of regular check-in meetings during the plan period, and a clear statement of consequences if the goals aren't met. The typical timeframe cited across multiple sources runs 60 to 90 days, long enough to give a genuine, fair chance at improvement without leaving the underlying problem unresolved indefinitely.

## Documentation That Actually Holds Up: What to Avoid

A specific, repeatedly emphasized legal and HR principle: documentation should describe the specific performance problem, never the person's character, and should avoid vague, subjective phrases entirely. "Bad attitude," "not a culture fit," and "needs to improve" (with no further detail) are specifically named across multiple sources as exactly the kind of documentation that fails to hold up — too subjective to defend, and providing the associate no actual coaching on what to change. The specific standard several sources describe: documentation should be detailed enough that a third party with no prior knowledge of the situation could read it and understand exactly what the problem was and what improvement was expected. This is the direct, high-stakes extension of Module 7's behavior-not-personality principle — the same discipline that makes a performance review useful also makes underperformance documentation legally defensible.

## Every Criticism Needs a Coaching Path and a Deadline

A specific, well-corroborated pairing: every documented performance criticism should come with specific coaching on how to improve and a clear deadline by which the employer expects that improvement. A document that only states what went wrong, without stating what "better" specifically looks like or by when, leaves an associate with no real path forward and leaves the store's own process looking punitive rather than genuinely corrective. This directly extends Module 5's GROW-model coaching content and Module 7's forward-plan structure into this more formal, higher-stakes context — the same principle, applied at the point where the stakes are highest.

## Two-Way Communication and Signed Acknowledgment

Two further, consistently recommended disciplines: the process should allow genuine two-way communication, giving the associate a real opportunity to discuss the issue and offer their own perspective rather than simply receiving a verdict, and every formal document in the process (a written warning, a PIP) should be signed and dated by the associate to confirm they've seen and discussed it — not necessarily agreement with its content, but confirmation that the conversation happened. Documentation should also note explicitly that this two-way conversation occurred, since a record showing only the manager's side of an exchange is weaker evidence than a record showing the associate had a genuine chance to respond.

## A Note on Legal Guidance

This module describes widely-cited HR and management best practices for progressive discipline and documentation. Employment law varies significantly by jurisdiction (including at-will employment rules, required notice periods, and protected-class considerations), and specific legal requirements should always be confirmed with qualified employment counsel or an HR professional before finalizing any store-specific disciplinary policy or acting on a specific termination decision. This module is a practical operations guide, not legal advice.

## Putting the Vocabulary to Work

The complete underperformance-management sequence this module establishes: recognize a genuine, sustained pattern (not an isolated slip) using the evidence log Module 7 already maintains; work through progressive discipline in order (verbal warning, written warning, PIP/final warning, termination) rather than skipping steps without good reason; if a PIP becomes necessary, build it with all five required components and a 60-90 day timeframe; document every step using specific behavior and impact, never character judgments, detailed enough for a third party to understand; pair every criticism with specific coaching and a deadline; and ensure genuine two-way conversation happens and gets documented, with signed acknowledgment at each formal stage.

## A Worked Scenario: The Documentation That Protected Everyone

An associate's performance has genuinely declined over two months — missed customer follow-ups, several coaching debriefs (Module 5) noting the same specific gap in closing technique, and one-on-one notes (Module 6) showing the associate acknowledging the issue without meaningful change. Rather than writing a vague "performance concerns" memo, the manager pulls the specific dated entries from the evidence log, states the specific behavior pattern and its impact on sales results, and issues a written warning naming the exact standard expected, specific coaching already provided, and a 30-day window before the next step. The associate signs, acknowledging the conversation occurred, and the manager schedules biweekly check-ins. When the pattern doesn't improve and the process eventually reaches a difficult final decision, the documentation — specific, dated, behavior-focused, and showing genuine coaching support offered throughout — gives both the associate and the store a clear, fair record of exactly what happened and what chances were given.

## Key Takeaways

- Progressive discipline is a structured, typically four-step sequence (verbal warning, written warning, PIP/final warning, termination) that a manager works through in order, giving an associate a genuine, fair opportunity to improve before the process escalates.
- A performance improvement plan has five specific components confirmed consistently across multiple sources: expectations, a factual performance overview (using Module 7's SBI structure), SMART goals, a defined check-in schedule, and stated consequences, typically over a 60-90 day timeframe.
- Documentation must describe the specific behavior, never the person's character, avoiding vague phrases like "bad attitude" entirely — the standard is detailed enough that a third party with no prior knowledge could understand exactly what happened.
- Every documented criticism needs a specific coaching path and a clear deadline attached, directly extending Module 5's coaching content and Module 7's forward-plan structure into this higher-stakes context.
- Genuine two-way communication and signed, dated acknowledgment at each formal stage are both essential: they confirm the associate had a real opportunity to respond, and they protect both the associate and the store with a clear, fair record of exactly what happened.
