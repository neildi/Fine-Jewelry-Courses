# Weekly One-on-Ones

## Why This Module Matters

Module 5 built the quick, five-minute debrief that happens right after a customer interaction. This module builds the different, longer conversation that happens on a fixed weekly schedule regardless of what happened on the floor that day — the structured check-in where an associate's priorities, obstacles, and development get real, protected attention rather than being squeezed into whatever moment a manager happens to have free.

## Weekly Is the Evidence-Backed Default, With a Real Nuance

Gallup research cited across multiple sources finds that employees who have regular one-on-one meetings with their manager are nearly three times as likely to be engaged at work as those who don't, and a genuinely well-corroborated finding across HR and management research points to weekly, 30-minute one-on-ones as the single most consistently favored cadence, rated most desirable by employees and correlated with the highest engagement levels in comparative studies. A more recent, careful synthesis of this same research area adds a genuinely useful nuance worth taking seriously rather than treating weekly-30-minutes as a rigid universal rule: the underlying research doesn't actually support a single fixed cadence for every employee in every situation. Gallup's own coaching framework specifically recommends a brief "quick connect" of one to ten minutes at least weekly, paired with a more substantive quarter-hour-to-half-hour conversation once or twice a month, with the exact mix adjusted for team needs, role complexity, and how much active coaching a given employee currently needs. A new hire still in their 30-60-90 day window (Module 4), or an associate working through a specific coaching focus (Module 5), genuinely benefits from a weekly cadence; an experienced, steady performer may be well served by a biweekly rhythm with brief informal check-ins between.

## The Employee Sets the Agenda, Not Just the Manager

A specific, well-corroborated structural principle: the one-on-one is employee-driven, not manager-driven. The employee prepares and sends the agenda in advance, leaving room for topics the manager needs to raise, rather than the manager unilaterally running through a status-update checklist. This isn't a minor formatting preference — it directly determines whether the meeting actually surfaces what the associate most needs to discuss, or only what the manager happened to think to ask about. A manager who always drives the agenda tends to hear about problems only once they're already serious, since an associate waiting to be asked the right question may simply never get asked it.

## The Five Recurring Areas a Good Agenda Covers

Across multiple independent one-on-one frameworks, five recurring content areas show up consistently, whoever sets the specific agenda in a given week: current priorities (what's actually being worked on and what matters most right now), obstacles (what's actually blocking progress, named specifically rather than glossed over), recent evidence and feedback (drawing directly on the observations and debriefs Module 5 already generates), recognition (specific, genuine acknowledgment of what's working, not a generic compliment), and development (the associate's own growth plan, connecting naturally to Module 5's GROW-model coaching conversations for a specific skill in progress). A one-on-one that only ever covers status updates and priorities, without ever touching recognition or development, is missing half of what this format is actually for.

## A Practical Time-Boxed Structure

A specific, workable structure for a standard 30-minute weekly one-on-one: five to ten minutes of genuine catch-up and rapport (not wasted time — this is where trust actually gets built over weeks), five to seven minutes reviewing the current week's priorities and any immediate obstacles, five to seven minutes on feedback and observations (both directions — manager to associate and associate to manager), and five to ten minutes closing with next steps: specific tasks, specific owners, and a specific quick win to aim for before the next meeting. Ending every one-on-one with named actions and owners, rather than a vague sense that "we had a good talk," is what makes the next week's meeting able to start with a genuine follow-up rather than starting from scratch.

## Recording Decisions So They Actually Get Revisited

A specific, easy-to-skip practical discipline: record the commitments and decisions made in each one-on-one, and open with a review of the prior meeting's commitments at the start of the next one. This is a direct extension of Module 5's follow-up principle applied to a longer time horizon — a one-on-one that never revisits what was discussed the week before quietly teaches an associate that what gets said in these meetings doesn't actually matter, exactly the same failure mode Module 5 warned against for a single floor debrief.

## Putting the Vocabulary to Work

The complete weekly one-on-one sequence this module establishes: set a default weekly, 30-minute cadence, adjusted toward more frequent brief check-ins for a new hire or someone in active coaching and toward a lighter biweekly rhythm for a steady, experienced performer; let the associate set the agenda, leaving space for manager topics; cover all five recurring areas (priorities, obstacles, evidence/feedback, recognition, development) rather than only status updates; use the time-boxed structure to keep the conversation focused; and open every meeting by reviewing the prior week's recorded commitments before moving to anything new.

## A Worked Scenario: The One-on-One That Surfaced a Problem Early

A manager running employee-driven one-on-ones notices an associate's agenda, three weeks in a row, includes a vague note about "scheduling stress." Rather than letting the note pass as small talk, the manager uses the obstacles portion of the agenda to ask directly what specifically is causing it. The associate reveals a recurring conflict between their posted schedule and a recently changed personal commitment — something they hadn't raised directly because it felt like a personal issue rather than a work topic, but which was quietly affecting their energy and focus on the floor. Because the agenda was associate-driven and the obstacles category was a standing part of the format, the issue surfaced in week three rather than only becoming visible months later as a performance or attendance problem.

## Practice Exercise

For your next scheduled one-on-one, ask the associate to send you their own agenda in advance, and add any topics you need to raise separately. Structure the conversation using the five recurring areas (priorities, obstacles, evidence/feedback, recognition, development), and end with a specific list of named actions and owners. At the start of the following week's one-on-one, open by reviewing whether those specific commitments were kept before moving to anything new.

## Key Takeaways

- Weekly, 30-minute one-on-ones are the most consistently favored default cadence across multiple independent studies, correlated with the highest employee engagement, though the underlying research also supports adjusting frequency and length based on role complexity and how much active coaching a given associate currently needs — a new hire or someone in active coaching benefits from a weekly cadence, while a steady performer may be well served biweekly.
- The one-on-one should be employee-driven: the associate prepares and sends the agenda in advance, since a manager who always drives the agenda tends to hear about problems only once they're already serious.
- Five recurring content areas show up consistently across independent one-on-one frameworks: current priorities, obstacles, recent evidence and feedback, recognition, and development — a meeting that only covers status updates is missing half of what the format is for.
- A time-boxed structure (catch-up, priorities/obstacles, feedback, next steps with named owners) keeps a 30-minute conversation focused and productive rather than open-ended.
- Recording commitments and reviewing them at the start of the next meeting is a direct extension of Module 5's follow-up principle applied to a longer time horizon, and is what makes a one-on-one feel consequential rather than a recurring, forgettable chat.
