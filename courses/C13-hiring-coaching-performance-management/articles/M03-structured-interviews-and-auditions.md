# Structured Interviews and Auditions

## Why This Module Matters

Module 2 built a role profile where every must-have competency has a named piece of evidence attached. This module builds the actual interview guide that generates that evidence — the specific questions, the audition exercises, and the scoring discipline that turns an interview from a pleasant conversation into a genuine measurement tool. This is the direct, practical payoff of Module 1's research finding that structured interviews are among the strongest predictors of job performance available to a hiring manager.

## What Makes an Interview "Structured"

A structured interview means every candidate for a given role answers the same predetermined questions, in the same order, and gets evaluated against the same predefined scoring criteria — as opposed to an unstructured conversation that wanders differently with each candidate. The specific build sequence: start with a comprehensive job analysis identifying the specific knowledge, skills, abilities, and other characteristics critical for success (this is exactly the must-have competency list Module 2 already produced), then develop targeted question sets mixing behavioral, situational, and competency-based questions that reflect actual job challenges, then create standardized scoring rubrics defining what an excellent, satisfactory, and unsatisfactory answer to each specific question looks like, and finally train every interviewer on delivery, objective evaluation, and bias mitigation before anyone conducts a live interview.

## The STAR Method for Behavioral Questions

The standard framework for behavioral questions — questions that start with "tell me about a time" — is STAR: Situation (the context), Task (the specific responsibility), Action (what the candidate personally did, not what a team did), and Result (the outcome, ideally with a number attached). A useful extension some retail-hiring sources add is STAR-plus: a fifth element asking the candidate to name the lesson learned and explicitly tie the story back to the role being interviewed for. A manager running the interview should listen specifically for the Action and Result components, since candidates often speak fluently about the Situation and Task while going vague exactly where the real evidence lives — what they personally did, and what actually happened as a result.

## Building Questions From Module 2's Must-Have List

The direct, practical link between this module and Module 2: each must-have competency in the role profile gets one specific behavioral or situational question, matched to the evidence source already named for that competency. For genuine product passion, a situational question like "describe a piece of jewelry you personally love, and what makes it special to you" surfaces authentic detail that a scripted, generic answer can't fake. For listening skill and restraint, a behavioral question like "tell me about a time a customer said they were 'just looking' — what did you do?" reveals whether a candidate's instinct is to back off appropriately or push forward regardless. For persistence without pushiness, "tell me about a customer who wanted to think it over — how did you follow up?" surfaces whether the candidate's version of persistence respects the customer's space or ignores it.

## The Sales Role-Play: A Live Audition, Not a Trick Question

A role-play or audition exercise puts a candidate in a live sales scenario, typically with an interviewer or trained colleague playing a customer, and is treated across sales-hiring sources as a genuine performance audition rather than a gotcha. The candidate should be given the brief in advance where practical (a short written scenario, 10 to 20 minutes before the exercise), told the specific goal and time limit, and then run through a scripted scenario while at least one dedicated rater scores the interaction against a predefined rubric rather than a general impression. What a manager watches for maps directly onto this course's own trait vocabulary: does the candidate ask open-ended discovery questions and genuinely listen before pitching, does the pitch feel tailored to what the "customer" just said rather than a memorized script, and does the candidate handle a raised objection with actual empathy rather than either capitulating or steamrolling past it.

## Behaviorally Anchored Rating Scales: Making Scoring Actually Consistent

A specific, well-documented scoring technique for role-plays and behavioral questions alike: a behaviorally anchored rating scale, or BARS, which pairs a numeric scale (typically 1 to 5) with one concrete, observable behavior written out for each point on the scale, for each competency being assessed. This solves a real, common problem: two interviewers scoring the same candidate's answer on a vague "communication skill, 1 to 5" scale will often produce very different numbers, because "good communication" means something different to each rater. A BARS anchor removes most of that drift by defining exactly what a 2 versus a 4 actually looks like in observable terms, and pairs naturally with the panel-interview practice (at least two or three raters scoring independently before comparing notes) that multiple sources recommend specifically to reduce individual bias.

## Calibrating Raters Before Live Interviews Begin

A specific, practical step easy to skip under time pressure: before using a new rubric on real candidates, have every interviewer who will use it independently score two or three recorded or scripted exemplar answers (a deliberately weak one, a middling one, and a strong one), then meet briefly to discuss any scores that differ by more than a point on any competency, resolving the disagreement by referring back to the specific anchor language rather than by argument or seniority. This calibration step is what actually makes a shared rubric produce comparable scores across different interviewers, rather than each interviewer quietly applying their own private standard while using the same numbers.

## Putting the Vocabulary to Work

The complete interview-guide-building sequence this module establishes: take Module 2's must-have competency list, write one behavioral or situational question per competency using STAR as the answer framework, add a live role-play exercise scored with a BARS rubric for the specific selling competencies (listening, restraint, tailored pitching, objection handling) that a verbal question can't fully surface, calibrate every interviewer on the rubric using recorded exemplars before live interviews begin, and use at least two independent raters for any candidate who reaches a role-play or final-round stage.

## A Worked Scenario: The Rubric That Caught What the Conversation Missed

A candidate interviews warmly, tells a confident story about a past sales role using clean STAR structure, and both interviewers initially feel positive. The subsequent role-play exercise, scored against a BARS rubric built specifically around listening and tailored pitching, reveals a specific gap: the candidate's pitch in the role-play barely references anything the "customer" said during discovery, running instead through what sounds like a memorized, generic product pitch regardless of what was actually asked. Because the rubric anchors "tailored pitching" to a specific observable behavior (referencing at least two details the customer stated) rather than a vague overall impression, both independent raters land on the same lower score for that specific competency — a gap the warm verbal interview alone would likely have missed entirely.

## Practice Exercise

Using the must-have competency list from your Module 2 role profile, write one STAR-format behavioral or situational question per competency. Then design a short role-play scenario (a specific customer type and objection) and build a BARS rubric for it: for one selling competency, write out what a 1, 3, and 5 look like in specific, observable terms. Ask a colleague to independently score a practice role-play against your rubric, then compare notes and refine any anchor language that produced disagreement.

## Key Takeaways

- A structured interview means every candidate answers the same predetermined questions in the same order, scored against the same predefined rubric — built from a job analysis (Module 2's must-have list), targeted questions, standardized scoring criteria, and trained interviewers.
- The STAR method (Situation, Task, Action, Result) is the standard framework for behavioral questions, with interviewers listening specifically for the Action and Result, since candidates often go vague exactly where the real evidence lives.
- Each must-have competency from Module 2's role profile gets one specific behavioral or situational question matched to its already-named evidence source — directly linking this module's interview guide to that module's role profile.
- A sales role-play audition, scored by a dedicated rater against a predefined rubric rather than general impression, surfaces whether a candidate actually listens and tailors their pitch, which a verbal-only interview often cannot reveal.
- Behaviorally anchored rating scales (BARS) pair a numeric scale with one concrete, observable behavior per point, and rater calibration on recorded exemplars before live interviews begin is what makes a shared rubric actually produce comparable scores across different interviewers.
