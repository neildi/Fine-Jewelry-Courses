# Floor Coaching: Observe, Debrief, Practice

## Why This Module Matters

Onboarding, built in Module 4, gets a new hire to a baseline of competence. What happens after that — the ongoing, week-to-week coaching that turns a competent associate into a genuinely strong one — is a distinct skill this module builds specifically. This is the direct source of evidence Module 7's performance reviews will eventually draw on, and it's the specific behavior this course's assessment (a recorded coaching debrief) asks a store leader to actually demonstrate.

## The Core Coaching Loop: Observe, Debrief, Practice

The three-part structure this module is named for maps onto a well-documented floor-coaching sequence. Observation happens during a live customer interaction, ideally on a regular, scheduled cadence rather than only when something goes visibly wrong. The debrief happens immediately after, or as close to immediately as practical — five minutes maximum, before moving to the next interaction or the next task, since coaching value decays quickly once too much time passes between the observed behavior and the conversation about it. Practice is the specific commitment the associate makes for their very next interaction, which the manager then follows up on at the next observation, closing the loop rather than leaving the conversation as a one-off comment.

## What Makes a Debrief Actually Land: Observation, Impact, Alternative

A specific, well-corroborated three-part structure for the debrief conversation itself: state the observation clearly and factually, with no judgment or interpretation attached ("in the first few minutes, you spent most of the time describing the setting before asking the customer anything about the occasion"); name the impact that behavior actually had ("the customer seemed to disengage, and by the time you asked about the occasion, they'd already started browsing on their own"); and only then offer an alternative, ideally after asking the associate for their own take first. This ordering matters specifically because it separates fact from judgment — an associate can disagree with an interpretation, but a specific, accurately observed behavior is much harder to argue with, which keeps the conversation productive rather than defensive.

## Self-Assessment First: Let Them Diagnose Their Own Interaction

A specific, consistently recommended opening move for any debrief: ask the associate how they think the interaction went before offering any observation of your own. This does two things at once — it surfaces whether the associate already has accurate self-awareness (in which case the manager's job is mostly to confirm and reinforce), and it makes the eventual coaching land better, since people generally accept feedback more readily when they've already started the diagnosis themselves rather than receiving it cold.

## The GROW Model: A Structure for the Bigger Conversation

Beyond a single interaction debrief, GROW is the most widely used framework for a longer, more developmental coaching conversation — useful for a weekly or biweekly session focused on a specific skill an associate is actively working to build, distinct from the quick five-minute debrief after any single customer interaction. Goal names what's being improved this session, agreed by both people at the start, one thing, not five. Reality asks what's actually happening right now, not what should be happening — a genuine, honest look at the current pattern. Options asks what the associate could do differently, with the manager deliberately letting the associate generate these options first before adding any of their own, since options an associate generates themselves get better buy-in than options handed down. Way Forward (sometimes called Will) locks in the one specific thing the associate commits to doing before the next conversation.

## A Five-Step Debrief Sequence for Structured Coaching Visits

A more formalized five-step debrief sequence, drawn from structured field-coaching practice in other sales-heavy industries, translates directly to a jewelry sales-floor coaching visit: self-assessment first ("how do you think that interaction went?"), then affirming one specific strength (not a generic "good job," but a specific, named behavior and why it worked), then naming exactly one development focus (one, not several, since an associate walking away with five things to fix rarely improves on any of them), then getting a specific commitment for the next interaction, and finally following up at the next observation to confirm whether that commitment was actually kept. This last step — following up — is what separates a genuine coaching practice from a series of disconnected comments; a commitment that's never checked on quietly teaches an associate that the commitment didn't really matter.

## Watch for the Same Trait Cluster This Course Has Already Built

A specific, practical continuity point: floor observation should watch for exactly the trait cluster established in Module 1 and screened for in Module 3's interview process — genuine listening before pitching, product enthusiasm that reads as authentic rather than scripted, and persistence that respects a customer's space rather than pushing past it. An associate who was hired specifically because they demonstrated these traits in a role-play should be coached against the same standard on the floor, not a different, informally improvised standard the manager invents in the moment.

## Putting the Vocabulary to Work

The complete floor-coaching sequence this module establishes: schedule regular observation rounds rather than only reactive ones, debrief within five minutes using the observation-impact-alternative structure and asking for self-assessment first, use GROW for a longer developmental conversation on a specific skill an associate is actively building, follow up at the next observation to confirm whether a committed change actually happened, and coach consistently against the same trait cluster this course has used since Module 1.

## A Worked Scenario: The Debrief That Actually Changed Behavior

A manager observes an associate spending most of a customer interaction describing a ring's technical specifications before asking anything about the occasion or the recipient. Rather than saying "you talked too much about specs," the manager uses the observation-impact-alternative structure: states specifically that the first several minutes were spent on carat weight and clarity grade before any discovery question was asked, names the impact (the customer's body language shifted, and they eventually asked to "just look around"), and asks the associate what they noticed themselves before offering the alternative (asking about the occasion first, then framing the technical detail around what the customer actually said they cared about). The associate, who had already sensed something felt off but couldn't name it, commits to leading with one discovery question on the very next customer. The manager checks in at the next observation and confirms the associate followed through.

## Practice Exercise

During your next scheduled floor observation, run a full five-minute debrief using the observation-impact-alternative structure: state one specific, factual observation, name its actual impact, ask the associate for their own take, and only then offer one alternative. Get one specific commitment for their next interaction, and set a reminder to check whether that commitment was kept at your next observation of that associate.

## Key Takeaways

- The core floor-coaching loop is observe, debrief, practice: scheduled observation, a debrief within five minutes using a structured approach, and a specific commitment for the next interaction that the manager follows up on later, closing the loop rather than leaving feedback as a one-off comment.
- A debrief lands best using the observation-impact-alternative structure: state a specific, factual observation with no judgment attached, name the actual impact that behavior had, and only then offer an alternative — ideally after asking the associate for their own self-assessment first.
- GROW (Goal, Reality, Options, Way Forward) is the standard framework for a longer, developmental coaching conversation on a specific skill, distinct from the quick debrief after a single interaction, with options deliberately generated by the associate first for better buy-in.
- A five-step structured debrief (self-assess, affirm one specific strength, name exactly one development focus, get a commitment, follow up at the next observation) is a well-corroborated practice, and the final follow-up step is what separates genuine coaching from disconnected comments.
- Floor coaching should watch for and reinforce the same trait cluster this course established in Module 1 and screened for in Module 3, keeping hiring standards and ongoing coaching standards consistent rather than inventing a different standard once someone is already on the floor.
