# C13-M09 Outline: Recognition, Incentives and Commission Fairness

## Module Metadata
- Course: C13 - Hiring, Coaching & Performance Management in Jewelry Retail
- Module: 9 of 10
- Persona: P7 (First-Time Store Leader), P8 (Multi-Store Leader), per official course architecture
- Working title: Recognition, Incentives and Commission Fairness
- Learning objective: Store leaders can apply frequency-and-specificity recognition principles, identify and avoid the most common incentive-plan design failures, apply the gross-profit-vs-sales-volume commission distinction, write a specific split-sale rule, and pair individual commission with a team-based bonus counterweight.
- Terminal outcome tie-in: This module supports the C13 KPI link (per-associate productivity, turnover) by covering the recognition and compensation-fairness practices that directly affect retention and motivation, complementing Module 8's corrective performance-management content with the positive-reinforcement and fair-pay side of the same system.

## Section Map

1. **Why This Module Matters** — Frames this module as the counterpart to Module 8's corrective process.
2. **Recognition: Frequent and Specific Beats Occasional and Generic** — The frequency/specificity findings and their measured link to retention.
3. **The Cost of Getting Incentive Design Wrong** — Vague criteria, inconsistent application, lack of transparency; the pure-sales-volume discounting risk.
4. **The Jewelry-Specific Commission Debate: Percentage of Sales vs. Percentage of Gross Profit** — The gross-profit recommendation and honest acknowledgment of real variation across sources.
5. **Split-Sale Rules: The Detail Most Plans Get Wrong** — The 50/50 turnover-close and 75/25 groundwork-close jewelry-industry default rules.
6. **Team Goals as a Counterweight to Pure Individual Commission** — The "shark" environment warning and the proportionate team-bonus fix.
7. **A Note on Compensation Design and Legal Review** — Explicit note that commission-design principles are not a substitute for wage-and-hour legal/payroll compliance review.
8. **Putting the Vocabulary to Work** — The complete recognition-and-incentive sequence.
9. **A Worked Scenario: The Split That Almost Cost a Sale** — Illustrative synthesis showing a written split-sale rule preventing a customer-experience problem.
10. **Practice Exercise** — Auditing the store's current commission structure against this module's five criteria.
11. **Key Takeaways** — Five-point recap.

## Production Notes
- The frequency-over-ceremony and specificity-over-generality recognition principles are sourced directly from goactify.com's "Retail Employee Recognition Program Examples" article, which explicitly names these as the top two design principles across successful retail-recognition programs ("Frequency over ceremony... Specificity over generality... 'Great job this week' is noise").
- The retail-specific retention/engagement statistics linked to integrated recognition (increased odds of planning to stay, sense of belonging, feeling fulfilled, high engagement, thriving at work) are sourced directly from O.C. Tanner's "5 Recognition Best Practices for Retail Organizations in 2026" white paper, and independently corroborated in direction (though with different specific figures) by nectarhr.com's "10 Strategies To Build A Successful Retail Employee Recognition Program" article, which cites separate survey data showing recognized retail employees feel more valued, more engaged, and more productive. Gallup's "Is Your Industry Delivering on Employee Recognition?" independently corroborates the general principle that authentic, equitable, personalized recognition combats burnout and turnover while boosting retention.
- The incentive-plan design-failure list (vague/complex criteria, inconsistent application, lack of transparency, gamed systems) is CONFIRMED CONSISTENT across multiple independent sources: BambooHR's "Why Incentive Programs Fail," Extu's "Avoid These 7 Common Sales Incentive Mistakes," and Forma.ai's "The Ultimate Guide to Sales Incentives for 2026," all naming a closely overlapping set of common failure modes.
- The pure-sales-volume-encourages-unsustainable-discounting risk is sourced from hrdailyadvisor.hci.org's "Unintended Consequences When Setting Sales Incentive Programs" article, which explicitly warns that "basing purely on sales often motivates salespeople to offer unwanted discounts or other special deals that wind up making the sale unprofitable." This module's explicit tie to this project's own C10 course content on luxury consumer psychology and negotiation is this module's own original synthesis connecting an externally-sourced general sales-incentive risk to this project's own prior, independently-verified course content.
- The gross-profit-based commission recommendation and the ~15-16% of gross profit jewelry-industry benchmark are sourced directly from JCK's "The Many Ways Retailers Pay and Reward Employees" article, which quotes a named jewelry-industry compensation consultant (Peterson) making exactly this recommendation and citing this specific benchmark figure. This module explicitly notes real variation exists across sources: a second JCK article ("The Savvy Jeweler's Guide to Compensation Packages") cites a different jewelry retailer offering straight sales-percentage commissions of 1-5%, and INSTORE's "Pros and Cons for 5 Popular Jewelry Store Commission Systems" article (citing jewelry-industry consultant David Geller) describes multiple viable commission-percentage-of-sales models running roughly 6-10% of sales depending on discount level and sales volume. This module presents the gross-profit recommendation as the more theoretically sound approach per Peterson's stated reasoning, while explicitly acknowledging that a range of models are used successfully across the industry and the right choice depends on a store's specific margin structure and goals.
- The split-sale rules (50/50 for turnover-and-close; 75/25 favoring the associate who did original groundwork like ordering or presenting) are sourced directly from jewelerprofit.com's "Pay and Commission Structure for Sales Personnel" document, a jewelry-industry-specific compensation-policy template, which states these exact percentages and scenarios, and independently corroborated by INSTORE's commission-systems article (citing David Geller's recommendation to "split the commission if two people help the same customer on the same day") and a Ganoksin Orchid jewelry-industry discussion forum post describing a comparable split-commission practice from a practicing jeweler.
- The "shark environment" warning and the team-goal counterweight recommendation (including the vendor-spiff-to-team-reward conversion practice) are sourced directly and specifically from JCK's "Don't Swim With Sharks" article, a jewelry-industry-specific source explicitly naming this exact dynamic and this exact fix ("Team members should not 'own' customers but share them... When a supplier offers a spiff... turn that into a team effort").
- The proportionate team-bonus distribution practice (tied to hours worked or sales percentage contributed, rather than an equal split) is independently corroborated by the same JCK "Many Ways Retailers Pay" article (citing a named jewelry-store general manager's proportionate-distribution practice) and the Ganoksin Orchid forum discussion.
- The note on wage-and-hour legal review before rolling out any incentive plan is sourced from worldatwork.org's "Incentive Plan Driving Unintended Behaviors?" article, which explicitly recommends legal, compliance, and governance review before rollout, consistent with this course's Module 8 practice of flagging genuine legal-jurisdiction variation rather than presenting compensation design as legal advice.
- The worked scenario (the split that almost cost a sale) and the practice exercise are ORIGINAL illustrative syntheses created for this module, not externally sourced cases.
