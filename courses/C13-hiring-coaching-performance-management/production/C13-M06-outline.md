# C13-M06 Outline: Weekly One-on-Ones

- Module: 6 of 10

## Production Notes
- The Gallup finding that employees with regular one-on-ones are nearly three times as likely to be engaged is CONFIRMED, cited consistently across multiple independent sources: Baylor University's "Manager Best Practices: One-on-One Meetings" and firsttimemanagers.com's "How Often Should You Have 1-on-1s?" both cite this exact Gallup statistic.
- The weekly, 30-minute cadence as the most consistently favored default, correlated with highest engagement in comparative research, is CONFIRMED CONSISTENT across multiple independent sources: Baylor University's guide, physicianleaders.org's "Make the Most of Your One-on-One Meetings" (citing original survey research finding weekly 30-minute meetings rated most desirable and correlated with highest engagement), Harvard Business Review's podcast with Steven Rogelberg (citing research showing weekly cadence as most ideal, followed by biweekly), and firsttimemanagers.com's direct statement ("If you want one answer, here it is: weekly, 30 minutes").
- MUST-NOTE NUANCE, now incorporated directly into this module's text: evalflow.com's "One-on-One Meeting Statistics and Research for 2026" article provides a more recent, more careful synthesis explicitly cautioning that "the research does not support a universal rule that every employee needs a 60-minute weekly one-on-one," and cites Gallup's own coaching framework recommending a brief 1-10 minute "quick connect" at least weekly paired with a more substantive 10-30 minute check-in once or twice a month, with exact cadence adjusted for role complexity, team needs, and coaching intensity. This module explicitly presents both the strong weekly-30-minute consensus and this more nuanced, flexible-by-need framing, rather than asserting a single rigid rule, consistent with this project's practice of presenting genuine complexity in the underlying research rather than oversimplifying.
- The employee-driven-agenda principle is CONFIRMED, sourced directly from Baylor University's guide ("The employee prepares the agenda... and sends it to the manager in advance. Having the employee set the agenda ensures you are covering the topics and information most important to them") and independently corroborated by Lattice's "7 Free One-on-One Meeting Templates" article ("The employee-led agenda format creates space for two-way feedback while ensuring managers cover critical topics").
- The five recurring content areas (priorities, obstacles, recent evidence/feedback, recognition, development) are sourced directly from evalflow.com's article ("Use five recurring areas: current priorities, obstacles, recent evidence and feedback, recognition, and development. End with named actions and owners"), and independently corroborated in substance (though with somewhat different specific wording) by O.C. Tanner's "2020 Global Culture Report" one-to-ones section (What's new, positive feedback/achievements, current issues, discussion/solutions, growth/development, plan of action) and AIHR's "12 Free One-on-One Meeting Templates" (performance discussion, goal setting, challenges, professional development, feedback, next steps).
- The time-boxed 30-minute structure (catch-up, priorities/obstacles, feedback, next steps with named owners) is sourced directly from HubSpot's "11 Questions to Ask Your Employees... in One on Ones" article, which provides this exact minute-by-minute breakdown, and independently corroborated in overall structure by PerformYard's "1:1 Meeting Agenda Templates" article, which describes a very similar five-part time-boxed weekly structure.
- The commitment-recording and next-meeting-review discipline is sourced directly from evalflow.com's article ("Record decisions and commitments, then reopen them at the next conversation") and independently corroborated by PerformYard's template ("Recap of Previous Actions & Follow-Up — Start by reviewing tasks from your last 1:1 meeting agenda").
- This module's explicit connections between the one-on-one agenda areas and Module 5's coaching content (recent evidence/feedback drawing on floor observations; development connecting to GROW-model conversations) are this module's own original synthesis, connecting confirmed general one-on-one frameworks to this course's own previously-established content; not itself an externally sourced claim.
- The worked scenario (the one-on-one that surfaced scheduling stress in week three) and the practice exercise are ORIGINAL illustrative syntheses created for this module, not externally sourced cases.
