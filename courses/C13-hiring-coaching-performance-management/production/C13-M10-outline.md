# C13-M10 Outline: Building Bench Strength for Your Own Promotion

## Module Metadata
- Course: C13 - Hiring, Coaching & Performance Management in Jewelry Retail
- Module: 10 of 10 (final module)
- Persona: P7 (First-Time Store Leader), P8 (Multi-Store Leader), per official course architecture
- Working title: Building Bench Strength for Your Own Promotion
- Learning objective: Store leaders can identify leadership potential using specific behavioral signals independent of sales rank, name that potential directly to identified associates, sort a bench into readiness tiers, apply a structured delegation discipline as a development tool, and recognize which responsibilities should never be delegated.
- Terminal outcome tie-in: This closing module supports this course's overall coaching and performance-management terminal outcomes by extending them into succession-oriented development, and closes the full ten-module cycle this course has built.

## Section Map

1. **Why This Module Matters** — Frames bench-building as both a service to associates and the clearest path to a manager's own next promotion.
2. **The Specific Behaviors That Signal Leadership Potential** — Asks why, helps unprompted, makes others better; explicitly distinct from sales-leaderboard rank.
3. **Say It Out Loud** — The easy-to-skip step of directly naming observed potential to the associate.
4. **Readiness Tiers: Not Everyone Is Ready at the Same Pace** — Ready now / within a year / within 2-3 years / longer-term potential.
5. **Delegation as the Development Tool, Not Just a Time-Saver** — The structured delegation discipline (outcome, person-selection, reasoning, authority, check-ins, feedback).
6. **What Should Never Be Delegated** — Performance evaluations, discipline, matters requiring the manager's own specific authority.
7. **Putting the Vocabulary to Work** — The complete bench-strength-building sequence.
8. **A Worked Scenario: The Associate Who Didn't Know They Were Ready** — Illustrative synthesis showing the impact of naming potential directly, using Module 4's onboarding framework as the first delegated responsibility.
9. **Practice Exercise** — Identifying, tiering, and delegating to real high-potential associates.
10. **Key Takeaways** — Five-point recap.
11. **Closing the Course** — Explicit synthesis tying all ten C13 modules together as a complete cycle.

## Production Notes
- The three specific leadership-potential behavioral signals (passes tests and asks clarifying/forward-thinking questions; helps without being asked; makes other people better, with newer associates naturally gravitating toward them) are sourced directly and specifically from retaildoc.com's "How to Develop Retail Managers From Within" article, which names these exact three signals and explicitly states "These are your future managers. Not because they sell the most. Because they demonstrate the behaviors you need in leadership" — directly supporting this module's claim that leadership potential is distinct from sales rank.
- The "say it out loud" practice (identifying the top two or three high-potential people and having a direct conversation naming the observed potential and describing a concrete path) is sourced directly from the same retaildoc.com article ("Week 3: Identify and Tell Your High Potentials... Have a conversation with each one. 'I see leadership ability in you. Here's what the path looks like.'").
- The readiness-tier framework (ready now, ready within one year, ready within two to three years, longer-term potential) is sourced directly from retailreadyinsights.com's "Retail Talent Strategy Planning and Execution for Growth" article, which explicitly names this exact four-tier classification.
- The general succession-planning principles (identifying high-potential employees, involving them in stretch assignments beyond their current role, preparing before a vacancy occurs) are CONFIRMED CONSISTENT across multiple independent sources: intoo.com's "Talent Management in the Retail Industry," LinkedIn's "How to Create a Succession Plan for Store Managers," retailreadyinsights.com's "Retail Leadership Bench Strength Development" article, and datacalculus.com's "Succession Planning for Retail HR Managers in Fashion."
- The structured delegation discipline (define the desired outcome, select the person deliberately, explain the reasoning, define the authority level, agree on check-ins, provide closed-loop feedback) is CONFIRMED CONSISTENT across multiple independent, detailed sources: the University of Michigan's "Delegating Checklist," the University of Sussex's "Effective delegation" guide, and The Management Center's "Effective Delegation Process and Worksheet," all describing a closely matching multi-step delegation sequence with only minor variation in exact step count and ordering.
- The specific "delegation is a development choice, not just unloading a task" reframe is sourced from the University of Michigan's "Delegating Checklist" ("Managers Must Delegate Effectively to Develop Employees") and independently corroborated by AMA's "Delegation Do's and Don'ts" article, which explicitly links effective delegation to "easier and more effective promotion and succession planning."
- The list of responsibilities that should never be delegated (performance evaluations; counseling, disciplining, and other confidential personnel matters; tasks requiring the manager's own specific expertise, authority, or leadership) is sourced directly and specifically from AMA's "Delegation Do's and Don'ts" article, which names these exact categories.
- This module's worked scenario explicitly reusing Module 4's onboarding framework as a delegated development responsibility is this module's own original synthesis, connecting the confirmed general delegation and succession-planning frameworks above to this course's own previously-established, independently-verified Module 4 content; not itself an externally sourced claim.
- The practice exercise and the "Closing the Course" synthesis are ORIGINAL syntheses created for this module, not externally sourced cases.
