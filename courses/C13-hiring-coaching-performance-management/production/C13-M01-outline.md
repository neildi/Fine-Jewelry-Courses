# C13-M01 Outline: What Great Jewelry Sellers Have in Common

## Module Metadata
- Course: C13 - Hiring, Coaching & Performance Management in Jewelry Retail
- Module: 1 of 10
- Persona: P7 (First-Time Store Leader), P8 (Multi-Store Leader), per official course architecture
- Working title: What Great Jewelry Sellers Have in Common
- Learning objective: Store leaders can name and apply the specific jewelry-selling trait cluster (emotional intelligence, genuine product passion, listening skill, persistence without pushiness), understand why cognitive-ability and personality research support but don't replace jewelry-specific hiring criteria, and reconcile the tension between general-sales drive and jewelry-specific restraint.
- Terminal outcome tie-in: This module lays the diagnostic foundation the entire C13 course depends on; it precedes and directly informs the course's stated terminal outcomes around role profiles, structured interviews, and coaching.

## Section Map

1. **Why This Module Matters** — Frames this as the foundational "what are we hiring and coaching for" question the rest of C13 depends on.
2. **The Jewelry-Specific Skill List** — Jewelers of America's named trait cluster, with the deliberate "persistent, not pushy" distinction highlighted.
3. **The Emotional Intelligence Finding in Luxury Retail Specifically** — EQ as the cornerstone trait, plus the fuller luxury-advisor personality profile.
4. **Serving, Not Selling** — The specific, screenable "never decide who can afford what" principle.
5. **What the Research on Predicting Sales Success Actually Shows** — Corrected, nuanced treatment of cognitive-ability/structured-interview validity research (Schmidt & Hunter 1998 vs. more recent re-analysis).
6. **The Personality Cluster That Complements Cognitive Ability** — Achievement, initiative, assertiveness, self-confidence, coachability, resilience.
7. **Reconciling Two Traditions: The Closer and the Advisor** — The drive-vs-restraint balance jewelry selling specifically requires.
8. **Putting the Vocabulary to Work** — Explicit tie-forward to Module 2 (role profiles) and Module 3 (structured interviews).
9. **A Worked Scenario: The Impressive Interview That Didn't Translate** — Illustrative synthesis of a strong-driver, weak-restraint mismatch hire.
10. **Practice Exercise** — Informally scoring a strong and struggling associate against this module's trait cluster.
11. **Key Takeaways** — Five-point recap.

## Production Notes
- The jewelry-specific skill list (personable/enthusiastic, storytelling, product knowledge, communication, listening, creative problem-solving, "determined, persistent and persuasive, but not pushy," even-tempered, trustworthy/ethical, versatile) is sourced directly and verbatim in substance from Jewelers.org's (Jewelers of America) "Jewelry Sales Professional" career-pathways page, an authoritative, named trade-association source. This is CONFIRMED as a direct, credible primary source.
- The luxury-retail emotional-intelligence cornerstone finding and the six-part personality profile (authentic passion, refined discretion, natural storytelling, cultural sophistication, poised confidence, adaptive communication) are sourced from questsearch.co.uk's "The Art of Selling: How Personality-Driven Hiring Elevates Luxury Retail" article, a specialized recruitment-industry source. This is a single-source claim not independently corroborated by a second luxury-retail-specific source during this module's research, though it is broadly consistent in direction with the general "listening/restraint over aggression" theme found across other jewelry-trade sources (rapnet.com, INSTORE, Jewelers of America) used in this module.
- The "serve, don't sell, never decide who can afford what" principle is sourced from retaildoc.com's "Selling Luxury Retail" page (The Retail Doctor), a named retail-consulting source with specific, attributed hiring guidance ("Hire optimists who serve rather than sell, take genuine joy in a customer buying something beautiful, and never decide who can afford what").
- The cognitive-ability/structured-interview predictive-validity research was INITIALLY drafted using a specific "2x/3x/4x" multiplier claim from a single hiring-assessment vendor source (Criteria Corp), which this module's research subsequently cross-checked against the broader personnel-selection literature and found to be an oversimplification. The revised, CONFIRMED treatment in this module's final text is based on: Schmidt & Hunter's widely-cited 1998 meta-analysis (general mental ability validity r = .51, tied with structured interviews at r = .51, unstructured interviews at r = .38, experience at r = .18, as reported consistently across cogn-iq.org's multiple detailed summary articles and Criteria Corp's own more precise citation of "1.6x as predictive as unstructured interviews and four times as predictive as job experience" — note this more precise multiplier, drawn directly from Criteria Corp's own site copy, differs from and is more defensible than the initially drafted "2x/3x/4x" figure, which appears to have been a research-stage transcription imprecision now corrected); and a more recent re-analysis discussed in Cambridge University Press's "Industrial and Organizational Psychology" journal and eSkill's "Best and Worst Predictors of Job Performance" article (citing I/O psychologist Paul Sackett's more recent meta-analysis, which ranks structured interviews above cognitive ability and places cognitive ability closer to fifth among methods studied, with a validity coefficient roughly .20 lower than the original Schmidt & Hunter figures). This module explicitly presents both the original and revised findings rather than asserting a single, now-contested figure as settled fact.
- The personality-cluster claims (achievement, competitiveness, initiative, assertiveness, self-confidence, coachability, resilience) are CONFIRMED CONSISTENT across multiple independent sales-assessment sources: Criteria Corp, Wonderlic's "Uncover Sales Superstars with Assessments" (citing TrueMatch's competency rankings: stress tolerance, integrity, persistence, dependability, achievement/effort, self-control, adaptability), and Testlify's "Sales Assessment Tests for Hiring" article.
- The "reconciling two traditions" synthesis (drive vs. restraint balance) is this module's own original synthesis connecting the jewelry-specific and general-sales-research findings above; not itself an externally sourced claim.
- The worked scenario and practice exercise are ORIGINAL illustrative syntheses created for this module.
