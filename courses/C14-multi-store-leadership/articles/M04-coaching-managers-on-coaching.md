# Coaching Managers on Coaching

## Why This Module Matters

C13 built a complete coaching toolkit: observation, debrief structures, GROW-model conversations, evidence-based reviews. That toolkit was built for coaching an individual associate. This module covers a genuinely different skill: coaching a store manager on how they coach their own associates — a level of removal this course calls meta-coaching, and one of the specific capabilities that most clearly separates a district leader from a store manager doing the same job at a bigger scale.

## The "Hats" Framework: Knowing Which Role You're in

A useful framing for the range of roles a district leader moves through: at different moments, the same person needs to be a strategist, a financial analyst, an operator, and a coach, and knowing which hat the moment actually calls for is itself a skill. The coach hat specifically means the leader's primary focus shifts to the store manager's own development, motivation, and performance, using powerful questions rather than direct instruction. The single most common pitfall this framing identifies: telling instead of asking, and solving problems for a store manager instead of teaching them how to solve problems themselves — precisely the trap Module 1 already warned against, now named specifically as a coaching failure mode rather than only an operational one.

## Coach the Manager on the Metric, Not the Associate on the Task

A specific, important distinction that separates this module from C13's coaching content: a district leader's job is to identify which store manager needs coaching on which specific metric, then coach that manager accordingly — not to bypass the manager and coach the associate directly. A district leader who steps around a struggling store manager to personally coach that manager's associates undermines the manager's own authority and skips the exact capability-building this course has emphasized since Module 1. The specific practice this requires: look across the full portfolio of stores, recognize that each one is genuinely unique, understand what's actually driving results at each specific location, and then coach the manager on that specific driver — the same diagnostic discipline Module 3 built for comparative KPI analysis, now applied to identify a coaching target rather than only a performance ranking.

## Teaching a Manager to Run Module 5's Coaching Loop Themselves

The specific, practical content of meta-coaching: teaching a store manager to actually run the observe-debrief-practice loop and the GROW-model conversations this project's own C13 course already built, rather than simply telling the manager to "coach your team better." This means modeling the coaching structure directly — demonstrating a debrief using the observation-impact-alternative structure with the store manager watching, then having the store manager practice the same structure with a district leader observing and giving feedback on the coaching itself, one level removed from the original associate-level skill.

## Invest Disproportionately in Your Middle Performers

A specific, counterintuitive resource-allocation principle: rather than spending the most coaching time on the store manager whose location is deeply struggling, invest disproportionate attention in the solid, middle-of-the-pack managers — the "B" players. The reasoning is direct: a small improvement in a B-player manager's coaching skill has a much bigger impact on the district's overall results than the same amount of time spent trying to fix a deeply broken location, since a genuinely struggling store often has problems beyond what coaching alone can fix in a reasonable timeframe. A related, specific practice: turn B-player managers into A-players, and then have those A-players mentor the C-players directly, extending the district leader's own coaching capacity through the manager layer itself rather than trying to personally coach every manager equally.

## Accountability Without Micromanagement

A specific tension multi-unit leadership introduces, distinct from single-store coaching: some retail-industry commentary specifically raises the question of whether district managers are held accountable for store performance with the same rigor store managers are held accountable for associate performance. The practical resolution this module recommends: hold store managers accountable for the specific coaching actions this module builds (running real debriefs, using GROW-model conversations, following up on commitments), not just for the store's raw output numbers — since raw output can shift for reasons outside a manager's control, but whether a manager is actually running the coaching discipline this course has built is a specific, observable, coachable behavior in its own right.

## Putting the Vocabulary to Work

The complete meta-coaching sequence this module establishes: consciously identify which "hat" a given moment calls for, and specifically resist telling or solving when the moment calls for coaching; look across the full store portfolio to identify which specific manager needs coaching on which specific metric, using Module 3's comparative diagnostic discipline; model this project's own C13 coaching structures directly for a store manager, then have that manager practice the same structure with feedback on their coaching itself; disproportionately invest coaching time in solid middle-performing managers rather than spreading attention evenly or over-indexing on the most visibly struggling location; and hold managers accountable specifically for running the coaching discipline itself, not only for the resulting numbers.

## A Worked Scenario: The Manager Who Learned to Debrief

A district leader notices, across several store visits, that a specific store manager's own associate-coaching conversations consist entirely of generic praise or criticism, with no structure resembling the observation-impact-alternative debrief this project's own C13 course established. Rather than personally stepping in to coach that manager's struggling associate, the district leader spends part of the next visit modeling a real debrief with the manager watching — stating a specific observation, naming its impact, then asking the associate for their own take before offering an alternative. At the following visit, the district leader watches the manager attempt the same structure independently, offering feedback afterward specifically on the coaching technique itself: the manager's observation was appropriately specific, but they'd skipped straight to the alternative without asking for the associate's own view first. Three visits later, the manager is running structured debriefs independently, and that specific store's associate-level coaching quality — not just the manager's own selling skill — has visibly improved.

## Practice Exercise

Identify one store manager in your portfolio who is a solid, middle-performing "B" player rather than your most visibly struggling location. Using Module 3's comparative diagnostic approach, identify the one specific metric or coaching behavior most worth developing in that manager. At your next visit, model the relevant C13 coaching structure directly, then commit to observing that manager attempt the same structure independently at your following visit, giving feedback on their coaching technique itself rather than on the underlying associate performance.

## Key Takeaways

- Meta-coaching — coaching a store manager on how they coach their own associates — is a genuinely different skill from coaching an associate directly, and requires consciously choosing the "coach" role over the instinct to tell or solve problems personally.
- A district leader's job is to identify which specific manager needs coaching on which specific metric or behavior, using the diagnostic discipline this course already built in Module 3, rather than bypassing a struggling manager to coach that manager's associates directly.
- The practical content of meta-coaching is teaching a manager to actually run this project's own C13 coaching structures (the observe-debrief-practice loop, GROW-model conversations) themselves, through direct modeling followed by observed practice and feedback on the coaching technique itself.
- Disproportionately investing coaching time in solid, middle-performing "B" managers, rather than the most visibly struggling location, produces a bigger overall district impact, and turning B-players into A-players who then mentor C-players extends a district leader's coaching capacity through the manager layer itself.
- Holding managers accountable specifically for running the coaching discipline (real debriefs, GROW conversations, following up on commitments), not only for raw output numbers, keeps accountability focused on something genuinely observable and coachable.
