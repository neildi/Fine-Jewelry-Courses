# C14-M04 Outline: Coaching Managers on Coaching

## Module Metadata
- Course: C14 - Multi-Store Leadership: Store Visits, Audits, Manager Development
- Module: 4 of 8
- Persona: P8 (Multi-Store Leader), per official course architecture
- Working title: Coaching Managers on Coaching
- Learning objective: Multi-unit leaders can consciously apply the "hats" framework to choose the coach role deliberately, diagnose which manager needs coaching on which specific metric using Module 3's comparative discipline, model and observe this project's own C13 coaching structures one level removed, disproportionately invest coaching time in middle-performing managers, and hold managers accountable for coaching behavior itself.
- Terminal outcome tie-in: Directly fulfills the C14 terminal outcome "coach managers rather than associates" per this project's official course architecture.

## Section Map

1. **Why This Module Matters** — Frames meta-coaching as a genuinely different skill from C13's associate-level coaching toolkit.
2. **The "Hats" Framework: Knowing Which Role You're in** — The strategist/analyst/operator/coach framing and the telling-vs-asking pitfall.
3. **Coach the Manager on the Metric, Not the Associate on the Task** — The specific distinction preventing a district leader from bypassing the manager.
4. **Teaching a Manager to Run Module 5's Coaching Loop Themselves** — Direct modeling followed by observed practice, one level removed.
5. **Invest Disproportionately in Your Middle Performers** — The B-player-to-A-player-to-mentor-C-players resource-allocation principle.
6. **Accountability Without Micromanagement** — Holding managers accountable for coaching behavior itself, not only raw output.
7. **Putting the Vocabulary to Work** — The complete meta-coaching sequence.
8. **A Worked Scenario: The Manager Who Learned to Debrief** — Illustrative synthesis showing direct modeling and observed practice building a manager's own coaching capability.
9. **Practice Exercise** — Identifying a B-player manager and running the model-then-observe sequence.
10. **Key Takeaways** — Five-point recap.

## Production Notes
- The "hats" framework (strategist, coach, financial analyst, operator) and the specific coach-hat pitfall ("Telling instead of asking; solving problems for your managers instead of teaching them how") are sourced directly and specifically from eathealthy365.com's "The Responsibilities of a District Manager" article, which names this exact framework and this exact pitfall description.
- The "invest disproportionate time in B-player managers, then have A-players mentor C-players" principle is sourced directly from the same eathealthy365.com article ("Invest your prime time in your 'B' players... A small improvement with them has a much bigger impact... Turn your 'B's into 'A's, and empower your 'A's to mentor your 'C's").
- The "look across the portfolio, recognize each store is unique, understand what's driving results, coach the manager accordingly" principle, and the "what store/store manager needs coaching on what metric" framing, are sourced directly from a RetailWire discussion article ("Should district managers be held more accountable for store performance?"), which contains this exact framing from a named retail-industry commentator in the discussion thread.
- The accountability-without-micromanagement tension (whether district managers are held accountable for store performance with the same rigor as store managers for associates) is sourced from the same RetailWire discussion, which explicitly raises this question as its central topic.
- The general finding that "coaching other managers" and "teaching, training and developing" store managers are core, explicitly named district-manager responsibilities is CONFIRMED CONSISTENT across multiple independent job-description and role-definition sources: Kohl's official "District Manager, Stores" careers page, AppleOne's "Retail District Manager Job Description," Indeed's "District Manager Job Description," and multiple real job postings (Circle K, and others) consulted during this module's research, all explicitly naming manager-coaching as a distinct core responsibility separate from associate-level training.
- The GROW model as the standard framework managers can be coached to use with their own teams, without requiring formal coach certification, is independently corroborated by coachingcultureatwork.com's "Coach Training for Leaders and Managers" article, which confirms the GROW model's applicability to this exact use case and confirms managers do not need formal certification to use coaching techniques in their day-to-day role — directly supporting this module's premise that a district leader can teach GROW-model coaching to store managers without either party needing formal coach training.
- This module's specific application of C13's coaching structures (observe-debrief-practice, observation-impact-alternative, GROW model) to the meta-coaching context, including the direct-model-then-observed-practice sequence, is this module's own original synthesis, connecting the confirmed general meta-coaching principles above to this project's own previously-established, independently-verified C13 content; not itself an externally sourced claim.
- The worked scenario (the manager who learned to run structured debriefs) and the practice exercise are ORIGINAL illustrative syntheses created for this module, not externally sourced cases.

- Huddle card should focus on the "coach the manager, not the associate" distinction and the B-player-investment principle as the two most immediately usable takeaways for a district leader's coaching time allocation.
