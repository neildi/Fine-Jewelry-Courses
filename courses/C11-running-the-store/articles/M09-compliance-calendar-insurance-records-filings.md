# Compliance Calendar: Insurance, Records, Filings

## Why This Module Matters

Running a jewelry store well on the sales floor and running it well on paper are two different disciplines, and the second one is unforgiving in a specific way: missing a filing deadline or letting an insurance schedule go stale doesn't show up as a bad quarter, it shows up as an uncovered loss or a federal compliance violation exactly when the store can least afford either. This module lays out the specific, recurring compliance obligations a store leader owns — insurance-schedule maintenance, cash-transaction reporting, sanctions and origin documentation, and physical-inventory record retention — and the calendar discipline that keeps all of it current without becoming a full-time job.

## Insurance: Keeping the Schedule Current, Not Just Filed

A jewelry store's insurance coverage is only as good as the inventory schedule behind it, and that schedule goes stale faster than most managers expect. The specific, insurer-recommended cadence: refresh the itemized inventory list provided to the insurance broker every quarter or at minimum every half-year, plus a complete January export of the full inventory list and photo archive to the broker as a year-end anchor point. Beyond that recurring refresh, a full documented itemized physical inventory needs to happen annually, with the resulting records retained for seven years, stored off-premises so a single event at the store (fire, flood, robbery) can't destroy both the merchandise and the only record of what was lost. A schedule that hasn't been updated in eight months, after the store brought in a new high-value bridal collection, is a schedule an insurer can reasonably contest at claim time — the coverage gap is invisible until the moment it matters most.

## Form 8300: The Federal Cash-Reporting Deadline

Any cash receipt over $10,000 in a single transaction or in related transactions triggers a Form 8300 filing obligation with the IRS, a requirement enforced across the jewelry trade specifically because high-value cash transactions are a recognized money-laundering risk point. The specific deadline structure that matters for a store leader's calendar: Form 8300 itself has to be filed within 15 days of the triggering transaction, and a separate obligation follows on the calendar year — payer statements documenting the filing must be furnished to the customer by January 31 of the following year. A store manager who owns this correctly maintains a running log of every cash transaction approaching the threshold, files each Form 8300 within its own 15-day window as transactions happen rather than batching them, and treats the January 31 payer-statement deadline as a fixed, non-negotiable calendar anchor separate from the individual filing deadlines.

## AML, OFAC, and G7 Origin Documentation

Beyond cash-transaction reporting, dealers in precious metals and stones carry broader obligations under the USA PATRIOT Act and the Bank Secrecy Act, which require maintaining a documented customer identification program and screening transactions against the Treasury's OFAC sanctions list. A newer, increasingly consequential layer sits on top of this: G7 sanctions requiring documentary proof of origin for non-industrial diamonds of 0.50 carats and larger, driven by restrictions on Russian-origin diamonds entering G7 markets. In practice, this means a store's diamond intake and vendor-documentation processes now need to capture and retain origin paperwork specifically for any qualifying stone, not just for compliance's own sake but because a store that can't produce origin documentation on request risks real business-continuity exposure — an inability to resell, insure, or defend the provenance of its own inventory. The Jewelers Vigilance Committee's Sanctions Resource Center is the specific, credible reference point store leaders should use to stay current on this fast-moving area, since sanctions requirements have changed materially in recent years and a policy learned two years ago may already be out of date.

## The Compliance Calendar: What Happens When

Building this into a genuinely usable calendar rather than a vague ongoing worry means anchoring each obligation to a specific recurring point. Cash transactions approaching or exceeding $10,000 trigger a Form 8300 filing within 15 days of that specific transaction — this is event-triggered, not calendar-triggered, and needs its own tracking log rather than being folded into a monthly review. Insurance inventory schedules get refreshed quarterly at minimum, with a January export as the year-end anchor. AML customer-identification and OFAC-screening logs get audited monthly as part of the store's existing rotating departmental audit discipline (the same discipline this course covered in Module 4's high-value verification protocol). Origin documentation for qualifying diamonds gets checked at intake, every time, not on any calendar cycle at all — it's a per-transaction control, like the high-value verification protocol itself. And the full physical inventory, retained seven years off-premises, happens annually.

## Building the Store's Own Compliance Binder

Every obligation covered in this module belongs in the store's own written compliance record — the binder this course's terminal assessment specifically asks a store leader to produce, using the seven-element SOP structure from Module 8. A Form 8300 entry in that binder names the task (file within 15 days of a qualifying cash transaction), the owner (typically the store manager or a designated compliance lead), the standard (complete, accurate IRS filing plus a January 31 payer statement), and the exception path (what happens if a transaction is discovered late, or if a customer disputes the reporting). The same structure applies to the insurance-schedule refresh, the AML audit, and the annual physical inventory. A compliance calendar that lives only in one manager's head disappears the day that manager takes vacation or leaves the company; a compliance calendar written into the SOP binder survives the transition.

## A Worked Scenario: The Missed January 31 Deadline

A store correctly filed a Form 8300 within 15 days of a large cash sale in November, satisfying the immediate federal reporting requirement. But nobody had a system tracking the separate January 31 payer-statement deadline, and it slipped past unnoticed until a customer called asking about a tax document they expected to receive. The store manager traces the gap to a specific root cause: the compliance calendar had event-triggered items (the 15-day filing) clearly tracked, but no year-end anchor point for the deadlines that follow on the calendar rather than the transaction date. The fix isn't more vigilance — it's adding a fixed December reminder to the store's own compliance calendar specifically to review every Form 8300 filed in the prior calendar year and confirm all corresponding payer statements go out by January 31.

## Key Takeaways

- Insurance coverage is only as strong as the inventory schedule behind it: refresh the itemized list to the broker at least quarterly, export the full list and photo archive every January, and retain a fully documented annual physical inventory for seven years off-premises.
- Form 8300 has two distinct deadlines that a store leader must track separately: filing within 15 days of a qualifying cash transaction, and furnishing payer statements to the customer by January 31 of the following year.
- Beyond cash-transaction reporting, dealers carry USA PATRIOT Act and Bank Secrecy Act obligations for customer identification and OFAC sanctions screening, plus a newer G7 requirement for documented origin proof on non-industrial diamonds of 0.50 carats and larger.
- Origin documentation for qualifying diamonds is a per-transaction, intake-time control, not a calendar-cycle item — it belongs in the same category of discipline as the high-value verification protocol from Module 4.
- Every compliance obligation belongs in the store's own written SOP binder, using the seven-element structure from Module 8, so the calendar survives staff turnover rather than living only in one manager's memory.
