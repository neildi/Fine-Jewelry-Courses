# Vendor, Repair and Custom Pipeline Management

## Why This Module Matters

A store leader doesn't personally do most of the vendor negotiating, bench repair, or custom-design consultation happening in the store — but they own whether all of it actually runs on schedule, stays financially sound, and doesn't quietly damage a client relationship somewhere in the pipeline. This module covers the specific leadership-level responsibilities across three interconnected areas: vendor and memo/consignment relationships, the repair pipeline, and custom-order production — each of which this project's course catalog develops in much greater bench-level and associate-level depth elsewhere, but which a store leader needs to see and manage at the pipeline level, across every job at once.

## Memo and Consignment Goods: A Distinct Liability, Not Just Inventory

Vendor relationships in jewelry retail commonly involve memo and consignment goods — merchandise a store holds but doesn't yet own outright. This distinction matters specifically because these goods are treated as a liability while in the store's possession, generating a payment due only after the item is actually sold or consumed, rather than being simple owned inventory from day one. A store leader's specific responsibility is tracking memo and consignment stock as its own distinct category — not commingled with owned inventory in a way that obscures what the store actually owes a vendor versus what it already owns outright — and ensuring the store's POS system's stock-status tracking (in stock, on memo, on consignment, in repair, reserved) is actually being used correctly and consistently by the team, not just available as a feature.

## Vendor Terms Determine Real Floor Decisions

When a visiting designer or vendor relationship involves a minimum purchase commitment or a consignment split (concepts this project's C9 course, Module 5, developed in the context of trunk-show events specifically), a store leader's job is ensuring every associate working with that vendor's line actually understands the terms in effect — since those terms directly shape real decisions on the floor: how aggressively to push a specific line, whether a piece can be held or specially ordered after a promotional event ends, and what happens to genuinely unsold inventory when it's time to settle up or return goods to the vendor. This is a leadership responsibility specifically because an associate operating without this knowledge can make a commercially reasonable-sounding promise to a client that the store's actual vendor agreement doesn't support.

## The Repair Pipeline: Visibility Across Every Job, Not Just the One in Front of You

This project's C8 course (Custom Design & Repair Workflow) developed the full bench-level intake, repair, and quality-control process in depth; a store leader's specific responsibility sits one level up — holding visibility across every job simultaneously, at every stage, rather than tracking any single job closely. The specific weekly management report this project's research establishes covers exactly this: how many repair jobs were completed and ready for pickup, how many are open and within their promised deadline, and how many are overdue, with overdue jobs specifically flagged for immediate follow-up rather than left to surface on their own when an already-frustrated client calls to ask. A store leader who can name, at any given moment, roughly how many jobs are in each of these three states is actually managing the pipeline; a leader who can only describe individual jobs when asked is managing job-by-job, which doesn't scale past a handful of repairs at once.

## Custom Orders: The Same Pipeline Visibility, Higher Stakes

Custom-design consultations follow a documented workflow (discovery, expectation-setting, documentation, design iteration, an explicit approval gate before manufacturing begins, production tracking, and presentation) that this project's C8 course develops in full depth from the associate and bench perspective. A store leader's pipeline-level responsibility is the same visibility principle applied to higher-stakes jobs: knowing which custom orders are still awaiting client approval before production can even begin, which are in active production and on schedule, and which have slipped — and specifically ensuring that clients are being proactively updated throughout production, rather than only hearing from the store when their piece is finally ready or, worse, overdue.

## Where Vendor, Repair, and Custom Pipelines Intersect

These three areas aren't actually separate in practice. A custom order might depend on a specific stone sourced through a vendor relationship with its own lead time; a repair might require a specific finding or component available only through a particular supplier; a memo piece a client wants to purchase might need to be formally acquired from the vendor before the sale can close. A store leader's pipeline-management responsibility includes recognizing these dependencies across categories, not just managing each pipeline as if it existed in isolation — a custom order quietly waiting on a vendor shipment is still a custom order at risk of missing its promised date, even though the actual bottleneck sits in a different category entirely.

## A Worked Scenario: The Custom Order Waiting on a Vendor

A store leader's weekly pipeline review reveals a custom engagement ring, promised for a proposal date in three weeks, is technically "in production" but has actually been waiting nine days for a specific melee-diamond shipment from a vendor. Because the leader reviews the full pipeline rather than only individual job statuses, this gets caught during the regular weekly review rather than at week four when the bench jeweler finally flags it as blocked. The leader contacts the vendor directly to expedite or identify an alternative source, and proactively updates the client on the delay with an honest explanation rather than letting the promised date arrive with no warning. The dependency between the vendor relationship and the custom pipeline is exactly what made this catchable early.

## Practice Exercise

Build a simple weekly pipeline snapshot for your store covering three categories: repairs (completed/on-track/overdue counts), custom orders (awaiting approval/in production/overdue counts), and any vendor-dependent jobs currently waiting on an external shipment or delivery. Run this snapshot for two consecutive weeks and discuss with your team whether it surfaced anything that wouldn't have been visible from tracking individual jobs alone.

## Key Takeaways

- Memo and consignment goods are a distinct liability category, not simple owned inventory, and a store leader's job is ensuring this distinction is tracked accurately and consistently in the store's actual systems, not just theoretically understood.
- Vendor terms (minimum purchase commitments, consignment splits) directly shape real floor decisions, and every associate working with a specific vendor's line needs to understand the terms currently in effect.
- A store leader's repair-pipeline responsibility is visibility across every job at once (completed, on-track, overdue counts), not deep tracking of any single job — this is what actually scales past a handful of repairs.
- The same pipeline-visibility principle applies to custom orders, with particular attention to proactive client updates throughout production, not just at pickup or when a job has already slipped.
- Vendor, repair, and custom pipelines intersect in practice, and a store leader needs to recognize cross-category dependencies (a custom order blocked by a vendor shipment, a repair waiting on a specific component) rather than managing each pipeline as if it existed in isolation.
