# Scheduling to Traffic and Peak Coverage

## Why This Module Matters

A schedule built around convenience — the same shifts, week after week, regardless of what's actually happening on the floor — quietly costs a store money twice over: too many hands during slow stretches inflates labor cost against sales, and too few during a genuine rush means missed sales, rushed clients, and exactly the kind of understaffing this course's Module 2 warned drags on discovery and trust-building quality. This module covers how to actually schedule to real traffic, rather than to habit.

## Map the Real Curve Before You Schedule Anything

The starting discipline, confirmed consistently across retail scheduling guidance, is to pull several weeks of real data — POS transaction timestamps and door-count data where available — and plot traffic hour by hour across a typical day, rather than scheduling from instinct or last year's rota. General retail patterns show predictable surges during lunch hours (commonly 11am-2pm) and an after-work window (commonly 4-7pm), with weekend afternoons typically running heaviest overall — though the specific shape of this curve varies by location and should be confirmed against a store's own actual data rather than assumed from general retail patterns. A specific, useful distinction: traffic peaks and purchase peaks don't always align — a jewelry store might see heavy browsing at one time of day but the actual majority of purchases close at a different time, meaning floor coverage and any specialized closing support may need to be staffed for two different peaks, not one.

## Build the Schedule Backward From the Peak, Not Forward From Open

Once the real curve is mapped, the schedule should work backward from the peak rather than simply assigning hours forward from opening time. A specific, widely repeated technique is the "anchor": never start a shift exactly when a rush begins — if data shows traffic climbing toward a 5pm peak, shift starts should be anchored roughly fifteen minutes to an hour ahead of that point, so the floor is genuinely staffed and settled before the rush actually arrives, rather than still getting organized as clients start walking in. Staggering arrival times across the team (rather than everyone starting at the same hour) maintains coverage across the full day rather than creating an artificial dip right before the first shift change. As the day tapers, coverage should taper too — holding a full crew through a genuinely slow stretch is exactly the kind of convenience-based scheduling this module argues against.

## Two Real Constraints on Top of the Curve

Traffic data alone doesn't fully determine a schedule — two other constraints from earlier in this course apply directly. First, Module 2's two-person minimum for opening and closing, and for general floor coverage throughout the day, is a security requirement, not merely a service-quality preference, and it sets an absolute floor beneath which staffing should never fall regardless of how quiet the traffic data suggests a given hour might be. Second, Module 9's C9 (Compressed-Time Selling) course established a specific staffing benchmark for events — roughly 30-40 minutes per full, unhurried client conversation — which applies directly to any high-traffic period where the store wants full discovery-and-trust-building quality, not just enough people to avoid an empty floor.

## Sales Per Labor Hour: Use It as a Compass, Not a Universal Number

General retail scheduling guidance commonly cites sales-per-labor-hour (SPLH) targets somewhere in a wide range depending on store type and source — figures anywhere from roughly $40-80 to $150-250 appear across different general-retail sources. This module treats these specific published figures as a general-retail benchmark, not a jewelry-specific standard, given how dramatically average ticket size (and therefore revenue per hour of any kind) differs between typical general retail and fine jewelry. The genuinely useful, transferable practice is the underlying discipline itself: calculate a store's own actual SPLH during normal periods, use that store-specific baseline as the real comparison point, and watch whether SPLH holds up or improves after any scheduling change — not whether it matches a number pulled from an unrelated retail category.

## Non-Customer-Facing Work Belongs in the Dead Zones

The lowest-traffic hours of the day are exactly where non-customer-facing tasks — the inventory counts and audits from Modules 3-4, window and case merchandising from Module 5, deep cleaning — should be deliberately scheduled, rather than competing for attention during the store's actual peak hours. This is a specific, practical way the daily and weekly rhythms established earlier in this course get built directly into the schedule itself, rather than existing as separate obligations squeezed in whenever there happens to be a spare moment.

## A Worked Scenario: The Schedule That Was Technically Fully Staffed

A store's weekly schedule shows the same headcount every day, evenly spread across open-to-close hours — technically, every hour has coverage. But a review of six weeks of actual POS data reveals a clear, consistent Saturday peak between 11am and 3pm that the flat schedule doesn't account for at all, while Tuesday afternoons run consistently, measurably quiet. The store leader rebuilds the schedule specifically around this real curve: Saturday gets two additional staggered arrivals anchored ahead of the 11am climb, and the freed-up Tuesday afternoon hours get reassigned to the weekly cycle count and case merchandising review from earlier modules, rather than simply cut. Total scheduled hours barely change, but coverage now actually matches when clients are in the store — and SPLH during the Saturday peak improves within a few weeks, exactly the outcome this kind of schedule redesign is meant to produce.

## Practice Exercise

Pull your own store's actual POS data for the last four to six weeks and plot traffic by hour for at least two representative days (one weekday, one weekend). Identify where your real peak falls, and redesign one day's schedule specifically around that peak using the anchor technique (shift starts 15 minutes to an hour ahead of the climb) rather than the store's current default schedule. Compare the total scheduled hours in your redesign against the current schedule, and discuss with your team what non-customer-facing work could move into the newly identified dead zones.

## Key Takeaways

- Scheduling should start from real, store-specific traffic data (POS and door-count patterns over several weeks), not from habit, convenience, or general retail assumptions about when peaks occur.
- Schedules should be built backward from the actual peak, using an anchor technique (shift starts 15 minutes to an hour ahead of a rush) and staggered arrivals, with coverage tapering down during genuinely slow stretches rather than holding a flat headcount all day.
- Module 2's two-person security minimum and C9's compressed-time staffing benchmark (30-40 minutes per full client conversation) are real constraints on top of traffic data, not suggestions to be overridden by a quiet-looking hour.
- Sales-per-labor-hour targets cited in general retail sources vary widely and should be treated as a general benchmark, not a jewelry-specific standard; the transferable discipline is calculating and tracking a store's own actual SPLH baseline, not matching an external number.
- The lowest-traffic hours of the day are the right place to deliberately schedule non-customer-facing work (inventory counts, merchandising review, cleaning) established in earlier modules, rather than letting that work compete with genuine peak-hour coverage.
