# C11-M06 Outline: Scheduling to Traffic and Peak Coverage

## Module Metadata
- Course: C11 - Running the Store
- Module: 6 of 10
- Persona: P1/P7 (First-Time Store Leader, per official course architecture)
- Working title: Scheduling to Traffic and Peak Coverage
- Learning objective: Store leaders can map real store-specific traffic data, build schedules backward from actual peaks using the anchor and stagger techniques, apply Module 2's security minimum and C9's compressed-time staffing benchmark as real constraints, use sales-per-labor-hour as a store-specific compass rather than an external benchmark, and deliberately schedule non-customer-facing work into low-traffic dead zones.
- Terminal outcome tie-in: Directly fulfills the C11 terminal outcome "manage scheduling to traffic and peak coverage" per this project's official course architecture.

## Section Map

1. **Why This Module Matters** — Frames convenience-based scheduling as costing money twice over (overstaffed slow periods, understaffed genuine peaks).
2. **Map the Real Curve Before You Schedule Anything** — Data-driven traffic mapping; the traffic-peak-vs-purchase-peak distinction.
3. **Build the Schedule Backward From the Peak, Not Forward From Open** — The anchor technique, staggered arrivals, tapering coverage.
4. **Two Real Constraints on Top of the Curve** — Module 2's security minimum and C9's compressed-time staffing benchmark as non-negotiable floors.
5. **Sales Per Labor Hour: Use It as a Compass, Not a Universal Number** — General-retail SPLH figures flagged as non-jewelry-specific; store-specific baseline tracking as the transferable practice.
6. **Non-Customer-Facing Work Belongs in the Dead Zones** — Scheduling earlier modules' inventory/merchandising work into genuinely low-traffic hours.
7. **A Worked Scenario: The Schedule That Was Technically Fully Staffed** — Illustrative synthesis showing a flat schedule redesigned around real Saturday-peak data.
8. **Practice Exercise** — Mapping real POS traffic data and redesigning one day's schedule around it.
9. **Key Takeaways** — Five-point recap.

## Production Notes
- The data-driven traffic-mapping discipline (pulling several weeks of POS/door-count data and plotting hour-by-hour patterns rather than scheduling from habit) is CONFIRMED CONSISTENT across multiple independent retail-scheduling sources: TCP Software's "Employee Scheduling in Retail" article, shiftsynch.org's "How to Schedule Retail Staff Based on Foot Traffic" guide, and getdor.com's "Retail Peak Hour Staffing" and "Retail Staffing Optimization" articles.
- The general retail peak-time patterns (lunch hours 11am-2pm, after-work 4-7pm, weekend afternoons heaviest overall) are sourced from fieldpie.com's "Store Schedule Guide" and turnozo.com's "Retail Scheduling Software" article, both independently describing similar general peak windows; this module explicitly notes these are general-retail patterns to be confirmed against a store's own data, not assumed universal, per xshift.ai's "Retail Holiday Staffing" article's explicit point that "peak traffic and peak sales do not always align."
- The "anchor" technique (starting shifts 15 minutes to an hour ahead of a projected traffic climb, rather than exactly when a rush begins) and staggered-arrival scheduling are sourced from getdor.com's "Retail Peak Hour Staffing" article (explicit "Step 2: Build the schedule backward from your peaks... layer in the rest of the team about an hour ahead of your peak") and shiftsynch.org's guide (the specific "15-minute anchor" terminology and example).
- The traffic-peak-vs-purchase-peak distinction and the transactions-per-labor-hour (TPLH) staffing-formula approach for extreme peak periods are sourced from xshift.ai's "Retail Holiday Staffing" article.
- The sales-per-labor-hour (SPLH) content is sourced from getmyroster.com's "Retail Staff Scheduling" guide (citing a $150-250 range for "retail" generally) and turnozo.com's article (citing a $40-80 range); IMPORTANT SOURCING NOTE: these figures vary substantially between sources and are general-retail benchmarks, not jewelry-specific, given how dramatically average ticket size differs between typical general retail and fine jewelry retail. This module explicitly flags this variation and recommends store-specific baseline tracking as the transferable practice rather than treating any specific cited figure as a jewelry-applicable standard.
- The dead-zone scheduling principle (non-customer-facing tasks like inventory, merchandising, and cleaning scheduled into genuinely low-traffic hours) is sourced from shiftsynch.org's guide ("Schedule Tasks for Dead Zones... Inventory, window displays, and deep cleaning should never happen during power hours") and is this module's own direct connection of that principle to the specific inventory/merchandising work already established in Modules 3-5 of this course.
- The application of Module 2's two-person security minimum and C9's compressed-time staffing benchmark as constraints on top of traffic-driven scheduling is this module's own reasoned synthesis, extending previously-established and independently fact-checked content from both this course (C11-M02) and this project's C9 course (C9-M05 factcheck) rather than introducing new externally-sourced claims.
- The worked scenario and practice exercise are ORIGINAL illustrative syntheses, not externally sourced cases.
