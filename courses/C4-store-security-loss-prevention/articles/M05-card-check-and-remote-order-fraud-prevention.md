# Card, Check, and Remote-Order Fraud Prevention

## Why This Module Matters

Module 4 covered recognizing behavioral patterns before or during an in-person incident. This module shifts to a different but related skill: recognizing fraud built into the transaction itself, where the "attack" isn't a person casing your store — it's a payment method or a purchase request engineered to separate you from merchandise without a legitimate payment ever actually landing in your account. Card fraud, check fraud, and remote-order fraud all share the same underlying vulnerability: the moment between "this looks like it will clear" and "this has actually cleared" is exactly where a fraudulent transaction does its damage.

## Card-Present Discipline: Why the Imprint and Signature Matter

For a stolen card used in person, card networks generally cover the jeweler's loss only if the jeweler followed proper procedure: obtained authorization, got an imprint of the card, obtained the customer's signature, and followed proper card procedures generally. The blunt version of this rule: if you don't get an imprint of the credit card and the customer's signature, you are personally at risk for the loss, not the card network.

This is worth internalizing precisely because it's counterintuitive in the chip-card era — many associates assume that if a card was accepted and authorized by the terminal, the store is automatically protected. That's not how liability actually works for a stolen card used in person; the protective steps (imprint, signature, following procedure) still matter and are still what determines whether the jeweler or the card network absorbs the loss.

## Remote and Phone/Email Orders: The Riskiest Transaction Type

Remote orders — placed by phone or email rather than in person — are flagged specifically as one of the riskiest transaction types in the jewelry trade, because several risk factors tend to cluster together in the same order:

- An unknown customer with no prior relationship or in-store history.
- A request to ship to a remote location, often not matching the cardholder's billing address.
- A demand for next-day or otherwise rushed delivery.
- Pressure to complete the transaction immediately, often paired with urgency-driven language.
- A professional-sounding cover story that explains away any hesitation you might raise.
- A callback attempt specifically designed to divert shipment away from the cardholder's actual address.

The single most useful diagnostic question, borrowed directly from trade fraud-prevention guidance: why would a person in a distant city be buying jewelry from your specific store when they could buy from a dozen jewelers locally? A legitimate long-distance purchase usually has an obvious answer (a referral, a prior relationship, a specific piece they couldn't find elsewhere). A fraudulent one usually doesn't have a good answer at all — it just has pressure to move quickly before you ask.

## Checks and "Cleared Funds": Why Calling the Bank Isn't Enough

It's tempting to think that calling a customer's bank to "confirm funds" resolves the risk of a bad check. It doesn't, for a specific reason: a confederate working with the fraudster can remove the money from the account right after your verification call goes through, before the check itself actually clears and settles. The only surefire protection is waiting until the money has actually transferred into the jeweler's own account before releasing merchandise — not until a bank representative verbally confirms sufficient funds exist at the moment you call.

Specific check red flags worth watching for, drawn from trade fraud-prevention guidance, include: out-of-state personal checks, checks with no bank-printed return address, and checks with unusually low sequential numbers (suggesting a newly-opened or rarely-used account). Jewelers Mutual's overlapping red-flag list adds: post-dated checks, rushed in-person customers pushing to close quickly, phone/email purchase requests generally, unusually high phone-order values relative to what's typical, a customer who supplies their own phone number for you to call to "verify" (rather than using the bank's published number), demands for rushed shipment, and a second ID that doesn't quite match the name on the check or card.

## Layered Card Verification: No Single Control Is a Silver Bullet

Larger jewelers use a stack of overlapping card-verification tools rather than relying on any one control: Address Verification Service (AVS), CVV code checks, extra scrutiny specifically applied to first-time client transactions through an internal fraud-review step, tokenization of stored card data so the actual card number isn't retained in a vulnerable form, and point-to-point encryption. Chip (EMV) cards are part of this stack too, but with an important caveat: chip technology protects card-present transactions, not online ones — it does nothing to prevent the remote-order fraud patterns described above.

One further liability detail worth knowing explicitly: if a bank has issued a chip-enabled (EMV) card and a fraudulent transaction gets processed through a terminal that isn't EMV-capable, liability for that loss shifts to the business — and that specific loss type is generally not covered under standard jewelers' insurance policies. This is a strong practical argument for making sure your store's card terminals are actually EMV-capable and being used correctly, not just installed.

## A Documented Sales-Order Process, Not Ad-Hoc Judgment

Trade association guidance frames fraud prevention as something that should be a documented process, not left to each associate's individual judgment in the moment: establishing a clear sales-order process that flags risk concerns, defining which transaction fields are actually required before a sale proceeds, verifying card and cardholder data consistently, maintaining an internal database of known fraudulent transaction patterns your store has encountered before, and setting explicit internal goals for reducing fraud as a share of total sales. For online or remote transactions specifically, this includes screening card transactions more heavily for large-ticket and otherwise high-risk purchases, requiring email confirmations for approved remote orders, and acting promptly whenever a chargeback comes in rather than treating it as routine paperwork.

## A Worked Scenario: The Remote Order That Doesn't Add Up

A caller from out of state wants to buy a $15,000 tennis bracelet, pay by card over the phone, and have it shipped overnight to an address different from the cardholder's billing address because it's "a surprise gift being sent to a family member." They're pleasant, articulate, and seem to know exactly what they want. They ask if you can process it today because "it needs to arrive before the weekend."

Run this through the risk factors above: unknown customer (yes), remote shipping address different from billing (yes), rushed delivery demand (yes), a professional, ready-made explanation for the mismatch (yes), and pressure to complete quickly (yes). Any one of these alone might be an innocent, legitimate order. All five together, on a single call, is the exact cluster this module describes. The correct response isn't necessarily to refuse the sale outright — it's to slow the process down: verify through AVS and CVV, consider requiring the shipment go to the billing address instead, require an email confirmation trail, and apply the same internal fraud-review scrutiny reserved for first-time high-value transactions, even if that means the "weekend deadline" isn't met.

## Practice Exercise

Think of a recent high-value or unusual transaction you were part of, in person or remote. Walk it through this module's red-flag lists: card-present procedure (imprint, signature, authorization), remote-order risk factors (unknown customer, remote address, rush demand, cover story, pressure), and check red flags if applicable (out-of-state, no printed address, low check number, post-dated). Note which, if any, applied — and if several did, discuss with a manager what additional verification step should become standard for that specific transaction pattern going forward.

## Key Takeaways

- For a card-present sale, the imprint and signature — not just terminal authorization — are what typically determine whether the card network or the jeweler absorbs a stolen-card loss.
- Remote phone/email orders concentrate multiple risk factors at once: unknown customer, remote shipping address, rush delivery demands, and pressure to move quickly — ask why this specific customer is buying from you rather than somewhere local.
- A bank's verbal confirmation of "sufficient funds" does not protect against check fraud; only waiting for funds to actually clear into your own account does.
- No single card-verification tool is sufficient on its own — AVS, CVV, fraud review on first-time transactions, tokenization, and EMV all work together, and EMV specifically does not protect online/remote transactions.
- Fraud prevention works best as a documented, consistent process (defined required fields, an internal record of past fraud patterns, prompt chargeback response) rather than case-by-case improvisation.
