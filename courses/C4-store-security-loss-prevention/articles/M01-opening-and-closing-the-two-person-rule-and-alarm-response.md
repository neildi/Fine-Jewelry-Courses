# Opening and Closing: The Two-Person Rule and Alarm Response

## Why This Module Matters

Jewelry is one of the highest-severity, highest-shrink retail categories that exists, and the data on when crimes happen is specific enough to change how you should actually work. The Jewelers Security Alliance (JSA) logged 1,233 crimes against U.S. jewelry firms in 2025, down 13% from 1,420 in 2024, but total dollar losses rose to roughly $145 million, driven by professional crews cutting through walls and roofs to reach safes. JSA's 2024 Annual Crime Report found robberies peaked between 5:00-6:00 p.m. (26 events) with a secondary peak at 12:00-1:00 p.m. (21 events), and there was no 60-minute period during a normal workday when robberies did not occur; grab-and-run thefts clustered between 1:00-6:00 p.m.

This is exactly why insurers and security associations treat opening and closing as the single riskiest routine of the day, and why they've codified it into a specific, non-negotiable sequence rather than leaving it to individual judgment. This module walks through that sequence step by step.

## Why Opening and Closing Are the Most Dangerous Times

Multiple independent sources converge on the same core reason: opening and closing are when a store is most vulnerable to an ambush. JSA's retailer security guide states it directly: "Opening and closing are the most dangerous times for a jewelry store or business." JCK's security primer adds identity control at the door as part of the same principle: never open or close alone, and carefully check the identification of delivery personnel and other workers arriving during those windows — "if in doubt, keep them out."

UK jewellers' block insurance guidelines add a specific reason why this procedure must never be done solo: criminals may hide inside the premises overnight specifically to ambush the keyholder who opens the store the next morning. That single fact is why every major guidance source, across multiple countries and insurers, converges on the same rule: never open or close alone.

## The Two-Person Rule and the All-Clear Signal

Jewelers Mutual's 24/7 security guidance prescribes a specific sequence:

1. Use two or more people during opening procedures.
2. Inspect the surrounding area for suspicious activity or signs of a break-in before approaching the door.
3. Have one person access the facility while the other watches them enter safely from a secure distance.
4. The person who entered locks the door immediately and inspects the interior for signs of a break-in.
5. Only after giving an all-clear signal do other employees enter — and the door is locked again immediately behind them.

JSA's retailer security guide states the same rule and adds the practical reasoning: open and close with at least two people so one can open while the other watches from a safe distance, cell phone in hand, ready to call police if anything looks wrong.

UK block-insurance guidance formalizes this further with a signal protocol and key segregation: the holder of the safe/strong-room keys ideally waits outside while a colleague searches the premises, then the searcher gives a pre-agreed visual signal or makes a call to confirm entry is safe. Staff are advised to carry portable panic alarms, and a pre-agreed duress code lets the person inside signal that something is wrong without alerting whoever may be watching or listening.

## Key Segregation: Why the Same Person Should Never Hold Both Sets

A detail that's easy to overlook but treated as essential in every source reviewed: premises keys and safe keys must be held by different people, and must never be controlled by the same individual. This isn't a redundant precaution — it's a structural control. If one person holds both premises and safe keys, a single compromised individual (through coercion, collusion, or theft) can open both the building and the vault. Splitting custody means a bad actor needs to compromise two people, not one.

The same guidance extends this principle to the end of the day: one person leaves with the safe/strong-room keys and travels a suitable distance away before giving a pre-arranged signal to the colleague who then secures the premises. At night, all keys to the safes are removed from the floor, and display-unit keys are locked inside the safe itself. When staff members leave the business — whether through resignation, termination, or role change — intruder-alarm codes and safe combinations must be changed, not just the physical keys reissued.

## The Opening Sequence: Merchandise Out, Doors Still Locked

The correct opening order is not "unlock the doors, then set up the store." It's the reverse:

1. Keep the doors locked while removing merchandise from safes and vaults and arranging displays.
2. Do not allow anyone — even a customer or a delivery person — inside during this setup process.
3. Make sure all display cases are locked before officially opening.
4. Unlock the doors only when you are genuinely ready for business.

Window and display work follows the same closed-store logic: dress or undress window displays only when the shop is closed, and never let anyone into the premises before or after business hours for any reason, including while working on window displays.

## The Closing Sequence: Customers Out, Then Merchandise Away

Closing reverses the same discipline. At the close of the day, the store is cleared of all customers and the door is locked before any merchandise is removed from display for put-away. Never reopen the doors after regular business hours for any reason. Showcases are kept locked, and their keys are removed unless a specific case is actively being shown from.

Nightly put-away volume is not a matter of personal preference — it is an insurance question. Each store's insurance policy typically carries an "in-safe warranty" that specifies how much merchandise, by value, must be secured in the safe overnight to remain compliant with the policy. Trade sources describe jewelers who keep roughly 70% of inventory in-safe overnight as a common benchmark, using display trays, rolling carts wheeled directly into the vault, and descending showcase inserts to speed up the setup/take-down process. Whatever your store's specific in-safe requirement is, treat it as a hard compliance line, not a target to approximate.

One rule that gets violated more than any other, and that professional burglars specifically look for: never substitute covers for proper put-away. A showcase left loaded overnight and covered with a cloth or blanket doesn't hide anything from someone casing the store — it does the opposite. As JCK bluntly summarizes the burglar's logic: "Why's that covered? Because something is in it." Every slot in a display tray should be filled with merchandise or a placeholder marker specifically so a missing item is immediately obvious, and every closed-store night, all goods belong in the safe, regardless of value — even low-value pieces visible from outside the store are a liability.

## Daily Physical Checks

Two habits belong at the very end of the closing routine, every single day, regardless of how uneventful the day was:

- **Inspect window frames** to confirm they haven't been tampered with — this is a genuinely daily check, not a weekly or occasional one.
- **Inspect showcases whenever you clean the glass**, specifically looking for evidence of tampering (scratches near locks, misaligned tracks, adhesive residue) rather than just checking for smudges.

Suspicious persons or vehicles noticed during the day should be logged in a Suspicious Incidents Book — a running record used both as an employee training tool and as material that can be shared directly with police if a pattern or incident develops later.

## Alarm Response Is a Defined Procedure, Not an Ad-Hoc Call-Out

When an alarm signal comes in — including notices of communication errors or power interruption, which should never be dismissed as technical noise — the response follows a fixed procedure, not individual judgment:

- **Never enter the premises alone** in response to an alarm. Wait to be accompanied by police.
- Once accompanied, **investigate the roof, sidewalls, and all possible points of entry**, including access through neighboring premises — many jewelry-store burglaries specifically exploit shared walls or roof access from an adjacent unit.
- **Keep the alarm call list current** for vacations, holidays, and weekends, because most burglaries happen during weekend periods when a store is unattended for the longest continuous stretch.
- **Periodically test the alarm system** rather than assuming it's functioning correctly because it hasn't triggered recently.
- **Verify inbound alarm calls by calling back on the alarm company's published number** — not necessarily the number provided by the caller, since a fraudulent caller could provide their own number to intercept your callback.
- **Never enter the premises without the police or an alarm-company guard physically present**, even if you're confident the alarm was a false trigger.

## Safe Specification Is Part of the Operating System

Not all safes offer meaningful protection against modern burglary methods. JSA specifically states that safes rated TL-15 or TL-30 are not adequate protection against today's burglars, and that a TRTL-30x6 rated safe is significantly harder for a professional crew to penetrate. Beyond the rating itself, placement matters: safes should not sit against an outside wall or a wall shared with a neighboring business, since that gives a crew more time and more concealment to work through the wall rather than the safe door itself. Rear safe and workroom gates should stay closed and locked at all times, not just during off-hours.

## How This Shows Up in Real Job Specifications

Employers write this responsibility directly into sales-associate and manager job postings, which tells you this isn't optional floor trivia — it's a stated condition of the role:

- Associate level: "Maintain showcase presentation, merchandising standards, and daily merchandise counts. Follow all security, loss prevention, and cash-handling procedures with care and precision. Support opening and closing procedures, including secure merchandise handling."
- Manager level: "Oversee opening and closing procedures, cash reconciliation, and bank deposits" and "Manage day-to-day store operations, including opening and closing procedures, cash handling, and security measures."

## Key Takeaways

- Opening and closing are statistically the most dangerous routines of the retail day — robberies cluster around midday and late afternoon, with no safe hour, which is why the sequence is fixed rather than improvised.
- Never open or close alone: one person enters and gives an all-clear signal before anyone else follows, per Jewelers Mutual and JSA guidance.
- Premises keys and safe keys must be held by different people and never combined, to prevent a single compromised individual from accessing both the building and the vault.
- Merchandise comes out of the safe before doors are unlocked in the morning, and goes back into the safe after customers are cleared and doors are locked at night — never substitute a cloth cover for proper put-away.
- Alarm response follows a fixed procedure: never enter alone, wait for police, verify callback numbers independently, and treat every alarm — including technical-error notices — as real until proven otherwise.
