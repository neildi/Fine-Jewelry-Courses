# Recognizing Distraction Theft and Robbery Patterns

## Why This Module Matters

Modules 1-3 covered fixed procedures: how to open and close, how to secure a safe, how to run a showcase interaction. This module is different — it's about pattern recognition rather than a fixed sequence, because distraction theft and robbery don't follow a script you can simply execute. They follow patterns you can learn to notice, often early enough to change the outcome. The data is specific enough to be genuinely useful here: JSA logged 1,233 crimes against U.S. jewelry firms in 2025, and its timing data shows robberies peaked between 5:00-6:00 p.m. with a secondary peak at 12:00-1:00 p.m., while grab-and-run thefts clustered between 1:00-6:00 p.m. That's not trivia — it tells you when your attention needs to be highest.

## The "Meet and Greet" as a Deterrent — and a Diagnostic

Security guidance distributed to retailers frames the simple act of greeting every person who walks in as a genuine security tactic, not just customer service. A prompt, direct greeting does two things at once: it signals to a legitimate customer that they're being attended to, and it signals to someone casing the store that they've been noticed and can't move through the space unobserved. This is also your best chance to start noticing behavior that doesn't fit — someone who avoids eye contact while scanning exits and camera locations, someone more interested in staff positioning and case layout than any specific piece, or someone who asks unusually specific questions about security measures, staffing levels, or closing time.

None of these behaviors alone proves anything — plenty of ordinary customers glance at exits or ask when you close. The point isn't to treat every unusual behavior as a threat; it's to notice when several unusual signals stack up in the same visit, and to trust that instinct enough to alert a colleague or manager quietly rather than dismissing it.

## Distraction Theft: Built on Team Coordination

As covered in Module 3, an unusual cash display is a specific documented distraction-theft cue. It's worth understanding the broader mechanic it belongs to: distraction theft is rarely a single person acting alone. It typically involves at least two people working in coordination — one who occupies your attention (with a large cash display, an urgent question, a dropped item, a loud complaint) while a partner works to access unattended merchandise or exploit a moment when your normal protocol lapses.

This is precisely why Module 3's full selling protocol — one item at a time, never leaving a customer alone with merchandise, keeping the case locked except in the exact moment an item is being shown — matters most under the conditions that feel most chaotic. A distraction crew is specifically trying to create a moment where your attention is divided and your protocol feels reasonable to bend "just this once." The correct response to feeling distracted, rushed, or unusually charmed by a customer is to slow down and tighten protocol, not relax it.

## Grab-and-Run: Speed Is the Entire Strategy

Grab-and-run theft works on an entirely different logic than distraction theft: instead of trying to avoid detection, it relies on speed — grabbing visible, accessible merchandise and leaving before anyone can react, whether or not the theft is noticed immediately. JSA's timing data showing grab-and-run clustering between 1:00 p.m. and 6:00 p.m. lines up with peak foot-traffic hours, when a crowded floor makes both immediate detection and any physical response more difficult.

The defenses against grab-and-run are largely the same habits covered in Module 3: showcases locked except in the exact moment of showing an item, only one item out at a time, and — critically — never positioning yourself in a way that blocks your own path between the case and the exit. If a case is left open or a tray of rings is sitting unattended on the counter for even a few seconds while you help another customer, that's the exact window grab-and-run is built to exploit.

## Casing Behavior: What to Watch For Before an Incident

Trade security guidance distributed to retail staff describes casing as a distinct pre-incident phase worth learning to recognize, separate from the incident itself. Common casing indicators include:

- Repeat visits from the same person or people over a short period, with little or no purchase interest each time.
- Someone paying closer attention to staff movement, camera locations, and door/lock mechanisms than to any merchandise.
- Someone asking about closing time, staffing levels on a specific day, or when a manager is or isn't present, framed in a way that goes beyond ordinary customer curiosity.
- A vehicle parked and occupied for an extended period near the store entrance, particularly if it recurs.

As with behavioral red flags generally, no single indicator here is proof of intent — but a pattern across a visit, or a pattern that recurs across multiple visits, is worth documenting in the Suspicious Incidents Book (covered in Module 1) and sharing with a manager and, where appropriate, with the JSA weekly crime bulletin habit covered later in this course.

## Identity Verification: A Habit That Extends Beyond Opening and Closing

Module 1 covered identity checks specifically in the context of opening and closing — never letting a stranger in before or after hours, and carefully checking identification of delivery personnel. That same discipline extends into business hours: JCK's security guidance is blunt about the underlying principle — "if in doubt, keep them out." This applies just as much to an unscheduled "vendor representative," an unexpected "insurance inspector," or anyone else asking for access to a back room, safe area, or inventory records during open hours, not only to people at a locked door before opening.

## What to Do During and Immediately After an Incident

If an incident does occur — a robbery, an armed confrontation, or a theft in progress — the overriding general safety principle across virtually all retail security guidance is the same: personal safety comes first, ahead of merchandise. Do not physically resist or attempt to intervene. Comply with instructions, move slowly and visibly, and avoid sudden movements that could be misread as resistance. Once it's safe to do so, contact law enforcement immediately, and preserve the scene as much as possible — don't sweep up, rearrange, or "tidy" anything the police may need to examine, including camera footage retention.

Every store should have a specific written active-incident and post-incident plan reviewed with all staff, since exact steps (who calls police, who calls the alarm company, who contacts ownership/insurance, how customers on the floor are directed to safety) need to be store-specific rather than generic. Treat the general safety principle above as the floor, and your store's actual plan as the detailed procedure to follow.

## Practice Exercise

Recall the last time a customer interaction felt slightly "off" to you — even if nothing happened and you can't fully articulate why. Walk back through what specifically felt unusual: was it timing, behavior, a question that seemed oddly specific, or something about how they were dressed or positioned in the store? Practice putting that instinct into words you could actually say to a manager or coworker in the moment ("that customer keeps asking when you leave for the day" or "he's been circling the case for ten minutes without asking to see anything"). The goal isn't to become suspicious of every customer — it's to build the habit of noticing and naming a pattern early enough that quiet action is still possible.

## Why This Module Comes Before the Fraud and Compliance Modules

This module sits between the physical/procedural modules (1-3) and the transaction-based fraud modules (5-6) deliberately. Recognizing behavioral and situational patterns is a prerequisite skill for the more specific fraud-recognition content coming next — many of the same underlying instincts (does this feel rushed, does this feel engineered to divide my attention, does this request go beyond normal customer curiosity) apply just as directly to a suspicious card transaction or a pressured remote order as they do to an in-person casing or distraction scenario.

## Key Takeaways

- JSA's timing data (robbery peaks around 5:00-6:00 p.m. and 12:00-1:00 p.m.; grab-and-run clustering 1:00-6:00 p.m.) should raise your attentiveness specifically during those windows, not replace attentiveness at other times.
- A genuine, prompt greeting for every person who walks in is both good service and a security deterrent — it signals you've noticed them.
- Distraction theft relies on team coordination and your attention being divided; the correct response to feeling rushed or unusually distracted by a customer is to slow down and tighten your normal protocol, not loosen it.
- Grab-and-run relies on speed and an open window of opportunity; the same showcase discipline from Module 3 (locked cases, one item at a time) is your primary defense.
- Casing behavior (repeat low-interest visits, unusual interest in staff/camera/closing patterns) is worth documenting even when no single visit proves anything on its own.
- "If in doubt, keep them out" applies to identity verification at any point during the business day, not just at opening and closing.
- During an actual incident, personal safety takes priority over merchandise — comply, don't resist, and follow your store's specific written incident-response plan rather than improvising.
