# Perpetual Inventory, Cycle Counts, and Shrink Control

## Why This Module Matters

Module 2 introduced the idea of a perpetual inventory system — unique item numbers, category codes, documented movement forms. This module covers how that system actually gets used day to day: how often you count, what you count, and — critically — exactly what to do before you conclude a missing item is a real loss rather than a paperwork error. Getting this discipline right doesn't just protect against internal and external theft; it's also what keeps your insurance documentation accurate and your shrinkage numbers meaningful rather than noisy.

## Why Annual-Only Counting Is Inadequate

Trade guidance is explicit on this point: every store should do regular inventory control weekly, monthly, or bimonthly, rather than relying on a single once-a-year count. Practices cited by experienced jewelers include inventorying a different department each month, personally counting diamonds on a monthly basis, running frequent case counts, doing random counts of SKUs in back stock, conducting unscheduled checks, and running unannounced surprise inventories focused on specific categories. That last detail — unannounced and surprise — matters for a specific reason: an employee who knows the counting schedule in advance can prepare for it, which defeats much of the point of counting as an internal control in the first place.

## A Concrete Cycle-Count Model You Can Actually Run

One published, workable cycle-count model: count one category each week, so that the entire store gets counted by year-end, and errors get caught as they happen rather than accumulating silently for twelve months. For high-value items specifically, one documented threshold-based approach calls for anything above roughly €5,000 to receive a physical check every month, using a specific four-step process:

1. Two people count independently, without comparing notes first.
2. The two count sheets are compared afterward.
3. A third person recounts any items where the two counts differ.
4. Only after that third recount is the system of record adjusted.

This four-step sequence matters because it builds in a check against both simple counting errors and the possibility that one counter's number was influenced, intentionally or not, by seeing the other's number first.

## The Discrepancy Protocol: What to Do Before You Declare a Loss

This is the single most important sequence in this module, because getting it backwards — jumping straight to "this was stolen" — creates both inaccurate shrinkage reporting and unnecessary internal suspicion pointed at innocent staff. The correct order:

1. **Recount first.** A large share of apparent discrepancies are simple counting errors, not losses.
2. **Check whether the piece is in a repair box.** An item sent to the bench for repair is still store property but temporarily outside its normal inventory location.
3. **Check whether it's out on approval with a customer.** Items loaned out for a client to consider at home are exactly the kind of legitimate absence a rushed count can misread as missing.
4. **Check recent sales around that item number,** in case of a scanning error — a common mistake is scanning the wrong item number at the point of sale, which makes one item appear to have vanished while another appears to be duplicated.
5. **Only as a last resort, write it off as a loss or suspected theft.**

The cultural point underneath this sequence matters as much as the steps themselves: don't automatically attribute disappearances to miscounts, but don't automatically attribute them to theft either. Try to actually determine the cause of every disappearance, and investigate clusters — several missing items around the same time, category, or associate — quickly and thoroughly rather than treating each one as an isolated, unexplainable event.

## What the POS System Should Actually Support

A genuine perpetual inventory system, running on modern jewelry POS software, typically supports three distinct physical-inventory modes:

- **Full physical inventory** — the entire store scanned at one time, typically for the annual count.
- **Case count** — scanning specific locations as needed, used for the weekly/monthly cycle-count model described above.
- **Partial scan** — a list-based count reconciled manually, useful for spot-checking specific SKUs rather than a full location.

The documented workflow for running any of these: start the inventory session, scan tags location by location, stop and resume as often as necessary (a count doesn't have to happen in one uninterrupted sitting), run a reconciliation step comparing the system's records against what was actually scanned, and generate a report of missing items to work through using the discrepancy protocol above. Many systems can also be configured to require a physical location field for every inventory record, which is what makes case-by-case counting meaningful rather than approximate.

Every piece in the system should carry a serial number and a defined stock status — in stock, on display, on memo, in repair, reserved, and so on — because the discrepancy protocol above only works if an item's supposed current status and location are actually recorded somewhere, not held in someone's memory.

## Segregation of Duties in the Counting Process Itself

Several specific internal controls apply directly to how counting and inventory movement are handled day to day, not just to the count event itself:

- Dual control on signing for incoming merchandise — two people should sign for anything coming in, not one.
- The person who handles incoming shipments should not be the same person who conducts inventory counts on that stock.
- Job rotation on a regular basis adds a check on records, prevents any one person from concentrating authority over a category or process, and frustrates possible collusion between two people who might otherwise always be paired together.
- Digital keys and locks, where used, should be programmed per employee and per schedule, so there's a record of who accessed a specific case or location and when.
- Unscheduled inspections of records and inventory — by a manager, and separately by an outside auditor — reinforce the same "don't announce the schedule" principle covered above. Some owners specifically come in unannounced on their own days off to count, precisely because a predictable pattern (even an owner's typical schedule) can be worked around.

## Reporting Cadence Tied to This Discipline

Beyond the counting itself, this system feeds a broader reporting rhythm: insurers typically want an updated inventory list every quarter or half year, with a full annual export (commonly done in January, as covered in Module 2) plus a photo archive sent to the broker. The counting discipline in this module is what makes that reporting cadence accurate rather than a rough estimate updated only when someone remembers to do it.

## A Worked Scenario: Applying the Discrepancy Protocol

A weekly cycle count of the diamond case turns up a three-stone ring that's recorded in the system as "in stock" but isn't physically in the case. Here's the correct sequence, not skipping any step:

1. **Recount the case.** It's genuinely not there on a second pass.
2. **Check the repair log.** Nothing shows this ring going to the bench recently.
3. **Check approval-out records.** No signed approval receipt exists for this item.
4. **Check the last week of sales transactions** for this item number specifically. A sale is found three days ago — but it was rung up under a different, visually similar item's number by mistake at a busy moment.
5. **Resolution:** this is a scanning error, not a loss. The system is corrected to reflect the actual sale, and the "missing" ring is now correctly recorded as sold.

If step 4 had turned up nothing, and steps 1-3 also came up empty, only then would this become a documented loss investigation — logged, escalated to a manager, and treated as a real discrepancy rather than assumed to be one from the start.

## Practice Exercise

Think of a time (in this store or a previous one) when an item briefly seemed to be missing during a count. Walk back through what actually happened, and map it onto the five-step discrepancy protocol above. Which step actually resolved it? If you can't remember a specific instance, use this as a forward-looking exercise instead: the next time a count turns up a discrepancy, deliberately narrate each step out loud (to yourself or a colleague) before concluding anything, so the sequence becomes automatic rather than something you have to consciously recall under pressure.

## Key Takeaways

- Annual-only counting is inadequate; regular weekly, monthly, or bimonthly cycle counts catch errors as they happen rather than letting them accumulate silently for a year.
- A workable model counts one category per week (full-store coverage by year-end) plus a monthly four-step independent double-count for high-value items above a defined threshold.
- Before declaring any missing item a loss: recount, check repair boxes, check items out on approval, and check recent sales for a scanning error — only then write it off as a loss or suspected theft.
- Investigate clusters of disappearances quickly and thoroughly rather than treating each one as an isolated, unexplainable event.
- Segregation of duties in the counting process itself (dual signing, rotated roles, unscheduled inspections) prevents any single person from having unchecked control over both physical stock and its recorded count.
