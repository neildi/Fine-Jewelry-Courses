# Showcase and Selling-Floor Security

## Why This Module Matters

Every showcase interaction on the sales floor is a small window of risk, repeated dozens of times a day. Unlike a robbery or a burglary, showcase and selling-floor security isn't about a rare, dramatic event — it's about a routine you repeat so many times that it's tempting to get casual about it. That casualness is exactly what distraction theft and switch schemes are designed to exploit. This module covers the specific, sequenced habits that keep every single showing safe, whether it's your first customer of the day or your fiftieth.

## The Core Selling Protocol

Jewelers Mutual's selling protocol lays out a specific sequence to follow every time merchandise comes out of a case to be shown:

1. **Lock showcases immediately after removing merchandise** to show a customer — never leave a case unlocked "just for a minute" while you're mid-conversation.
2. **Loupe items before and after showing them** to a customer. This isn't just product-knowledge theater — it's a documented security control, covered in more detail below.
3. **Show only one item at a time.** Multiple pieces out simultaneously multiply both the switch risk and the grab-and-run risk.
4. **Require a photo ID when showing high-value merchandise.**
5. **Use a private showroom to show items of exceptionally high value**, rather than the open floor.
6. **Never leave customers alone with merchandise** — not even for a moment, not even with a customer who seems perfectly trustworthy.
7. **Always keep showcase keys with you**, using a wrist key holder rather than setting keys down on a counter or in a pocket you might forget.

## The Anti-Switch Control: Why You Loupe Before AND After

The loupe-before-and-after step deserves its own explanation, because it's easy to treat it as simply "checking the stone's quality" rather than what it actually is: a timed authenticity check built into the selling sequence itself.

JSA's retailer security guide frames it directly: examine and quickly polish each item when the customer hands it back to you, and replace it in the showcase. This gives you time to determine whether it's the authentic item — the anti-switch control. The sequence works because a switch has to happen in the moment the item is in the customer's hands; if you loupe the item both before you hand it over and immediately after you receive it back, you have a structured moment to notice a substitution (a cubic zirconia swapped for a diamond, a lower-karat piece swapped for the original) before the customer leaves the counter, when there's still something you can do about it.

This is also why the rule about never leaving a customer alone with merchandise matters as much as it does — a switch requires an unobserved moment, and the entire selling protocol is designed to remove every unobserved moment from the interaction.

## One Item at a Time, and Distributed Storage for High-Value Pieces

Showing only one item at a time isn't about slowing down the sales conversation — it's about limiting what's exposed and vulnerable at any single moment. If three rings are out on the counter simultaneously, a switch or a grab-and-run has three targets instead of one, and your attention is divided three ways instead of focused on one piece and one customer's hands.

This same distributed-risk logic extends to how high-value pieces are stored, not just how they're shown: AGTA/JSA guidance recommends that high-end watches and loose diamonds be distributed across several cases rather than concentrated in one location. Concentrating your highest-value inventory in a single case creates a single point of maximum loss if that one case is ever compromised — whether through a smash-and-grab, a switch, or an internal-theft scenario.

## Cash as a Red Flag, Not a Reassurance

It's intuitive to think that a customer showing you cash is a sign the sale is secure — no card decline risk, no check to wait on clearing. Trade guidance flips that intuition specifically for large cash amounts: "Nobody comes into your jewelry store and flashes $10,000 in cash. If they do, it's game on — you're probably going to be the victim of a distraction theft."

The logic here isn't that cash itself is dangerous — it's that flashing a large amount of cash is often specifically used as a distraction tactic, designed to shift your attention and trust onto the money and away from normal selling-floor discipline (locking cases, one item at a time, never leaving merchandise unattended) at the exact moment a distraction theft crew needs you to relax those habits. Treat an unusual cash display as a cue to tighten your normal protocol, not loosen it.

## Photo ID and Private Showrooms: Escalating Controls for Escalating Value

The photo-ID requirement for high-value merchandise and the private-showroom recommendation for exceptionally high-value items aren't separate rules — they're the same underlying principle applied at increasing intensity as value increases. A modest-value piece shown on the open floor with normal protocol is proportionate. A five- or six-figure piece deserves the added friction of ID verification and, ideally, a controlled private setting where you're not managing the item, the customer, and the rest of the open floor's foot traffic simultaneously.

This escalation logic is worth internalizing rather than just following as a checklist: the appropriate level of control should scale with what's actually at risk in that specific interaction, not stay flat regardless of the item's value.

## Wrist Key Holders: A Small Habit With an Outsized Payoff

Always keeping showcase keys on your person — specifically via a wrist key holder rather than a pocket, a counter, or a drawer — solves a failure mode that has nothing to do with dishonesty and everything to do with normal human distraction. Keys set down "for just a second" during a busy moment get forgotten, picked up by the wrong person, or simply lost track of during a hectic shift. A wrist key holder makes losing track of your keys physically require you to notice, because they're attached to you, not resting somewhere they can silently disappear.

## How This Connects to the Rest of the Course

This module's protocol works because it removes discretion from moments that feel routine. Module 1 established that opening/closing are dangerous because they're predictable; this module establishes that ordinary selling-floor moments are risky for the opposite reason — because they feel too routine to warrant care. Both modules point to the same underlying principle that runs through this entire course: security failures rarely come from dramatic, obviously risky moments. They come from the ordinary moments where the correct protocol felt unnecessary that one time.

## A Worked Scenario: Putting the Sequence Together

A customer approaches the case and asks to see a specific diamond tennis bracelet. Here's what the full protocol looks like end to end:

1. Unlock the case, remove only the tennis bracelet requested — not two or three "similar options" pulled out at once.
2. Loupe the piece briefly before handing it over, noting anything distinctive (a specific inclusion, a particular setting detail) that would help you confirm it's the same piece when it comes back.
3. Lock the case immediately, even though you're about to be standing right at it — the habit needs to be automatic, not conditional on how long you think the interaction will take.
4. Stay with the customer for the entire time they're handling the piece; never step away to help another customer or answer a phone while merchandise is out.
5. When the piece comes back, loupe it again, comparing against what you noted in step 2.
6. Return it to the case and re-lock, or complete the sale — either way, the piece never sits unlocked and unaccounted for.

If the customer then mentions they're also interested in a ring and a pendant, resist the instinct to pull all three out to "save time." Finish the bracelet interaction — piece back in the case, locked — before bringing out the next item.

## Practice Exercise

Think through your last five showcase interactions from memory. For each one, ask: did I loupe the item both before and after? Was the case locked the entire time it wasn't actively in the customer's hands? Was I ever managing more than one item out of the case at once? If you can't confidently answer "yes, yes, no" for any of them, treat that as a signal to slow the sequence down deliberately on your next several showings until it becomes automatic again.

## Key Takeaways

- Follow the full selling sequence every time: lock the case immediately after removing an item, loupe before and after showing it, show only one item at a time, and never leave a customer alone with merchandise.
- The loupe-before-and-after step is specifically an anti-switch control, not just a quality check — it creates a structured moment to catch a substitution before the customer leaves the counter.
- Distribute high-value inventory (watches, loose diamonds) across multiple cases rather than concentrating it, so no single compromised case represents your maximum possible loss.
- Treat a customer flashing an unusually large amount of cash as a possible distraction-theft cue, not a reassuring sign the sale is secure — and respond by tightening protocol, not relaxing it.
- Keep showcase keys on your person at all times via a wrist key holder; keys set down "for a second" are how ordinary distraction becomes a security failure.
