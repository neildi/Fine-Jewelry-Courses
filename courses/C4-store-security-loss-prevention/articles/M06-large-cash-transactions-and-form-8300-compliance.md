# Large Cash Transactions and Form 8300 Compliance

## Why This Module Matters

Modules 4 and 5 covered recognizing fraud and theft patterns. This module covers something different in kind: a specific federal reporting obligation that applies regardless of whether anything suspicious is happening at all. Any trade or business receiving more than $10,000 in cash in a single transaction, or in related transactions, must file IRS/FinCEN Form 8300. This isn't a loss-prevention best practice you can calibrate to your store's risk tolerance — it's a legal filing requirement, and getting the mechanics wrong (or right but mishandled) creates real compliance exposure.

## What Triggers a Filing

The $10,000 threshold applies to cash received in one transaction, or in related transactions — meaning a customer cannot avoid the filing requirement by splitting a single purchase into multiple cash payments across separate visits if those payments are connected to the same underlying transaction. Since January 1, 2024, electronic filing is required for businesses that must e-file certain other information returns, which covers most jewelry retailers of any meaningful size.

It's worth being precise about what counts as "cash" for this purpose in your own mental model: this filing requirement is specifically about cash (and cash-equivalent instruments in certain contexts), not about card or check payments, which carry their own fraud-prevention considerations covered in Module 5 but do not trigger Form 8300 on their own.

## The Required Filing and the Customer Statement

Filing the form with the IRS is only half of the requirement. The filer must also furnish a written statement to each person named on the form, on or before January 31 of the following year, containing:

- The business's name and address.
- A contact name and phone number at the business.
- The total reportable cash received from that person during the 12-month period.
- Language stating that this information is being furnished to the IRS.

An invoice can serve as this required statement, as long as it contains all the required elements listed above — you don't necessarily need a separate, standalone document, but whatever document you use must include everything the statement is required to contain.

## What NOT to Send as the Statement

This is the single most important operational detail in this entire module, and it cuts against what might feel like the "transparent" or "thorough" instinct: sending a copy of the actual Form 8300 itself to the customer as their statement is not advisable, because of the sensitive information the form contains. Use a properly-constructed invoice or a separate statement containing only the required elements — not the filed form itself.

And if the "suspicious transaction" box on Form 8300 was checked when you filed it: do not send the form as the statement, and under no circumstances should the parties named on the form be made aware that a suspicious-transaction filing was made. This isn't a suggestion about discretion — it's a hard rule. Tipping off someone that you flagged their transaction as suspicious defeats the entire purpose of the suspicious-transaction reporting mechanism and can create its own separate legal exposure.

## Documenting Your Attempts to Get Required Information

Sometimes a customer is unwilling or unable to provide all the information the form requires (a full legal name, an address, an identifying number). The IRS's own guidance encourages businesses to document their attempts to obtain the required information from customers, even when those attempts are unsuccessful. This documentation matters because it demonstrates good-faith compliance effort if the filing is ever questioned — the goal is to show you tried to get complete information through reasonable means, not to simply skip the filing because a customer was uncooperative.

## Connecting This to the Rest of Your Loss-Prevention Practice

This module sits deliberately after Modules 4 and 5 for a reason: the same instinct that helps you notice a distraction-theft cue or a clustered remote-order risk factor is relevant here too, but pointed in a different direction. A large cash transaction isn't inherently suspicious — plenty of legitimate customers pay in cash for entirely ordinary reasons. What Form 8300 asks of you is procedural discipline regardless of suspicion level: track the threshold accurately, file correctly and on time, furnish the required statement using the correct document (not the form itself), and never tip off a customer to a suspicious-transaction filing. The compliance requirement exists independently of whether you personally believe anything is wrong with the transaction.

## Related Transactions: Why Splitting Payments Doesn't Avoid the Requirement

The "related transactions" language in the filing threshold exists specifically to close an obvious workaround: a customer paying $6,000 in cash today and $6,000 in cash next week for the same piece, a layaway balance, or a connected purchase doesn't avoid the filing requirement just because no single payment crossed $10,000 on its own. If the payments are connected to the same underlying transaction or a series of transactions within a short period, they get aggregated for purposes of the threshold. This matters specifically in jewelry retail because layaway plans, deposits followed by balance payments, and custom-order deposits are common — and each of these payment structures needs to be tracked cumulatively against the threshold, not evaluated payment-by-payment in isolation.

## A Worked Scenario: The Layaway Payment That Crosses the Threshold

A customer puts a piece on layaway with a $4,000 cash deposit in March, then returns in April with another $4,000 cash payment, and finally pays the remaining $3,500 in cash in May to complete the purchase. No single payment exceeded $10,000. But the total cash received across these related, connected payments is $11,500 — over the threshold. Because these payments are all tied to the same underlying transaction (completing the purchase of one specific piece), Form 8300 filing is triggered by the cumulative total, not waived because each individual payment stayed under $10,000.

This is exactly the scenario the "related transactions" language is designed to catch, and it's the kind of situation where a store's own point-of-sale or layaway tracking system needs to flag cumulative cash totals per customer/transaction, not just per individual payment, so the filing requirement doesn't get missed simply because no one payment looked large enough to trigger it on its own.

## Practice Exercise

Think through your store's layaway and deposit-based sales (custom orders, special orders, bridal holds). For each payment structure your store commonly uses, identify: is cumulative cash tracked per customer/transaction, or only per individual payment? If it's only tracked per payment, that's a gap worth raising with a manager, since it's exactly the kind of structure where a real Form 8300 obligation could be missed without anyone realizing cash payments had crossed the aggregate threshold.

## Key Takeaways

- Form 8300 must be filed for any cash transaction, or related series of transactions, exceeding $10,000 — electronic filing has been required since January 1, 2024 for businesses that e-file other information returns.
- Beyond filing with the IRS, you must furnish a written statement to each named person by January 31 of the following year, containing specific required elements — an invoice can serve as this statement if it contains everything required.
- Never send a copy of the actual filed Form 8300 as the customer statement, due to the sensitive information it contains.
- If the suspicious-transaction box was checked, do not use the form as the statement, and never let the named parties learn that a suspicious-transaction filing occurred — this is a hard rule, not a discretionary judgment call.
- If a customer won't provide required information, document your attempts to obtain it rather than simply skipping the filing.
