# Safes, Vaults, and Nightly Put-Away Discipline

## Why This Module Matters

Module 1 covered the two-person rule for opening and closing. This module goes deeper into the single asset that rule protects: the safe or vault itself, and the record-keeping discipline that has to travel alongside it. A safe is only as good as three things working together — the physical rating of the safe itself, the discipline of what actually goes into it every night, and the paper (or digital) trail that lets you prove what's supposed to be there. Miss any one of the three, and the other two don't matter as much as you'd hope.

## Safe Rating Is Not a Detail — It's the Whole Point

As covered in Module 1, the Jewelers Security Alliance (JSA) states plainly that safes rated TL-15 or TL-30 are not adequate protection from today's burglars, and that a TRTL-30x6 rated safe is significantly harder for a professional crew to penetrate. It's worth understanding what these ratings actually mean in practice, because the label determines what your insurer will actually cover.

These Underwriters Laboratories ratings describe how long a safe is designed to resist a specific attack method under test conditions — TL ratings describe resistance to tools, TR ratings add resistance to torches, and the number indicates the tested resistance time in minutes. A TL-15 safe is tested to resist common hand and power tools for 15 minutes; a TRTL-30x6 safe is tested to resist tools and torch attacks on all six sides for 30 minutes. Given that JSA's own data shows professional crews now cut through walls and roofs specifically to reach safes, a lower-rated safe gives a determined crew meaningfully more working time — and working time is the one thing a burglar needs most and fears losing most.

Placement compounds the rating. JSA and AGTA guidance is explicit: safes should not sit on an outside wall or a wall shared with a neighboring business, because either placement gives a crew concealment and extra time to work through the wall rather than the safe door. Rear safe and workroom gates must stay closed and locked at all times, not only during off-hours — a propped-open gate during a busy afternoon is exactly the kind of momentary lapse that turns a rated safe into an irrelevant precaution.

## The In-Safe Warranty: Your Insurance Policy Sets the Floor

Every jeweler's insurance policy typically carries an "in-safe warranty" — a contractual requirement specifying how much merchandise, by value, must be secured in the safe overnight for coverage to remain valid. This is not a best-practice suggestion; it's a condition of your coverage. If a loss occurs and the store wasn't compliant with its in-safe warranty at the time, the insurer can have grounds to deny or reduce the claim, regardless of how the loss happened.

Trade sources describe jewelers who keep roughly 70% of inventory in-safe overnight as a common benchmark, but this number varies by policy and by store — some warranties are structured around dollar thresholds rather than percentages (for example, "no more than $X of inventory outside the safe overnight"). Whatever your store's actual policy language says, treat it as the hard floor, not a target to approximate on a slow night when put-away feels like it can wait.

To make full nightly put-away actually achievable within a reasonable closing window, trade guidance recommends specific tools rather than relying on willpower alone: display trays that lift directly into the vault, rolling carts wheeled straight from the sales floor into the vault, and descending showcase inserts that drop stock into a secured lower compartment without needing to be individually repacked. The goal is to make full compliance the path of least resistance, not the thing that gets rushed or skipped when the store is closing late.

## What a Vault Actually Needs to Hold — Beyond Just Merchandise

A vault isn't only a container for jewelry. Jewelers Mutual's inventory record-keeping guidance sets a specific floor for what documentation needs to exist and be protected alongside the merchandise itself:

- An **annual, detailed, itemized inventory** — not a rough estimate, but a piece-by-piece accounting.
- **Purchase invoices** for everything currently in stock.
- **Component cost records** — what you actually paid for stones, metal, and findings, not just a retail price.
- **Traceable sales receipts** for everything that's left inventory.

This documentation should be **retained for 7 years** and **stored securely off-premises** — a critical detail, because if your only copies of purchase invoices and cost records are sitting in the same building as the merchandise they document, a fire, flood, or burglary destroys your ability to prove what you owned and what it cost, right at the moment you need that proof most.

## Building a Perpetual Inventory System, Not Just an Annual Count

Beyond the annual documentation floor, Jewelers Mutual's guidance recommends building a genuine perpetual inventory system:

- **Unique inventory numbers** for every piece, so nothing is tracked by description alone.
- **Category codes** (for example, "CH" for chains, "D" for diamonds) that let you filter and audit by product type quickly.
- **Sales tags** that travel with the piece and get captured at the point of sale.
- **Documented forms for every movement type**: receiving, selling, returns, and inter-store transfers — each with its own paper or digital trail, not an informal notation.

This isn't just an administrative nicety. A perpetual system is what makes the discrepancy protocol in Module 7 actually work — you can't recount, check repair boxes, or check recent sales around an item number if the item never had a unique number to begin with.

## Special Handling Categories: Not Everything in the Safe Is "Your" Inventory

One of the most operationally important — and most commonly overlooked — pieces of this system is that not everything sitting in your safe or vault is store-owned inventory, and each category needs distinct handling:

- **Pawned items** — held as collateral, not owned by the store, with specific redemption and default terms that must be tracked separately.
- **Estate merchandise** — often carries different provenance and authentication documentation than new stock.
- **Memo goods** — owned by a vendor, held on consignment-like terms; the store owes the vendor either the item back or payment, not both.
- **Consignment goods** — owned by a customer, not a vendor; the store owes the customer proceeds if sold, or the item back if not.
- **Scrap gold** — often weighed and valued differently from finished jewelry, and may be awaiting refining rather than resale.
- **Customer repairs** — never store-owned, and require the documentation chain covered in later course modules on repair intake.
- **Non-invoiced goods** — anything in the safe without a matching purchase invoice is a red flag that needs resolution, not a category to leave ambiguous.
- **Custom-made jewelry** — often has a customer deposit attached and a production status that needs to be tracked alongside physical location.
- **Gold sold by the gram** and **bulk loose stones** — typically tracked by weight/count rather than by unique serialized piece, requiring a different counting method than finished jewelry.
- **Giftware** — lower average value but still requires the same discipline, since aggregate giftware shrink can add up unnoticed if it's treated as "not worth counting carefully."

The reason this level of category-specific handling matters: if your vault treats a memo watch, a customer's repair ring, and store-owned stock as functionally the same thing during a count, you will eventually either overstate your own inventory value or understate what you owe a vendor or customer — and either error surfaces at the worst possible time, usually during an insurance claim or an audit.

## Insurance Reporting Cadence: Keeping the List Current

Insurers typically want an updated inventory list every quarter or half year, plus a full annual export (commonly done in January) along with a photo archive sent to the broker. JSA adds a specific and easily-missed detail: jewelers should update their inventory list with their insurance company to reflect the current price of gold — a static inventory valuation from a year ago can significantly understate what you'd need to be made whole after a loss, given how much gold prices can move in twelve months.

## Practice Exercise

Walk your own store's (or a hypothetical store's) vault contents in your head, category by category, using the list above: store-owned stock, memo goods, consignment goods, customer repairs, custom orders, scrap gold, bulk loose stones. For each category present, ask yourself: does it have a unique identifier, a documented form tracking how it entered the vault, and a clear record of who actually owns it? Note any category where you're not confident the answer is yes, and raise it with a manager rather than assuming it's someone else's responsibility to have checked.

## Key Takeaways

- Safe rating determines how much time a burglar has to work, not just whether the safe "looks secure" — TL-15/TL-30 are considered inadequate; TRTL-30x6 is the higher bar cited by JSA.
- Your insurance policy's in-safe warranty is a contractual condition of coverage, not a best-practice suggestion — treat whatever percentage or dollar threshold it specifies as a hard floor.
- Documentation (annual itemized inventory, purchase invoices, cost records, sales receipts) must be retained 7 years and stored off-premises, so a single on-site incident can't destroy both your merchandise and your proof of what it was.
- A genuine perpetual inventory system — unique numbers, category codes, and documented forms for every movement — is what makes later discrepancy investigation possible at all.
- Not everything in the safe is store-owned: pawned, memo, consignment, repair, and custom-order items each carry different ownership and liability, and must be tracked as distinct categories, not lumped in with regular stock.
