# Running the Room: Trunk Show and Pop-Up Event Logistics

## Why This Module Matters

Everything in Modules 2 through 4 assumed a single conversation between one associate and one client. Real compressed-time events involve dozens of conversations happening at once, a visiting designer's inventory that isn't the store's own, and genuinely elevated security risk simply because so much high-value merchandise is concentrated in one place for a short window. This module covers the operational layer underneath the sales conversation — staffing, setup, security, and vendor terms — that determines whether the skills from earlier modules actually get a fair chance to work.

## Security: The Risk Is Real and Well-Documented

The jewelry industry's own security experts are explicit that trunk shows and similar events represent genuine, elevated risk, not a hypothetical concern. The Jewelers' Security Alliance has specifically warned that trunk show season is "a magnet for criminals," precisely because these events concentrate unusually high-value merchandise, often including a visiting designer's inventory, into a temporary setup that doesn't have a store's normal built-in security infrastructure.

The core practices that address this risk directly:

- **Display only one high-value item at a time**, keeping everything else secured in locked cases, exactly as during normal store operations, and inspecting each piece before returning it to the case.
- **Never leave the event area unattended**, and assign at least two staff members specifically during setup and teardown — the two most vulnerable windows of the entire event, since attention is naturally divided and merchandise is more exposed than during the show itself.
- **Conduct and document inventory counts** at arrival, multiple times throughout the event, and again before closing — not just once at the start and once at the end.
- **If a visiting designer is bringing outside inventory**, get a list and photos of everything being brought in advance, clarify what's actually covered by the designer's own insurance versus the store's, and consider armed security for a designer's items being moved in and out, since JSA sources specifically note that when you invite people to a high-value event, "you have a responsibility to make sure that they're safe."
- **Brief every staff member on a predetermined alert phrase** that signals a potential problem without alarming clients — a code phrase decided on before the event, not improvised in the moment.
- **Consider hiring dedicated event security**, meeting with the guard beforehand to review the actual layout, identify the specific vulnerable points of this particular space, and confirm the guard is present through setup, the full event, and teardown — not just show hours.

## Staffing: Matching People to the Actual Conversation Load

Compressed-time selling depends on the discovery, trust-building, and closing work from earlier modules getting real, unhurried attention — which is only possible if staffing actually supports it. A useful benchmark from trunk-show planning guidance: a full, unhurried client conversation (discovery through close) commonly runs 30-40 minutes, meaning a single consultation station can realistically handle somewhere in the range of 12-16 client conversations across a full event day. Understaffing an event relative to expected attendance doesn't just slow things down — it forces the exact discovery-compression and trust-building work from Modules 2-3 to compress further than the format can actually support, degrading the outcome for every client, not just the ones at the end of the line.

The practical implication: staffing plans should work backward from expected attendance and the realistic time-per-conversation figure, rather than simply assigning "everyone who's scheduled that day" without matching headcount to actual expected conversation volume.

## Vendor Terms: Know What's Actually Being Agreed To

When a trunk show involves a visiting designer's inventory rather than the store's own stock, the commercial terms matter as much as the sales technique. Common industry structures include a minimum purchase commitment (the store agrees to buy a minimum dollar amount of the designer's line, sold or not) and consignment-style terms (unsold inventory returns to the designer, with the store keeping a negotiated split — commonly the store retaining the retail markup while the designer receives wholesale pricing on what actually sells). Whichever structure applies to a given event, every associate working the floor should understand it, since it directly affects real decisions during the event — how aggressively to push a specific line, whether a piece can be held or specially ordered after the show ends, and what happens to anything genuinely unsold when the designer's inventory travels to the next stop.

## Setup and the Day-Of Sequence

Trunk show planning guidance consistently recommends arriving well before doors open — commonly at least two hours ahead — specifically to allow real time for inventory verification against the designer's or vendor's list, display setup, and a team briefing before the first client arrives. That team briefing should cover, at minimum: the security plan and alert phrase, the vendor terms currently in effect, staffing assignments and consultation station locations, and any last-minute inventory notes (a piece that arrived with an issue, a specific item the designer wants highlighted). Skipping this briefing to save fifteen minutes of setup time routinely costs far more than fifteen minutes once the event is underway and something needs to be improvised on the spot instead.

## A Worked Scenario: The Morning Before a Trunk Show

A store manager arrives three hours before a visiting designer's trunk show. The designer's inventory is checked against the provided list and photographed as it's unpacked, revealing one piece not on the original manifest — this gets flagged and confirmed with the designer immediately, before the show opens, rather than discovered mid-event. Two staff members are assigned specifically to the setup window, per the two-person minimum for vulnerable periods. The team briefing covers the day's alert phrase, confirms the consignment split in effect for this particular designer, and assigns four associates to two consultation areas, worked backward from an expected 40 RSVPs and the 30-40-minute-per-conversation benchmark. By the time doors open, every associate knows the security plan, the vendor terms, and their specific role — none of which had to be figured out on the fly once clients started arriving.

Compare your draft against a colleague's and discuss any gaps.

## Key Takeaways

- Trunk shows and similar events carry genuine, well-documented elevated security risk, not a hypothetical concern — the Jewelers' Security Alliance specifically warns that trunk show season is "a magnet for criminals."
- Core security practices include displaying one high-value item at a time, a strict two-person minimum during the vulnerable setup and teardown windows, documented multi-point inventory counts, and a predetermined staff alert phrase.
- Staffing plans should work backward from expected attendance and a realistic 30-40-minute-per-conversation benchmark, since understaffing forces the discovery and trust-building work from earlier modules to compress further than the format can support.
- Every associate working a vendor's trunk show should understand the actual commercial terms in effect (minimum purchase commitment vs. consignment split), since those terms directly shape real decisions made on the floor during the event.
- Arriving well before doors open (commonly at least two hours) for inventory verification, setup, and a full team briefing prevents exactly the kind of on-the-fly improvisation that costs far more time once the event is underway.
