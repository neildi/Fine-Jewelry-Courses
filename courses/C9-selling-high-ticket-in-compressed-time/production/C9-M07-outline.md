# C9-M07 Outline: When It Doesn't Close On the Spot: Fast, Honest Follow-Up

## Module Metadata
- Course: C9 - Selling High-Ticket in Compressed Time
- Module: 7 of 8
- Persona: P1
- Working title: When It Doesn't Close On the Spot: Fast, Honest Follow-Up
- Learning objective: Associates can explain why fast follow-up matters disproportionately after a compressed-time conversation, apply a realistic multi-touch follow-up cadence, and distinguish honest, value-adding follow-up from manufactured-pressure follow-up.
- Terminal outcome tie-in: "Recover a compressed-time opportunity that didn't close on the spot, using appropriate fast follow-up."

## Section Map

1. **Why This Module Matters** — Frames an unclosed conversation as a normal outcome, not a technique failure, and follow-up as the next real opportunity.
2. **The Window Closes Faster Than It Feels Like It Should** — Speed-to-lead research and its steep response-time drop-off curve; explicit domain-adaptation flag.
3. **A Realistic Follow-Up Cadence for an Event** — Multi-touch cadence (24-hour, several-days, one-to-two-week) and the persistence-vs-single-attempt finding.
4. **What Makes Follow-Up Feel Honest Rather Than Pushy** — Direct application of Module 1's urgency-vs-pressure and Module 3's genuine-vs-generic-attention principles to the follow-up context.
5. **A Worked Scenario: The Morning After** — Illustrative synthesis showing a fast, honest, value-adding follow-up sequence leading to an eventual close.
6. **Practice Exercise** — Drafting a full three-touch follow-up sequence and auditing it for inadvertent manufactured pressure.
7. **Key Takeaways** — Five-point recap.

## Ties to Other Modules
- Directly fulfills the C9 terminal outcome on recovering an unclosed compressed-time opportunity via fast follow-up.
- Directly reapplies Module 1's genuine-urgency-vs-manufactured-pressure distinction to the follow-up context, explicitly noting that a client who already declined once is specifically primed to notice pressure.
- Directly reapplies Module 3's genuine-vs-generic-attention principle (referencing the actual conversation rather than a template) to follow-up messaging.
- Completes the sales-conversation arc that began with Module 2's discovery, running through Module 4's close, by covering the realistic case where the close doesn't happen in the room.

## Production Notes
- The speed-to-lead response-time statistics (leads contacted within 5 minutes commonly found to be roughly 21x more likely to qualify than those contacted after 30 minutes; qualification odds dropping sharply, by a factor of dozens, when follow-up is delayed to 24+ hours vs. the first hour) are CONFIRMED CONSISTENT across an unusually large number of independent sources, most tracing back to the same foundational MIT/InsideSales.com Lead Response Management study: Verse.ai's "25 Eye-Opening Speed to Lead Statistics," LeadAngel's speed-to-lead statistics article, ZoomInfo's "Sales Follow Up Statistics" (explicitly citing a Harvard Business Review 2011 study on 7x qualification within one hour), Amplemarket's speed-to-lead article, farias.co's post-event marketing strategy article (explicitly citing the same MIT/InsideSales.com study and translating it to a 60x qualification penalty for waiting one hour), and resources.rework.com's "Lead Response Time" playbook (providing a detailed response-time drop-off curve). IMPORTANT SOURCING NOTE: essentially all of these sources are general (B2B/marketing) sales-response-time research, not jewelry- or event-retail-specific — this module explicitly and repeatedly flags this as a directional finding adapted to the jewelry compressed-time-event context, not a jewelry-specific statistic, given the strong likelihood that exact multipliers vary by industry and context.
- The multi-touch post-event follow-up cadence (24-hour initial touch; several-days value-add touch; one-to-two-week final low-pressure check-in) is adapted from momencio.com's "10 post-event follow-up emails to convert leads into sales" article (recommending a within-24-to-48-hour first touch, a 3-5-day second touch, and a 1-2-week final touch) and farias.co's post-event marketing strategy article (a similarly staged day-by-day cadence). Both sources are general event-marketing sources (not jewelry-specific), adapted here to a compressed-time jewelry sales-event context.
- The persistence finding (a large share of eventual sales require 5+ contacts, while a substantial share of salespeople give up after one attempt) is sourced from ZoomInfo's "Sales Follow Up Statistics" article ("80% of deals need 5+ contacts, yet 44% of reps quit after one attempt"), again a general sales-research source rather than jewelry-specific, and presented as a directional finding on persistence rather than a precise industry-specific figure.
- The application of Module 1's urgency-vs-pressure distinction and Module 3's genuine-vs-generic-attention principle to the follow-up context is this module's own direct extension of those previously-established and fact-checked C9 principles, not a new externally-sourced claim requiring independent verification here.
- The worked scenario and practice exercise are ORIGINAL illustrative syntheses, not externally sourced cases.
