## Does your team know who they are the moment they walk in?

A luxury clienteling platform's own framing of this question is worth sitting with: "Your top 5% of clients generate over 40% of your revenue. Do your store associates know who they are the moment they walk through the door?" A parallel figure from KPMG's 2025 luxury industry report, reported by The Straits Times, puts a similar concentration on the very top: VICs, fewer than 2 percent of a brand's customers, generate close to 40 percent of revenue. Whichever exact cut you use, the underlying fact is the same. A small number of clients in any jewelry book carry a disproportionate share of its future revenue, and if your service, attention, and outreach are spread evenly across every name in your file, you are almost certainly under-serving the relationships that matter most and over-committing time to ones that don't need it yet.

## Why this is a genuine operational risk, not just a nice-to-have

The risk of not segmenting isn't abstract. A seasoned associate at a busy store may have a real relationship with 200 clients, but without a system to prioritize and structure that book, she can realistically stay in regular, personal contact with maybe 30 of them. The other 170 drift, not from neglect exactly, but from the simple math of attention. Worse, if that associate leaves the store, her personal knowledge of who her best clients actually are, and why, walks out the door with her, unless it has been captured somewhere the rest of the team can see.

## Five signals that actually identify a VIC

Rather than guessing by gut feel or simply ranking by total spend, a more reliable segmentation approach looks at five separate signals together, per Endear's own clienteling framework for luxury retail:

| Signal | What it looks like in a jewelry book |
|---|---|
| Revenue concentration | Total lifetime spend and how recently they last purchased, not just a single big-ticket sale years ago |
| Purchase frequency | How often they buy, and whether that pace is increasing, steady, or declining |
| Category depth | Whether they buy across categories (bridal, fashion jewelry, watches, repairs) or stay loyal to one product type |
| Engagement signals | Do they respond to your outreach? Attend events? Refer other clients to you? |
| Relationship potential | A client with modest spend so far but clearly early in a growing relationship, worth investing in even though today's numbers look small |

That last signal matters more than it might seem. A client who just bought her first fashion-jewelry piece from you, mentioned she's newly engaged, and responded warmly to a follow-up text is a completely different prioritization case than a client with an identical spend total who bought once, years ago, and never responded to anything since, even though their numbers on paper might look similar today.

## How wide should the top tier be

Conventional practice reserves the VIC label for a brand's top 1 to 2 percent of spenders, but Gogo Cheng, a client-loyalty consultant who has led client departments at Louis Vuitton and Burberry, notes that many brands are now widening that circle to the top 5 or even 10 percent, precisely because fewer new, entry-level buyers are walking in the door than a decade ago. Her framing of the stakes is direct: "The one percent could protect up to 30 percent of revenue, but what about 70 percent?" For an independent jewelry store, the exact percentage matters less than the principle: decide, deliberately, how wide your top tier is based on your own book's actual purchase distribution, not an arbitrary round number, and revisit that line periodically as the book changes.

## The four things a top-tier client actually gets

Cheng's research splits VIC treatment into four categories, and while a global maison's version of these (private jet trips, dinners with a fashion house's owning family) is well beyond a single jewelry store's budget, the same four categories translate to something realistic at almost any scale:

- **Accessibility.** First look at new arrivals before they hit the case; a private appointment outside normal walk-in hours for a client who prefers discretion.
- **Service.** A dedicated associate who actually knows the client's history, rather than whoever happens to be free; priority scheduling for repairs and resizing.
- **Experiences.** An invitation to a small in-store event, a trunk show preview, or simply being remembered and greeted by name and history the moment they walk in.
- **Recognition.** Small, specific touches that come from genuinely knowing the client, referencing a past purchase by name, remembering a detail about their family or an upcoming occasion, rather than a generic loyalty-tier email.

None of these four require a large budget. They require the client profile discipline from Module 1, the cadence discipline from Module 2, and the daily habit from Module 3, applied with deliberate priority to the clients who have earned it.

## Tiering without abandoning everyone else

The most important caution in this entire module is the one Cheng herself makes explicit: the relationship skills used on a top client are "actually very applicable to everyday customers," and the smarter brands are learning to extend at least some of that same attentiveness broadly, not reserve it entirely for the top of the pyramid. Segmentation should scale the intensity of attention, not create a hard line between clients who get real service and clients who get none. A practical three-tier structure keeps this honest:

| Tier | Approximate share of book | What they get |
|---|---|---|
| Tier 1 (top VICs) | Top 5-10%, calibrated to your own book's distribution | Personal associate relationship, first access, proactive outreach ahead of new arrivals, priority scheduling |
| Tier 2 (regulars) | Next meaningful slice, frequent but not top-tier spenders | Regular, personalized outreach on the Module 2 cadence, with a lighter touch than Tier 1 |
| Tier 3 (everyone else) | The remaining majority | Consistent, well-timed automation-supported outreach per Module 2's calendar, still genuinely personal, never neglected entirely |

## Objections, mistakes and edge cases

| Situation | The trap | Better move |
|---|---|---|
| Ranking clients by total historical spend alone | Missing recency, frequency, and relationship-potential signals entirely | Use all five signals together: revenue, recency, frequency, category depth, engagement, and relationship potential |
| A client has high spend from one purchase years ago and nothing since | Treating them as a current top-tier client based on outdated history | Weight recency; a client who hasn't engaged in years belongs in a win-back flow (Module 2), not automatic Tier 1 status |
| A newer client shows strong engagement but modest spend so far | Ignoring them because today's numbers look unremarkable | Recognize relationship potential; some of tomorrow's top clients are today's promising newer relationships |
| An associate with a large book tries to personally manage all 200 relationships | Attention gets spread too thin and top relationships suffer | Accept the real capacity limit (roughly 30 genuinely personal relationships) and let the CRM and cadence system support the rest |
| A top associate leaves the store | Losing all institutional knowledge of who the real VICs were and why | Make sure Tier 1 status and the reasoning behind it live in the shared CRM, not only in one person's memory |
| Segmenting is treated as a one-time exercise | Tiers become stale as clients' behavior changes | Revisit the tiering periodically, since purchase pace and engagement shift over time |
| Lower-tier clients feel like an afterthought | The store's best relationship habits only ever get applied to the top of the list | Apply the same attentiveness principles broadly, scaled by capacity, not entirely withheld below Tier 1 |

## On the floor: applying it this week

- **Monday:** Pull your own client list and sort by the five signals (revenue, recency, frequency, category depth, engagement, relationship potential), not just total spend.
- **Tuesday:** Identify your realistic Tier 1: the clients whose relationship you can actually maintain personally, given your real capacity, not an aspirational number.
- **Wednesday:** For one Tier 1 client, plan a specific accessibility, service, experience, or recognition touch this month, no large budget required.
- **Thursday:** Check whether your Tier 1 reasoning is documented in the shared CRM, not just in your own head, so a colleague could step in if needed.
- **Friday:** Review one client who looks unremarkable on total spend but shows strong recent engagement, and consider whether they belong higher in your tiering than their numbers alone suggest.
- **All week:** Notice whether your Tier 3 clients are still getting the Module 2 cadence consistently, or whether attention has quietly drifted entirely toward the top of the book.
