## Two clients, two kinds of silence

One client bought a bracelet eighteen months ago and has heard nothing since. She has, in practical terms, been forgotten. Another client bought a ring last spring and has received four unrelated promotional texts since, none of which mentioned her by name or referenced what she actually bought. She unsubscribed after the third one. Neither client experienced anything resembling clienteling. One got silence; the other got noise. Both are now less likely to buy from this store again than the day they walked out with their purchase.

A clienteling calendar solves both problems with the same tool: a small number of specific, well-timed triggers, each with a real reason to exist, run consistently rather than either abandoned or blasted. As one jewelry-industry marketing consultant put it, describing what makes automated outreach still feel personal, the standard to hold yourself to is that "automation should feel like thoughtful anticipation, not mass marketing."

## The post-sale sequence: 30, 90, 365

The single most reliable cadence a new associate can run starts the moment a sale closes, using a three-step timeline that WJewel, a jewelry retail software provider, describes as simple, scalable, and absent from most stores. Thirty days after a sale, send a short note from the associate who helped the client, asking how the piece is working out. Ninety days after, send a complimentary cleaning-and-inspection reminder. At the one-year mark, send an anniversary note about the piece itself, not a generic greeting: "It has been a year since we helped you pick out the sapphire ring." Each of these three touches has a distinct, legitimate reason to exist, which is what separates it from a promotional blast.

## The date-based sequence: three dates, two weeks' notice

Beyond the post-sale window, the highest-return recurring cadence in jewelry retail runs on three captured dates: the client's birthday, their partner's birthday, and their anniversary, per WJewel's guidance. The trigger fires roughly two weeks before each date, and it targets the gift-giver, usually the partner, with a gentle reminder and, where the file supports it, a specific wishlist hint. WJewel is specific about both the channel and the tone that works here: SMS outperforms email for these messages by a wide margin when the sender is a recognized person, and the copy should stay short and helpful, never pushy. A working example: "Your anniversary is April 26. If you want ideas, stop by or text us back, we have notes from your last visit."

## The service-based sequence: cleanings and inspections

Every piece sold should carry a standing invitation to a free cleaning and prong inspection, with reminders at six and twelve months. WJewel frames this plainly as a retention program dressed up as service, not a marketing program at all, and the mechanics matter: the client comes in for a cleaning, an associate spots a loose prong and sells a repair, the client sees new inventory near the cleaning station, and the visit refreshes the client record either way. Stores that run this cleaning-and-inspection cadence consistently see repeat-purchase rates 10 to 20 points higher than stores that don't, per WJewel's reported data. Any add-on or service pitched during one of these visits should still follow the ethical framework from Course C2 Module 8, genuine service, clearly disclosed, never assumed.

## The win-back trigger: a reason, not a discount

Every client list has names with no purchase in a year or more, and Clientbook's guidance on this exact gap is specific about the two rules that keep a win-back message from feeling awkward or desperate: don't lead with a discount, and give the message a real reason to exist. A new collection arriving from a designer the client bought before, an invitation to bring existing pieces in for a complimentary cleaning, or a private preview ahead of a trunk show all work, because "warmth plus a concrete reason beats a coupon." Leading with a percentage-off code teaches a client to wait for markdowns, which works directly against the store's own margin, the same discounting discipline covered in Course C2 Module 5.

## The wishlist trigger: follow through, don't just capture

A wishlist entry is a client's own stated purchase intent, and in most stores it gets logged once and then forgotten, which wastes the single easiest follow-up trigger available. Clientbook's guidance is to follow through specifically when something changes: a similar piece arrives, the item gets featured ahead of an event, a relevant gift occasion approaches and the partner should hear about it, or a set amount of time (about thirty days) passes with no decision. The habit that makes this possible starts at the counter, not in the CRM: ask every client, "Would you like me to note what caught your eye today?" and log it, per the same wishlist-capture practice covered in Course C3 Module 1.

## How much is too much

The single most common way a well-intentioned cadence backfires is simple over-contact. WJewel's own guidance sets a specific ceiling: two to four intentional touches per year is plenty for most jewelry clients, covering birthday, anniversary, a cleaning reminder, and a holiday note, and going meaningfully beyond that causes unsubscribe rates to spike. Quality of message matters more than volume. Channel choice matters too: SMS works for time-sensitive, personal messages (a repair ready for pickup, an anniversary reminder to someone the store actually knows), while email carries longer content like a newsletter or an event invitation. Neither channel should ever be blasted to an entire list at once; Clientbook's guidance is direct that a proper clienteling platform excludes opted-out clients automatically and lets a store target a specific segment rather than the full list, and tighter segments produce both better response rates and fewer opt-outs.

## The master calendar

| Trigger | Timing | Channel | Example message |
|---|---|---|---|
| Post-sale check-in | 30 days after purchase | SMS or personal call, from the associate who helped | "Just checking in, how's the ring working out for you?" |
| Cleaning/inspection reminder | 90 days, then 6 and 12 months | SMS | "It's been a few months, want to swing by for a free cleaning and quick check on the setting?" |
| Anniversary-of-purchase note | 365 days after purchase | SMS or short email | "It's been a year since we helped you pick out the sapphire ring. Hope it's still getting worn often." |
| Birthday / partner's birthday | 2 weeks before the date | SMS to the gift-giver | "Your anniversary is April 26. If you want ideas, stop by or text us back, we have notes from your last visit." |
| Win-back (12+ months inactive) | Ongoing, quarterly review of inactive list | Email or SMS, segment-targeted | "New arrivals just came in from [designer you bought before]. Would love for you to see them first." |
| Wishlist follow-through | When a matching item arrives, or ~30 days pass | SMS | "Something just came in that reminded me of the piece you liked last visit, want a photo?" |

## Objections, mistakes and edge cases

| Situation | The trap | Better move |
|---|---|---|
| A client hasn't heard from the store in over a year | Continuing to say nothing, assuming they'll return on their own | Run a win-back trigger with a genuine reason (new arrivals, a cleaning invite), never leading with a discount |
| You want to reach several clients at once about an event | Sending one mass blast to the entire list | Segment the message to a relevant group, and exclude anyone opted out, per platform best practice |
| A client's anniversary or birthday is coming up | Sending a generic, unattributed greeting | Reference specific history from their file and target the actual gift-giver, not a blanket message |
| You've already sent a birthday, anniversary, cleaning, and holiday message this year | Adding a fifth or sixth touch "just in case" | Hold the line at 2-4 intentional touches per year unless there's a genuine, specific reason |
| A wishlist item was logged months ago and nothing has happened since | Treating capture as the finish line | Follow through when something changes: new arrival, event, approaching occasion, or a set time elapsing |
| A client comes in for a free cleaning | Treating it as a pure cost with no sales value | Use the moment to spot repair needs and show relevant new inventory, per the service-as-retention framing |
| You're deciding between SMS and email for a message | Defaulting to whichever is easier that day | Match the channel to the message: SMS for short, time-sensitive, personal notes; email for longer content |

## On the floor: applying it this week

- **Monday:** Pull up one client from your book who hasn't purchased in 12+ months and draft a win-back message with a genuine reason, no discount attached.
- **Tuesday:** Check whether any clients you helped in the last 30 days have received a post-sale check-in. Send any that are missing.
- **Wednesday:** Review your file for upcoming birthdays or anniversaries in the next two weeks and send the reminder to the gift-giver, referencing specific history.
- **Thursday:** Ask at least three clients today, "Would you like me to note what caught your eye today?" and log the answer.
- **Friday:** Count how many total touches your top ten clients have received this year. If any exceed four, plan to space future contact out further.
- **All week:** Keep a running tally of which trigger (post-sale, date-based, service, win-back, wishlist) produced a response. That tells you which parts of the calendar are actually working for your book.
