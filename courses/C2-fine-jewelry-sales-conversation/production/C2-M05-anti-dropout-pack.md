## 1.2 Three-Question Diagnostic Pre-Check

*Instructions to learner: Answer honestly based on what you actually do on the floor, not what you know you're "supposed" to do. This is not graded for a score report — it sorts you into a starting track.*

**Question 1 — The Reflex Test**
A customer picks up a bracelet, looks at the tag, and says, "Can you do anything on this?" Before you've said a single word back, what is your gut instinct?

- A) Start calculating in your head how much you *could* knock off.
- B) Smile and say something like "Let me see what I can do" while you go find a manager.
- C) Ask a question that finds out what "better" would actually mean to them before responding to the price at all.

**Question 2 — The Online Price Test**
A customer shows you a nearly identical piece on their phone for 30% less from an online retailer. What do you say first?

- A) "Yeah, online places can do that because they don't have our overhead" (an apology disguised as an explanation).
- B) "Let me go check with my manager if we can match that."
- C) Ask what specifically they're comparing — carat, cut, certification, setting metal, warranty — before responding to the price gap at all.

**Question 3 — The Walk-Away Test**
A couple says, "We love it, but we want to think it over the weekend." What happens in your head in the next three seconds?

- A) Panic that the sale is dying, followed by an offer to "hold the price" or throw in something free if they decide today.
- B) "No problem, take your time!" — and you let them walk with no next step.
- C) You calmly create a specific, time-bound reason to reconnect that has nothing to do with lowering the price.

**Scoring:**
- **Mostly A answers — "Discount Reflex" track.** You default to solving price objections with price. This module will retrain your first eight seconds of response.
- **Mostly B answers — "Stall & Hope" track.** You avoid conflict by deferring or being agreeable, but you don't actually resolve the objection — you postpone it, usually into a loss. This module will give you language to engage instead of dodge.
- **Mostly C answers — "Emerging Master" track.** You already sense that price objections are really certainty objections. This module will sharpen your timing and give you the exact scripted architecture so this becomes automatic under pressure, not just occasional instinct.

# Section 2: The A3 Objection Architecture (Acknowledge → Align → Advance)

## 2.1 The Framework, Defined

**Acknowledge** — You validate the customer's right to ask the question without agreeing that the price is wrong. This removes their need to repeat or escalate the objection. It costs you nothing and disarms defensiveness.

**Align** — You reposition yourself next to the customer, not across from them, by reconnecting the conversation to what they actually came in wanting: certainty, meaning, and a specific outcome (an engagement, an anniversary, a gift that lands). You do not argue the number. You realign the frame around value delivered per dollar, not dollars alone.

**Advance** — You move the conversation forward toward a decision, using a specific next action — never a discount, never an apology, never a vague stall. The advance always ends in a question that requires the customer to respond with information, not just "yes/no."

The sequence is **fixed order**: skipping Acknowledge and jumping to Align sounds dismissive. Skipping Align and jumping to Advance sounds pushy. All three, in order, feel like being taken care of by someone in control.

***

## 2.2 Scenario 1: "Can you do any better on the price?"

### Option A — Amateur Mistake (Discount Reflex)

**Verbatim script:**
> Customer: "Can you do any better on the price?"
> Associate: "Um, let me see... I think I can probably knock off maybe five percent? Let me go ask my manager."
> *(Associate walks away, returns.)*
> Associate: "Okay, I got you 8% off — that's the best I can do."

**Psychological analysis:** The associate answered the literal question instead of the real one. By instantly offering a number, three things happen simultaneously: (1) the tag price is now proven to be fictional in the customer's mind, so every future price they see from this associate — or this store — is discounted by default in their head; (2) the "let me ask my manager" trip signals the number was always negotiable and manufactures a sense that further pushing will yield more, inviting a second round of negotiation; (3) the customer's certainty need is never addressed, so even after getting 8% off, they often still hesitate, because the real anxiety ("am I being overcharged regardless of the discount") was never resolved. This is the single most margin-destructive behavior on a jewelry floor, and it is learned entirely from panic, not training.

### Option B — Polite Stall

**Verbatim script:**
> Customer: "Can you do any better on the price?"
> Associate: "That's a great question — pricing is really set by corporate, so there's not a lot of wiggle room, unfortunately. But it really is a beautiful piece!"
> *(Associate smiles and waits.)*

**Psychological analysis:** This avoids the discount trap, which is progress, but it fails on Align and Advance. Blaming "corporate" outsources authority and makes the associate seem powerless rather than confident, which subtly lowers the customer's trust in the associate as an expert. The compliment ("it really is a beautiful piece") is generic and disconnected from the customer's actual motivation, so it lands as filler, not reassurance. Critically, there's no Advance — the conversation just hangs, and in that silence, most customers either repeat the objection more firmly or start moving toward the door, because nothing has actually answered their underlying "am I getting fair value" question.

### Option C — Master Execution (A3 Architecture)

**Verbatim script:**
> Customer: "Can you do any better on the price?"
> Associate (Acknowledge): "I completely understand — this is a real investment, and you should feel confident about every dollar of it."
> Associate (Align): "Can I ask — when you picture giving this to her, what's the moment you're imagining? Is this for something specific, or more of a 'just because'?"
> *(Customer answers — e.g., "It's for our tenth anniversary.")*
> Associate: "Ten years — that's huge. So this isn't really about finding a lower number, it's about finding the piece that actually matches what ten years together deserves, right?"
> Associate (Advance): "This one is graded and certified at [specific grade], which means every dollar on that tag is backed by a lab, not a guess. What I'd rather do than shave the price is make sure this is genuinely the right piece for that moment — do you want me to pull the certification so you can see exactly what you're getting?"

**Psychological analysis:** Acknowledge validates the question without conceding the price is negotiable, which prevents the customer from feeling dismissed or escalating. Align does the real work: by asking about the occasion, the associate shifts the entire frame from "is this number fair" to "does this piece match the meaning of the moment" — and once a customer articulates an emotional stake out loud (a ten-year anniversary), backing away from that piece over a percentage feels smaller than the occasion itself, a well-documented anchoring effect. Advance offers a concrete, no-cost action (pulling certification) that increases certainty exactly where the real anxiety lives — "am I being overcharged" — without touching price. The question at the end forces continued engagement rather than a yes/no exit.

## 2.3 Scenario 2: "I found this identical 2-carat setting online for 30% less."

### Option A — Amateur Mistake

**Verbatim script:**
> Customer: "I found this exact setting online for 30% less."
> Associate: "Oh wow, really? Let me see the listing... okay, um, I could probably talk to my manager about matching that, or getting close to it."

**Psychological analysis:** This immediately concedes the premise that the two items are "identical," which is almost never actually true — cut quality, certification lab, metal purity, prong construction, and after-sale service differ constantly between a boutique piece and an anonymous online listing. By skipping straight to price-matching, the associate forfeits the entire value conversation and trains the customer that any printed screenshot is a legitimate negotiating weapon, which invites this tactic on every future visit and referral.

### Option B — Polite Stall

**Verbatim script:**
> Customer: "I found this exact setting online for 30% less."
> Associate: "That's interesting — online retailers definitely have lower overhead than we do. I'm not sure I can match that, but let me mention it to my manager and see."

**Psychological analysis:** This is honest but self-defeating — "lower overhead" is presented as though it's the store's problem to apologize for, rather than reframing what the overhead actually buys the customer (in-person inspection, adjustments, insurance documentation, immediate possession, a relationship with a real person for resizing or repair). Sending it "to the manager" again signals the number is soft, and the customer leaves the interaction with an open loop rather than a resolved decision, often walking out to "think about the online option," where the sale dies quietly.

### Option C — Master Execution

**Verbatim script:**
> Customer: "I found this exact setting online for 30% less."
> Associate (Acknowledge): "I'm really glad you're comparing — that tells me you're serious about getting this right, and you should absolutely compare."
> Associate (Align): "Can I see the listing for a second? ... Okay, so a few things I want to point out, not to talk you out of it, just so you're comparing apples to apples: does that listing show a lab certification number you can independently verify, or is it the seller's own grading? What's the return and resizing policy if it doesn't fit her finger the first time?"
> *(Customer typically admits uncertainty on one or more points.)*
> Associate: "That's exactly why the price is different — this stone comes with an independent certificate from [lab], I can resize it in-house at no cost, and if anything ever needs repair, you walk it in here and it's handled same day. You're not paying 30% more for the same ring — you're paying for the ring plus a lifetime of service behind it."
> Associate (Advance): "Would it help if I pulled the certificate right now so you can see exactly what's backing this stone?"

**Psychological analysis:** Acknowledge validates comparison-shopping as smart rather than insulting, which prevents the customer from feeling accused of trying to cheat the associate. Align uses specific, answerable questions (certification verifiability, return policy, resizing) that almost always reveal genuine gaps in the online offer — this isn't manipulation, it's surfacing real, material differences the customer hadn't considered, which restores the "identical" claim to "similar-looking, not identical." Advance converts the abstract case into something tangible (the physical certificate) — tangible proof reduces anxiety far more effectively than verbal reassurance, and it keeps the customer physically engaged with the piece in-store rather than mentally halfway back to their phone.

## 2.4 Scenario 3: "We love it, but we need to go home and think about it over the weekend."

### Option A — Amateur Mistake

**Verbatim script:**
> Customer: "We love it, but we should probably think it over the weekend."
> Associate: "I totally get it! But you know, if you decide today, I could probably get you a better deal on it — I don't want you to miss out."

**Psychological analysis:** Offering an unsolicited discount to rush a decision does two damaging things: it signals the price was inflated in the first place (why else would today matter?), and it introduces artificial urgency that experienced buyers recognize instantly as a sales tactic, which erodes trust right at the moment trust is most needed. It also frequently backfires — customers who feel pressured into an on-the-spot decision return within days to cancel, because the decision was made from pressure, not conviction.

### Option B — Polite Stall

**Verbatim script:**
> Customer: "We love it, but we should think it over the weekend."
> Associate: "Of course, no rush at all! Here's my card, just come back whenever you're ready."

**Psychological analysis:** This respects the customer's autonomy, which is good, but it creates zero structure for follow-through. "Come back whenever" has no trigger, no reason, and no accountability — most customers who leave with this exact interaction never return, not because they changed their mind about the piece, but because life fills the gap with other priorities and the moment of desire fades without anything to reactivate it.

### Option C — Master Execution

**Verbatim script:**
> Customer: "We love it, but we should think it over the weekend."
> Associate (Acknowledge): "That makes total sense — this is a big decision, and you should feel a hundred percent good about it, not rushed into it."
> Associate (Align): "Just so I make sure I've given you everything you need to think it through properly — is there anything about the piece itself you're still unsure of, or is it more just wanting to sit with the decision as a couple?"
> *(Customer clarifies — usually confirms it's just wanting time, not a specific doubt.)*
> Associate: "That's completely fair — this isn't a decision to rush. Here's what I'd suggest: I'll hold this exact piece under your name until [specific day/time], so you're not thinking it over and then finding out it sold to someone else in the meantime. That way your weekend is just about the decision, not about logistics."
> Associate (Advance): "Can I get your name and a good number for the hold, and would Monday morning or Monday afternoon work better for me to check in with you?"

**Psychological analysis:** Acknowledge removes any pressure feeling, which paradoxically makes customers more comfortable staying engaged rather than defensively retreating. Align asks a diagnostic question that separates a genuine unresolved objection (which needs handling now) from simple decision-processing time (which just needs structure) — treating these identically is a common error that either overwhelms an undecided customer with more pitching or under-serves a hesitant one who actually needed one more piece of information. The Advance offers a concrete, no-discount mechanism — a hold — that gives the customer a legitimate reason for urgency (scarcity of the specific piece, not price) and locks in a specific callback appointment, which converts an open-ended "maybe" into a scheduled touchpoint with dramatically higher follow-through than an unstructured "come back anytime."

# Section 3: The 1-Page Countertop Laminated Field Guide

*(Format as a single laminated 8.5x11 card, front and back, for under-counter reference during live customer interactions.)*

## FRONT OF CARD

### THE A3 SEQUENCE — SAY THIS, IN THIS ORDER, EVERY TIME

1. **ACKNOWLEDGE** (validate, don't concede):
   "I completely understand — this is a real investment, and you should feel confident about it."

2. **ALIGN** (reconnect to their actual reason for being here):
   "Can I ask what moment you're picturing this for?" / "What matters most to you about getting this right?"

3. **ADVANCE** (offer a concrete next step that isn't a discount):
   "What I'd rather do than change the price is [pull certification / show comparison / offer a hold]. Does that help?"

### PHYSICAL BODY LANGUAGE — DO THIS, NOT THAT

- **Stand square, not defensive.** Keep shoulders open toward the customer, hands visible on the counter — never crossed arms.
- **Slow down your pace when price comes up.** Rushed speech signals panic; a half-second pause before responding signals confidence.
- **Maintain the piece in your hands or on the pad, not locked back in the case.** Returning it to the case too early signals the sale is over; keep it present while you talk.
- **Nod while they object, not while you respond.** Nodding during their objection shows you're listening; nodding while you speak can look like you're trying to convince yourself.
- **Lower your voice slightly on the Align question.** A quieter, more personal tone during the "what moment are you picturing" question makes it feel like a real conversation, not a script.

### THE 3 BANNED WORDS/PHRASES — NEVER SAY THESE AT THE COUNTER

1. **"Discount"** — Even offering one. Say "adjust the offer" only if a manager-approved promotion applies; never introduce the concept unprompted.
2. **"Sorry"** — Never apologize for the price. "Sorry, that's just what it costs" signals the price itself is a burden you regret imposing.
3. **"Let me ask my manager"** (as an immediate reflex) — This phrase, used as a first response to price pushback, tells the customer the number is soft and invites further negotiation. Reserve manager escalation only for genuine policy exceptions, after the A3 sequence has been fully run.

## BACK OF CARD

### THE 60-SECOND RECOVERY PROTOCOL
*(Use this immediately after you catch yourself making an Amateur Mistake — mid-conversation or right after.)*

**Second 0–10 — Stop the bleeding.**
Do not keep talking past the mistake. Pause, take a breath, and reset your posture to square and open.

**Second 10–25 — Re-anchor verbally.**
Say: "Actually, let me back up for a second — I want to make sure I'm giving you the full picture, not just a number." This signals a deliberate correction, not confusion, and resets the frame.

**Second 25–45 — Re-run Align.**
Ask the diagnostic question you skipped: "Can I ask what this piece is actually for?" or "What would make this feel like the right decision for you today?" This recovers the certainty-focused conversation you bypassed.

**Second 45–60 — Advance without the discount you implied.**
Close with a concrete, non-price action: "What I'd really like to do is show you the certification/comparison/setting detail so you have everything you need — can I grab that for you?" This moves the conversation forward on value terms, even after a price slip, and prevents the interaction from ending on a negotiated number.

# Section 4: Post-Lesson Spaced Repetition Drip Cadence

## 4.1 Day 2 — Retrieval Quiz (with explanations)

*Delivered via LMS notification 48 hours after lesson completion. Multiple choice, immediate feedback per question.*

**Question 1:** What is the correct order of the A3 Architecture?

- A) Align, Acknowledge, Advance
- B) Acknowledge, Align, Advance ✓
- C) Advance, Acknowledge, Align
- D) Order doesn't matter as long as all three happen

*Explanation:* Order matters because each step depends on the emotional state created by the one before it. Acknowledge lowers defensiveness before Align asks a personal question; Align surfaces the real motivation before Advance proposes a specific next step. Reversing the order — e.g., advancing before acknowledging — feels pushy because the customer hasn't yet felt heard.

**Question 2:** A customer says, "I found this online for less." What should your very first question be?

- A) "How much less?"
- B) "Can I see the listing?" ✓
- C) "Do you want me to match it?"
- D) "Is that from a trustworthy site?"

*Explanation:* Seeing the actual listing lets you identify real, material differences (certification, return policy, metal purity) rather than arguing about an unseen comparison. Asking "how much less" centers the conversation on the gap in price rather than the gap in value, and jumping to "do you want me to match it" concedes the negotiation before you've even assessed whether the items are truly comparable.

**Question 3:** Which of these is one of the three banned phrases at the counter?

- A) "Let me ask my manager" (as a first reflex) ✓
- B) "This piece is certified"
- C) "What moment are you picturing this for?"
- D) "I'd rather show you the certification"

*Explanation:* Using "let me ask my manager" as an automatic first response to price pushback — before running the A3 sequence — signals the price is negotiable and invites further pushing. It's appropriate only as a genuine last resort for real policy exceptions, never as a reflex.

**Question 4:** True or False: Acknowledging a customer's price objection means agreeing the price should be lower.

- True
- **False** ✓

*Explanation:* Acknowledge validates the customer's *right* to ask, not the correctness of a lower number. "I understand this is a big investment" costs nothing and doesn't concede anything — it simply removes the customer's need to repeat or escalate the objection.

**Question 5:** In the "think it over the weekend" scenario, what does the Master Execution advance offer instead of a discount?

- A) A free gift with purchase
- B) A time-bound hold on the specific piece ✓
- C) A follow-up email newsletter
- D) A price freeze if they return within 90 days

*Explanation:* A hold creates legitimate urgency around scarcity of the specific piece (someone else could buy it) rather than manufactured urgency around price, and it locks in a specific callback time, which produces dramatically higher follow-through than an open-ended "come back whenever."

## 4.2 Day 7 — Floor Action Challenge

*Delivered as a task card in the LMS with a manager sign-off field.*

**Challenge:** In your next three genuine price-objection conversations on the floor, run the full A3 sequence out loud, in order, without skipping to a discount. Immediately after each interaction (within 5 minutes, before the details fade), fill out this micro-log:

1. What did the customer actually say?
2. What did you say for Acknowledge? (write the exact words you used)
3. What did you ask for Align, and what did their answer reveal about their real motivation?
4. What specific, non-discount action did you offer for Advance?
5. Did the interaction end in a sale, a scheduled follow-up, or a walk? If a walk, what would you say differently next time?

**Manager sign-off:** Manager reviews the three logs within 48 hours and initials whether the sequence was followed in the correct order at least twice out of three. If fewer than two of three logs show correct sequencing, the associate re-does the Day 7 challenge with a live role-play alongside the manager before moving on.

## 4.3 Day 30 — Mastery Simulation Rubric

*Delivered as a live or recorded role-play assessment, evaluated by a manager or trainer using the scoring grid below. Associate is presented with one randomly selected scenario (equivalent difficulty to Scenarios 1–3) they have not rehearsed in advance.*

**Scoring grid (5 points per category, 25 points total):**

**Acknowledge execution (0–5):**
- 5: Validates the customer's concern specifically and warmly, with no hint of apology or concession, in under 5 seconds.
- 3: Acknowledges but sounds scripted, rushed, or slightly apologetic.
- 0: Skips Acknowledge entirely or jumps straight to a number.

**Align execution (0–5):**
- 5: Asks a genuine, specific diagnostic question that uncovers the customer's real motivation or the real gap in a comparison, and adapts the next line based on the answer.
- 3: Asks a generic question ("Is this for something special?") without adapting to the answer given.
- 0: Skips Align or asks a closed yes/no question that doesn't surface any new information.

**Advance execution (0–5):**
- 5: Offers a specific, concrete, non-discount next step tied directly to what was uncovered in Align, and ends with a question that requires more than yes/no.
- 3: Offers a vague next step ("let me see what I can do") or ends with only a closed question.
- 0: Advances with a discount, a stall with no structure, or no advance at all.

**Composure and non-verbal delivery (0–5):**
- 5: Open posture, steady pace, no defensive body language, piece remains visibly present throughout the interaction.
- 3: Generally composed with one lapse (e.g., crossed arms, rushed speech, returning the piece to the case too early).
- 0: Visibly defensive, rushed, or apologetic tone throughout.

**Banned language compliance (0–5):**
- 5: Zero use of "discount," "sorry," or reflexive "let me ask my manager" throughout the entire simulation.
- 3: One slip, self-corrected using the 60-Second Recovery Protocol.
- 0: Multiple slips, or a slip not recovered.

**Overall mastery bands:**
- **22–25: Certified Master** — Ready to train others; no further coaching cycle required.
- **15–21: Proficient, needs reinforcement** — Repeat the Day 7 Floor Action Challenge for another week before re-testing.
- **Below 15: Retrain** — Return to Section 2 scenario scripts for guided practice with a manager before re-attempting the Day 30 simulation.
