## The two words that cost the store margin

A client has just heard the price on a 1.5-carat solitaire: $15,000. She pauses, then says, "That's more than I expected." The associate, uncomfortable with the silence, jumps in: "I can probably do $13,500 for you today." The client's face brightens, not because she trusts the value more, but because she has just learned the first number was never firm.

Sixty percent of responses to INSTORE's industry-wide "Brain Squad" survey named price as the hardest objection jewelry salespeople face. But according to the same trade reporting, working sales trainers agree on something counterintuitive: price is rarely the actual reason a customer walks. "Too expensive" usually means something else entirely — "I wasn't planning to spend this much," "you haven't shown me enough value yet," "I want to feel like I negotiated," or simply "I'm testing whether this number is real." Discounting on reflex answers none of those, and it teaches the client that every price in your store is a suggestion.

## Objections are rarely the real objection

Sales author Jeffrey Gitomer's rule, cited across jewelry trade press, applies directly here: "There are very few real objections in selling. Most are stalls." A customer who has genuinely decided the piece isn't for them simply leaves. A customer who pushes back on price is, more often than not, still in the conversation and still imagining the piece as theirs. Katrina Sustachek of Rasmussen Diamonds frames it this way: customers raising a price objection are frequently "asking you to tell them why they should spend the money." Treat the objection as a request for more conviction, not a rejection.

Southern Jewelry News takes this further with a specific figure worth remembering: up to 60 percent of stated objections are false reasons for not buying, and as much as 70 percent of those false objections trace back to price, with the customer too embarrassed to admit the number is the real issue. That embarrassment shows up especially often in cross-gender buying situations, where one partner doesn't want to admit in front of a salesperson that they can't comfortably afford what's being shown.

## Diagnose before you answer

The single most important move in this module happens before you say a word in response. Jewelry sales trainer Shane Decker uses two diagnostic questions when a customer says a price is too high, both delivered with a smile, not a challenge:

1. "Compared to what?" This forces the customer to name a reference point — a competitor, an online listing, last year's price, or a number they simply invented — which becomes the actual thing you can address.
2. "Is the price too high, or is it just more than you want to spend?" This single question separates a value problem from a budget problem, and the two require completely different responses.

Southern Jewelry News frames the same distinction from the other direction: a price objection is either a **value objection** (the customer doesn't yet see enough value to justify the number) or a **budget objection** (the customer sees the value clearly but genuinely cannot stretch that far right now). Angela Cisneros of Angela Cisneros Jewelry Concierge warns against skipping this step: "When you try to blanket all price objections with a standard value response, you're going to lose. Dig deeper and listen." Misdiagnose, and you'll either discount a customer who only needed more conviction, or lecture a customer who simply needs a different number to work toward.

| Signal | What it usually means | Your first move |
|---|---|---|
| Sharp intake of breath, raised eyebrows, "Whoa" | Sticker shock — value hasn't landed yet | Educate on craftsmanship and materials before touching the number |
| Names a specific competitor or has a printout | Comparison shopping, but still engaged | Be glad, not defensive; address service and trust, never bash the competitor |
| Names a specific number they're comfortable with | Budget objection | Move to option negotiation on the piece, not a discount on the price |
| Vague, disengaged "that's a lot" with little follow-up | Polite exit, low real interest in this piece | Pivot to a different piece rather than defending this one |

## Price versus cost

Once you know which kind of objection you're facing, the late Zig Ziglar's classic distinction reframes the entire conversation: price is what you pay once; cost is what you live with afterward. A cheaper alternative has its own cost — the resetting a poorly made piece eventually needs, the smaller stone a customer often ends up wanting to upgrade in five years, or the warranty and repair-counter support they won't have anywhere else. Answering a price objection with a cost conversation, instead of a smaller number, keeps the value argument in the room.

Jewel360, a jewelry-industry point-of-sale provider, recommends making that cost concrete rather than abstract: walk the client through what's actually behind the number — the hours a master jeweler spent hand-setting each prong, the precision of the cutting, the karat purity of the metal — so the price starts to make sense as labor and material rather than an arbitrary figure.

## Reframe the number, don't shrink it

Two reframing techniques let you keep the price exactly where it is while changing how large it feels.

**Per-day or per-year math.** Kelly Hughes of Jamie's The Diamond Mine does this calculation for anniversary and milestone pieces: a $9,000 ring worn for 50 years works out to roughly fifty cents a day. Jewel360 recommends the same technique framed annually — a $6,000 ring becomes $120 a year across fifty years of wear, compared with a $200 fashion piece that needs replacing every year or two. Said with a smile and a small pause, either version turns an overwhelming number into a unit that isn't.

**Forecast the regret.** Patty Gallun Hansen of Dorothy Gallun Fine Jewelry offers a line worth memorizing: "It's not the items I've bought that I've regretted — it's the items I didn't." W.J. Smith III's version asks the client to do the math themselves: "Ten years from now, will the extra money mean anything to you? If it will, don't buy it. But if it won't, go for it." Both work by shifting the client's attention from today's number to a future without the piece, which is usually the frame that actually worries them.

Client: "Fifteen thousand feels like a lot for a ring."

Associate: "I understand — it's a real number. Here's one way to think about it: worn every day for the next 40 years, that's just over a dollar a day for something you'll never stop noticing on your hand. Most of the regret I hear about isn't buying the ring — it's the ones people didn't."

## Option negotiation instead of discounting

When a customer has named a genuine budget ceiling, Decker's move is to change the item to match the price, never the price to match the item: "I can do $10,000. The 1.5-carat is $15,000, but we can stay at the same quality at one carat for $10,000." The customer either accepts the smaller stone at the quality they were shown, or talks themselves into the original piece at full price. Either way, the tag on the original piece never moves. JCK's own reporting on this technique frames it the same way: if a customer is fixed on a number, adjust the color, clarity, or carat weight of the stone rather than the store's price.

Jewel360 extends the same principle to materials and settings, not just carat weight: a similarly durable colored stone in place of a diamond, a different metal, or a modestly smaller stone can preserve the spirit of the piece the client fell in love with, without discounting the piece itself. The caveat matters as much as the technique: never steer a client toward a lower-quality option just to close the sale today. A disappointed client after the fact is a client you lose permanently.

## Holding the line when they ask directly

Sometimes a customer skips the indirect version and asks outright, "Can you do better on this?" Eve Alfillé of Eve J. Alfillé Gallery & Studio has a five-word answer worth practicing until it feels natural: "No, this is the price." Delivered with a smile, it is polite, firm, and final, and it communicates something more important than the words themselves: the number was real the first time, and repeating it calmly just confirms that.

The late Kate Peterson's related warning is worth internalizing before you ever reach for "store policy" as your reason for holding firm: the moment you blame a policy, you've demoted yourself from a professional to a clerk. Kate Peterson's fix: "I can't do that. But here's what I can do" — then offer something real, whether that's a service, a payment plan, or the option-negotiation move above, rather than a flat refusal with no alternative attached.

## If you must concede, make it cost something

Some price objections are genuinely budget-limited, and a modest concession is sometimes the right business call. Jessica Gylsen of XL Diamonds calls this "go low and slow": small concessions, held firm, each attached to a stated reason rather than handed over on request. Sales trainer Megan Crabtree goes further and makes every concession bilateral. One version she uses directly: "If I can get you to the price you're requesting, can you send me a referral?" Roughly eight in ten customers say yes. A discount disappears the moment the client walks out the door; a referral compounds for years.

Trade guidance from JCK's own show-floor reporting adds a useful discipline if a discount is genuinely warranted: keep the first concession small, no more than about 10 percent, and if a second concession becomes necessary, make it roughly half the size of the first. A sale should rarely be discounted more than 15 percent in total. Treating a discount as a rare, bounded, reasoned exception — not a reflexive response to hesitation — protects both your margin and the client's trust in your prices going forward.

## Never compete on the competitor's terms

If a client arrives citing a lower price elsewhere, resist the instinct to run down the competitor. J. Dennis Petimezas of Watchmaker's Diamonds & Jewelry trains every new hire on this rule directly: bashing a competitor reads as fear of losing the sale, not confidence. Talk only about what your store actually offers — warranty coverage, a repair counter, sourcing standards, a relationship that lasts beyond the transaction — never about what the competitor lacks. Peter Hannes IV of Craig Husar Fine Diamonds offers a genuinely useful reframe for this exact moment: be glad, not defensive, when a client shows you a competitor's price, because "if something were actually wrong with the ring, they'd talk about the ring." Citing a price means they still want it.

## Ask, then stop talking

After you deliver a reframed price, a value answer, or a firm hold like Alfillé's, resist the urge to keep talking. David Geller's framing, cited widely in jewelry trade press, calls this "the power of shut up": after you state a price, whoever speaks next loses ground. Most associates cannot tolerate the silence and fill it with an unprompted concession, undoing the exact value case they just built. This is the same discipline covered in Module 4 applied specifically to price: state the number and the value behind it, then hold still.

## Objections, mistakes and edge cases

| Situation | The trap | Better move |
|---|---|---|
| Client says "that's too expensive" | Immediately offering a discount to fill the silence | Ask "Compared to what?" or "Is it the price, or is it just more than you want to spend right now?" |
| Client cites a competitor's lower price | Bad-mouthing the competitor or panicking | Say you're glad they researched it, then talk only about what your store offers |
| Client names a firm budget number | Discounting the piece they're looking at to fit it | Use option negotiation: adjust carat, metal, or stone to meet the number without touching the tag |
| Client asks directly, "Can you do better?" | Caving immediately or getting defensive | Hold the line calmly: "No, this is the price," then offer a real alternative if one exists |
| You genuinely need to concede something | Giving the full ask immediately | Go low and slow, attach a reason to any concession, and consider trading it for a referral |
| Client seems embarrassed rather than argumentative | Pushing harder on value | Recognize this may be a budget objection in disguise; offer payment options or a smaller variant with dignity |
| You just stated a reframed price or value case | Filling the silence that follows with more talking | Stop talking; let the client respond first |
| Client uses jargon-triggered confusion as a stall | Repeating the same explanation, louder | Re-explain in plain language; check whether the real issue is confusion, not price |

## On the floor: applying it this week

- **Monday:** The next time you hear "that's too expensive," practice asking "Compared to what?" before saying anything else, and notice what the client actually names.
- **Tuesday:** Practice the per-day or per-year math on three pieces at different price points until you can do it out loud without hesitating.
- **Wednesday:** If a client cites a competitor's price, practice Hannes's reframe in your head before responding: they still want it, or they wouldn't mention the number.
- **Thursday:** Try Alfillé's line, "No, this is the price," delivered with a genuine smile, the next time someone asks directly for a better deal.
- **Friday:** After every price objection today, ask yourself: was that a value problem or a budget problem? Write down which one it was and what you did about it.
- **All week:** Track how many times you catch yourself about to discount before diagnosing the objection, and how many times you stopped yourself.
