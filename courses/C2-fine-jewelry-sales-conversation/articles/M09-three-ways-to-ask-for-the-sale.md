## The step almost everyone skips

Sales trainer Brad Huisken's estimate, published in Southern Jewelry News, is worth sitting with: as high as 60 percent of the time, no attempt is made to close the sale at all. Not a failed attempt. No attempt. A client is shown beautiful pieces, treated warmly, walked through every feature, and then simply... allowed to leave, because nobody actually asked them to buy. Huisken's standard is blunt: "Anything short of not asking, is simply not doing your job."

This module is not about manipulation or pressure. It's about the specific, simple sentence that has to happen once a buying signal appears (Module 4), objections have been handled (Modules 5 through 7), and the presentation has done its job. You already know when that moment has arrived. This module gives you three specific ways to use it.

## Recognize the moment first

Kate Peterson, writing for The Plumb Club, frames closing as something you're always watching for, not a single scripted event at the end: "There is never a bad time to close the sale once trust and value have been established." Both verbal signals ("I like this one better," "It'll look great this weekend") and nonverbal ones (a lingering glance, hesitating to hand the piece back, a nod after a trial close) tell you the decision point has arrived. Once you see it, the only mistake left to make is staying silent.

## Way one: the assumptive close

Kate Peterson describes this as especially effective with indecisive customers, and it starts well before the actual ask. Throughout the presentation, you speak as though the sale is already happening, describing the piece as "yours," "his," or "hers" instead of "ours," and helping the client identify the best option rather than debating whether to buy at all. When the moment feels right, you ask a question that assumes the decision has been made and moves straight to a detail:

- "He's really going to love this! Shall I gift-wrap it for you?"
- "From all you've told me, this is the perfect choice. When are you going to give it to him?"
- "I can have the ring sized by tomorrow morning. What time would you like to pick it up?"

Notice that none of these ask "Do you want to buy this?" They ask about gift-wrapping, timing, or pickup, questions that only make sense if the purchase is already decided. Answering any of them is, functionally, saying yes.

## Way two: the alternate choice close

Southern Jewelry News's version of this technique, credited to trainer Brad Huisken, calls it the Win/Win Close: give the customer two choices, and structure them so that either answer results in a completed sale. Kate Peterson's framing adds that this works especially well once a client has narrowed things down to two items, or once you're confident the moment to close has arrived:

- "Would you like to wear the watch home, or shall I wrap it for you?"
- "Would you like to pay with a check or a credit card?"
- "Do you prefer the ring in yellow or white gold?"
- "Will it be the necklace or the earrings, or both?"

The client's attention goes to the choice itself, not to the underlying decision of whether to buy, which has quietly already been settled by the structure of the question.

## Way three: the reflexive close

Huisken's Reflexive Close takes a question the client asks and reflects it straight back as a completed decision, once trust and value are already established. If a client asks "Can I get this ring sized by the end of the week?" the reflexive answer is not just "Yes" — it's "Absolutely, we can have it sized by Friday. What time would you like to pick it up?" Kate Peterson describes the same mechanic from the other direction: if a client asks "How large a deposit would I have to put down?" you can respond with "If I can arrange a $100 deposit, will you buy it?" rather than only answering the surface question. The client asked a logistics question; you answer it while simultaneously asking for the sale.

A close, cousin technique worth knowing by name: the Ask For It Close, which Huisken describes as simply asking the customer to buy, directly. "Let me write the earrings up for you. How did you want to pay for them?" No indirection at all, just a calm, confident, direct request.

| Technique | Best used when | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Assumptive close | Client is engaged but indecisive; you've been building toward "yours" language throughout | "I can have the ring sized by tomorrow. What time would you like to pick it up?" |
| Alternate choice close | Client has narrowed the field to two options, or a decision point (payment, gift-wrap) has arrived | "Would you like to pay with a check or a credit card?" |
| Reflexive close | Client asks a logistics question (sizing, deposit, delivery) that itself signals readiness | "Can I get this sized by Friday?" → "Absolutely, what time would you like to pick it up?" |

## Confidence is part of the technique

Kate Peterson's warning applies to every one of these three ways equally: "Remember that your confidence at the moment you ask for the sale is critical. The customer will sense and react to any uncertainty on your part." A perfectly worded assumptive close delivered with a hesitant, apologetic tone undercuts itself. Practice each script out loud, before you need it, until it sounds like a natural, calm statement rather than something you're testing for the first time on a real client.

## If you get an objection instead of a yes

Sometimes a close surfaces an objection rather than an agreement, and that is useful information, not a failure. Peterson's guidance is direct: "even if you encounter an objection at this point, don't be discouraged. Treat it like any other objection, and then try closing again." That means routing straight back to the relevant module, price (Module 5), a stall like "I'll think about it" (Module 6), or an online comparison (Module 7), resolving it, and then returning to one of these three closes rather than abandoning the attempt entirely.

## Objections, mistakes and edge cases

| Situation | The trap | Better move |
|---|---|---|
| You've shown the client everything but haven't asked them to buy | Assuming they'll say so themselves if interested | Ask, using one of the three techniques, every single time merchandise has been shown |
| Client seems indecisive between similar options | Continuing to add more information | Use the alternate choice close: narrow it to two specific options, both leading to a sale |
| Client asks a logistics question (sizing, deposit, timing) | Answering only the literal question | Use the reflexive close: answer it while simultaneously asking for the sale |
| You deliver a close but sound uncertain or apologetic | Assuming the words alone will work | Practice the script out loud beforehand so your tone matches your words |
| A close produces an objection instead of a yes | Treating it as a lost sale and giving up | Resolve the objection using the relevant module, then close again |
| You're not sure which of the three techniques fits | Freezing and saying nothing | Default to the simplest: ask directly, "Let me write this up for you. How would you like to pay?" |
| Client hands the piece back without deciding | Letting the moment pass without a final ask | Use a genuine trial close first ("How does that feel?"), then move into one of the three closes on a positive response |

## On the floor: applying it this week

- **Monday:** Practice the assumptive close out loud on three different pieces, using "yours," "his," or "hers" instead of "ours" throughout.
- **Tuesday:** The next time a client narrows their choice to two items, use the alternate choice close instead of continuing to present more options.
- **Wednesday:** Listen for a logistics question today (sizing, deposit, pickup time) and practice answering it with a reflexive close.
- **Thursday:** Say the words "Let me write this up for you. How would you like to pay?" out loud to a colleague until it feels natural, not scripted.
- **Friday:** Review every client interaction this week where merchandise was shown. For each one, ask honestly: did I actually ask for the sale?
- **All week:** Track how many presentations end without any of the three closes being attempted. That gap, per Huisken's estimate, may be as high as 60 percent industry-wide, and it's entirely within your control to close.
