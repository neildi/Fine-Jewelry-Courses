## The phone on the counter

A client has spent twenty minutes admiring a 1-carat solitaire, then pulls out her phone. "I found the same thing online for $3,800 less." The associate's stomach drops, and the instinct is to either apologize and discount on the spot, or get defensive about the store's prices. Neither response addresses the actual question sitting on the counter: is it really the same thing?

Showrooming, using a physical store to see and try something in person, then buying it cheaper elsewhere, is a real and common pattern in jewelry retail. But Kyle Bullock, manager of Bullock's Jewelry in Roswell, New Mexico, reframes it usefully: a customer holding up a phone usually isn't finished shopping, they are still shopping, and the fact that they're standing in your store with a screen in hand instead of buying elsewhere already tells you something. As Bullock puts it, if you accept that people use the internet to interact with and learn about the products you sell, "that's a good thing... it doesn't have to be our enemy."

## Why they're still standing in your store

If the online price were truly the whole story, this client wouldn't be here. Bullock's own observation is direct: customers who arrive citing an online price usually aren't finding what they actually want on a website, and that's precisely why they walked into a physical store in the first place. "They aren't finding out what they want on websites. That is why they came in to the store." The internet gave them a number. It did not give them the chance to see, touch, or compare the actual piece, which is exactly what you can offer that the website cannot.

## Verify before you react

The first move, before defending your price or offering a discount, is to confirm the comparison is genuinely apples-to-apples. Jewelry prices vary enormously across seemingly similar specifications, and a "same" diamond online is very often not the same diamond at all. Van Hildebrand, owner of Hildebrand Jewelers in Austin, makes a habit of looking closely at the source a client cites, since price lists sometimes come from wholesaler sites the client was never meant to have access to, which are not a fair retail comparison at all.

Ask directly and warmly, not defensively: "That's great that you're doing your research — can I see what you found? I want to make sure we're comparing the exact same thing." Check carat weight, cut grade, color, clarity, certifying lab, and whether the listed price includes setting, sizing, and warranty the way your price does. A lower number on a screen frequently turns out to be a different, lesser stone, a different metal purity, or a price that excludes services your ticket already includes.

| What to check | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Same carat weight and shape | Small weight differences move price significantly |
| Same cut grade, not just color/clarity | Two stones with identical color and clarity grades can look completely different depending on cut quality |
| Same certifying lab (GIA, AGS, etc.) | Grading standards and rigor vary meaningfully between labs |
| Setting, sizing, and warranty included or not | An online price for a loose stone isn't comparable to an in-store ticket that includes the mounting and service |
| Seller reputation and return policy | An unfamiliar or marketplace seller carries risk a known jeweler's price already accounts for |

## Let them choose with their eyes, not a spec sheet

Once you've confirmed what they're actually comparing, Bullock's core technique reframes the entire conversation: instead of leading with specifications, find a few diamonds in your case that are close to the online listing's stated specs but span a range of price points, and let the client choose which one they like best purely by looking, before you say a single number. "We don't even bring up the specs of the diamond. We just let them look and pick the one they like the best." Only after they've chosen do you reveal the specifications and prices, then ask the one question that does the actual selling: "Do you see a difference in the diamond you picked that justifies the price for you?"

This works because it removes you from the position of arguing for your price and puts the client's own eyes in that position instead. Two diamonds with identical color and clarity grades on paper can look meaningfully different in person, and a client who picks the better-looking, higher-priced stone with their own eyes has just made your argument for you.

## Make the quality gap physical, not theoretical

Devon Bond, co-owner of Garrick Jewelers in Hanover, Pennsylvania, keeps a specific prop at the ready: a small collection of rings the store has purchased directly off the internet, kept expressly for sales presentations. "It runs the gamut from razor thin prongs or precious metal porosity in the prongs to missing gemstones and hollow ring shanks." One even has "Made in China" stamped inside the shank. Pulling out a real, physical example of what a lower price actually buys does more in ten seconds than any verbal argument about quality, because the client is holding the evidence rather than being told to trust it.

Bond's pricing observation is also worth internalizing: in his experience, the gap between what a client is willing to pay after seeing an online image and what the in-store piece actually costs is often surprisingly small, frequently only around $500. Framing that gap concretely, as the cost of thicker prongs, more secure settings, and a jeweler who is part of the client's community and available afterward, turns an abstract price difference into a specific, justifiable one.

## Tell the truth about what the internet can't show

Tim Lewis, CEO of Lewis Jewelers in Moore, Oklahoma, addresses the deeper issue behind identical-looking grading reports: "The grading report just tells a diamond's measurements, and it doesn't tell you whether or not the diamond is pretty." He shows clients two diamonds with the same color and clarity grade that look completely different in person, which reliably resolves the comparison without any need to argue. His broader framing is worth repeating honestly, without exaggeration: "Selling is nothing more than conveying that the benefits outweigh the cost of the product. You don't get that online." This is not fear-mongering about the internet; it's pointing at something concretely true, that a grading report captures measurements, not beauty, fit, or service.

## When you genuinely can't compete

Sometimes a client has found a price you cannot responsibly match, and the honest, long-term-relationship move is to say so plainly. Van Hildebrand describes a client who found a 3-carat oval at a price he "simply could not match." Rather than losing the client's trust by pretending otherwise, he told the client to buy it, but to bring the diamond and its grading report back in so the store could verify everything was legitimate before setting it. It was. "This customer appreciated the honest answer and has been a very good customer of ours." Being the trustworthy expert in the room, even when it costs you a single sale, is frequently what wins the relationship and the referrals that follow.

## Never discount reflexively, and never bash the internet

Adam Foster, owner of Adam Foster Fine Art Jewelry in St. Louis, states the risk of the opposite approach bluntly: "When a jeweler does this, it's the beginning of the end of their business," referring to routinely matching or beating internet prices on demand. His store instead leans entirely on unique, non-comparable product and a strong online presence of its own, so the comparison itself becomes less relevant. If your store does have a genuine price-match policy, apply it with discipline rather than case-by-case improvisation: match only identical items (same specs, same certifying lab, in stock now), only from reputable, verifiable sellers, and never below your own margin floor. A policy applied inconsistently teaches every client to negotiate, the same lesson Module 5 warns against with reflexive discounting.

If a client cites a price and keeps pushing after you've verified it's genuinely comparable and shown the value gap, Jennifer P. Riley's floor-tested approach, shared in JCK's retailer roundtable, works without ever touching your actual price: show a slightly lower grade at the number they're asking for, so the product changes rather than the price. If a client simply won't stop, Annette Piper's advice, also from that roundtable, is to hold your price calmly and consistently, and if it truly goes nowhere, say plainly, "I'm sorry, but I don't appear to be able to help you today," and move on. Clients often reconsider once the pressure they were applying stops working.

## Objections, mistakes and edge cases

| Situation | The trap | Better move |
|---|---|---|
| Client shows a lower price on their phone | Apologizing and discounting immediately, or getting defensive | Ask to see it, then verify carat, cut, clarity, certifying lab, and whether setting/service are included |
| The comparison turns out to be genuinely identical | Insisting your price is still justified anyway | Acknowledge it honestly; use the side-by-side technique or decide if a modest, disciplined concession is warranted |
| Client seems to be researching, not yet decided | Treating the phone as a threat to shut down | Recognize they are still shopping, and still in your store; that is an opportunity, not a loss |
| You can't responsibly match the price at all | Pretending you can, then losing trust later | Say so honestly, and offer to verify the online purchase's legitimacy once it arrives, protecting the relationship either way |
| Client keeps pushing after a fair, verified comparison | Continuing to negotiate indefinitely | Hold your price calmly and consistently; if truly at an impasse, it's acceptable to say you can't help them today |
| You want to argue quality verbally | Explaining specs at length with no visual proof | Use a physical prop (a real lower-quality piece) or a side-by-side selection so the client sees the difference themselves |
| Client cites an unfamiliar or marketplace seller | Treating that price as automatically valid | Note the source; wholesaler or unverified marketplace listings aren't a fair retail comparison |

## On the floor: applying it this week

- **Monday:** The next time a client mentions an online price, practice asking to see it and checking carat, cut, clarity, and certifying lab before responding to the number itself.
- **Tuesday:** Set up a side-by-side selection with two or three pieces at different price points and practice letting a colleague choose by eye before you reveal specs.
- **Wednesday:** If your store has samples of lower-quality pieces (or you can source one), start building a small, tangible prop collection for future comparisons.
- **Thursday:** Practice saying, calmly and without apology, "I understand — here's the difference in what you're getting," the next time a price comparison comes up.
- **Friday:** Review this week's online-comparison conversations. For each one, ask: did you verify the comparison before responding, or react to the number first?
- **All week:** Notice how many showrooming clients are still standing in your store, phone in hand, rather than already gone. That is information, not a loss.
