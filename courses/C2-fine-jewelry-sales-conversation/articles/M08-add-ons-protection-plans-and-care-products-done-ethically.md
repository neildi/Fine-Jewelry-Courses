## The add-on that isn't really a service

A client buys a $4,000 engagement ring. Before she leaves, the associate says, "You'll want our protection plan too, it's only $200 more," without explaining what it covers, whether her homeowner's insurance might already cover loss and theft, or that a jewelry-specific policy actually covers different things than a store warranty does. She buys it because she was told to, not because anyone helped her understand what she was protecting and from what.

Nothing about that exchange was illegal. But it also wasn't service, and jewelry-specific add-ons, done right, genuinely are one of the most valuable things you can offer a client. This module is about doing them right.

## Why these add-ons genuinely matter

Independent jewelers have four real add-on categories worth offering every client, according to point-of-sale revenue guidance built specifically for jewelry retailers: jewelry insurance referrals, appraisals, warranty or service plans, and ongoing care. Each raises average ticket and attachment rate, which matters for the store, but each also solves a real client problem when explained honestly. Insurance protects against loss, theft, and damage the way a store warranty typically doesn't. An appraisal gives a client the documentation an insurer will actually require. A service plan covers maintenance and common repairs, which brings a client back to your counter rather than a stranger's. None of that requires exaggeration to be worth offering.

| Add-on | What it actually covers | Honest pitch |
|---|---|---|
| Insurance referral | Loss, theft, and mysterious disappearance, which most store warranties do not cover | "Most people insure a piece like this. Would you like me to send you a link to a quick quote so it's protected right away?" |
| Appraisal | Documentation of value and specifications an insurer will require for coverage | "We can include an appraisal so you have documentation for your records and protection options." |
| Warranty / service plan | Repairs, resizing, prong retipping, and routine maintenance | "If you want ongoing coverage for maintenance and common repairs, we offer a service plan you can add today." |
| Care products | Cleaning, storage, and upkeep between professional services | Recommended based on the specific metal and stones in the piece, not sold as a blanket add-on to everyone |

## Know what each one actually is, so you never misrepresent it

Jewelry insurance and a store warranty are genuinely different products, and conflating them is the single most common way an otherwise well-intentioned pitch becomes misleading. Insurance industry guidance on jewelry coverage distinguishes replacement cost value, where the insurer pays what it costs to replace the item today without factoring in depreciation, from actual cash value, which does account for depreciation, and a store warranty typically covers neither loss nor theft at all, only manufacturing defects or specific added protections the plan states in writing. Before you pitch anything, know exactly what it covers so you can say so accurately rather than letting a client assume broader protection than they're actually getting.

## The appraisal conflict of interest you should disclose

Here is the part of this module that separates ethical practice from a technically-legal-but-misleading one. When a jewelry store issues its own in-house appraisal for a piece it just sold, it is not a neutral act, because the store has a direct financial interest in that number: an appraisal that comes in high makes the sale look like a good deal and supports the client's decision to buy. Independent appraisal analysis is direct about this: a seller "cannot remain impartial in evaluating their own merchandise," and even a third party the store hires and pays is still working for the store, not the client, which means it isn't truly independent either. A genuinely independent appraisal is one the client commissions and pays for directly, from an appraiser with no relationship to the sale.

This doesn't mean you shouldn't offer an in-house appraisal. It means you say plainly what it is: useful documentation of specifications and a starting value estimate, prepared by the store that sold the piece, and that for insurance purposes involving a significant investment, the client is always welcome to commission an independent appraisal from a gemologist with no connection to the sale. Saying this builds trust rather than undermining the sale, because it demonstrates you have nothing to hide about how the number was reached.

## The line between an ethical add-on and a scripted one

Consumer-protection principles developed for other high-ticket retail categories translate directly to jewelry, even though they weren't written for it: don't misrepresent an optional add-on as required, disclose clearly what is and isn't included, let the client decline without friction, and never sell coverage that duplicates something the client likely already has or provides no real service at all. Applied to a jewelry counter, that means never implying insurance is mandatory, never bundling a protection plan into a total without a clear yes, and never pitching a service plan to a client who mentions they already have a comprehensive homeowner's rider covering the exact same piece.

## An ethical framework for the pitch itself

A useful discipline, adaptable from broader ethical-selling guidance, reframes every add-on pitch around genuine service rather than attachment-rate pressure, in four steps:

1. **Observe.** Notice something specific and real, not a generic opportunity. A client mentioning she travels for work is a genuine occasion to mention insurance. A client with an heirloom-adjacent piece is a genuine occasion to mention appraisal documentation.
2. **Flag it specifically.** Name what you noticed, without immediately pitching. "You mentioned you travel a lot for work, that's exactly the situation insurance is built for."
3. **Offer real options, including one that costs nothing.** "You could add our service plan, get independent jewelry insurance on your own, or simply keep the receipt and grading report somewhere safe for now."
4. **Accept the answer.** If the client says not today, say "Of course" and move on. Don't circle back to it three more times before they leave.

## Timing and tone matter as much as the words

Jewelry trade guidance on upselling without alienating a client points to two practical rules worth following on every add-on pitch. First, timing: raise an add-on after the client has committed to the main piece, not before, since suggesting extras before the primary decision is made reads as distraction or pressure rather than service. Second, assumption without insistence: frame the add-on as something most clients in this exact situation choose, briefly state the benefit, and ask permission to say more if the client seems interested, rather than delivering a long unsolicited pitch. A hands-on moment, letting the client actually hold or feel the difference a care product makes on a piece, works better than description alone, the same principle Module 7 uses for quality comparisons.

Associate: "Since you're getting this resized for her, a lot of clients also add our care kit, it keeps the setting looking new between professional cleanings. Want me to walk you through what's in it?"

Client: "Sure, what's in it?"

That is a complete, ethical upsell: specific, honestly framed, and easy to decline if the answer had been no.

## Objections, mistakes and edge cases

| Situation | The trap | Better move |
|---|---|---|
| Pitching a protection plan to every client identically | Treating it as a scripted line rather than a real fit assessment | Notice something specific about this client's situation before pitching, per the observe-flag-offer framework |
| Client asks what a warranty actually covers | Being vague or overstating coverage to close the add-on | State plainly what is and isn't covered, including that most warranties don't cover loss or theft |
| Client already has broader insurance | Pitching a duplicate policy anyway | Ask what their current coverage includes before recommending anything additional |
| Offering an in-house appraisal | Presenting it as a fully independent, neutral valuation | Disclose that it's prepared by the selling store, and mention the independent-appraisal option for significant purchases |
| Client declines an add-on | Continuing to pitch it again before they leave | Accept the answer gracefully and move on; repeated pressure erodes trust built earlier in the visit |
| Bundling an add-on into the total without a clear ask | Assuming silence is consent | Always get an explicit yes before adding any protection plan or insurance referral to the ticket |
| A care product doesn't fit this specific piece | Selling it anyway because it's part of a standard bundle | Recommend care products based on the piece's actual metal and stones, not a blanket default |

## On the floor: applying it this week

- **Monday:** Before your next add-on pitch, notice one specific, real detail about the client's situation, and mention that instead of a generic script.
- **Tuesday:** Practice explaining the actual difference between a store warranty and jewelry insurance out loud until you can do it in two sentences without notes.
- **Wednesday:** The next time you offer an in-house appraisal, practice disclosing plainly that it's prepared by the store, and mention the independent-appraisal option for significant pieces.
- **Thursday:** Practice the observe-flag-offer-accept framework on one real add-on conversation today, including offering an option that costs nothing.
- **Friday:** Review this week's add-on pitches. For each one, ask: did the client actually need this, or did I pitch it identically to everyone?
- **All week:** Track how many clients say yes to an add-on after a specific, honest pitch versus a generic one. The gap is the value of doing this ethically.
