# Module 3 — Presenting the Piece: Story, Craft, and Value Without a Script

> **Course:** C2 — The Fine Jewelry Sales Conversation  
> **Learning objective:** Turn a confirmed discovery brief into an accurate, client-specific product presentation that invites participation and makes required information clear.

## 1. A presentation is not a recital

A client tells you they want a yellow-gold pendant for everyday wear: refined, personal, and not overly bright. You show a diamond bezel pendant and begin: “This is 18K gold, with a round brilliant diamond, and it has a beautiful chain.” Everything may be true, but none of it proves you listened.

A better presentation begins with the client’s words:

> “You said you wanted something refined enough for every day and a little personal, without a high-profile look. I selected this bezel pendant because the warm gold gives you that quiet presence, and the smooth setting keeps the diamond close to the neckline. May I show you how it sits when layered?”

The product did not change. The meaning did. A strong presentation connects an observable feature to a client need, then pauses for the client’s reaction.

## 2. The six-part presentation loop

Use this sequence as a guide, not a monologue.

1. **Connect:** Recall the relevant discovery detail.
2. **Show:** Identify the piece and one visible feature.
3. **Translate:** Explain why that feature may serve the client’s stated preference.
4. **Invite:** Let the client handle, try on, compare, or view the piece from a meaningful angle.
5. **Check:** Ask what they notice and what feels right or wrong.
6. **Disclose and next step:** State material facts using approved records, then agree on the next comparison or action.

Example:

> “You mentioned a low-profile ring for daily wear. This one has a bezel setting, so the stone is framed by metal rather than held by raised prongs. Try it on and see how the profile feels next to your hand. What do you notice compared with the prong-set version?”

Do not promise that any setting is indestructible. The client’s routine, the exact design, and the approved product information determine what may be said.

## 3. Translate features into client meaning

A feature is a verifiable attribute. A benefit is how that attribute may matter to this client. Keep the benefit conditional and specific.

| Feature | Client-focused translation |
|---|---|
| Bezel setting | “Its smooth outline may suit the lower-profile, streamlined feel you described.” |
| 18K yellow gold | “This gives the richer yellow tone you said you reach for in your existing pieces.” |
| Adjustable chain | “You can test two lengths to see whether it works best alone or with your layers.” |
| Textured surface | “The texture creates interest without relying only on a larger stone or more sparkle.” |
| Matching earrings and pendant | “These can create a coordinated look, while still letting you choose one piece today.” |

Avoid turning a feature into an unsupported guarantee. “This bezel setting will never catch” and “18K is always better” are claims, not thoughtful translations.

## 4. Story, craft, and value

### Story should be relevant

Use a design story only when it helps the client understand the piece or connect it to their purpose. A story may be about silhouette, technique, motif, collection inspiration, or the maker—but only use approved, verified language.

Good: “The repeated links create movement, which may be why it feels less formal than the solid bangle you tried.”

Not good: “This is inspired by an ancient royal design” unless that statement is documented by the brand or product record.

### Craft should be observable or documented

You can point to what the client can see: hand-applied texture, a hinged construction, articulated links, polished and satin finishes, or a stone’s setting style. Use care around hidden construction, stone security, origin, artisan attribution, or production method. If you cannot confirm it in an approved source, do not infer it.

GIA’s bench guidance illustrates why exact setting language matters: secure setting depends on factors such as fit, sufficient contact, and the absence of gaps that permit movement. An associate should therefore describe the setting type and direct a concern to the workshop or service team—not claim a technical inspection result from the sales floor. [GIA Bench Tip 17](https://www.gia.edu/UK-EN/bench-tip-avoid-stone-loss-with-quality-assurance-benchmarks)

### Value is not a resale promise

Value can mean design, workmanship, materials, rarity as documented, service, versatility, or emotional significance. It does not mean an associate should predict appreciation, resale value, insurance value, or future market performance.

The FTC Jewelry Guides apply to direct and implied representations about jewelry, including type, grade, quality, metal content, size, treatment, durability, origin, price, and value. Use the product record and your approved pricing language for these claims. [FTC Jewelry Guides](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/topics/tools-consumers/jewelry-guides)

Useful language:

- “The difference in price here reflects the documented diamond size and the 18K-gold construction; let’s compare them side by side.”
- “I can explain what is included in the current price and connect you with the appropriate team for appraisal or insurance questions.”
- “I do not want to overstate it; let me check the product record before I answer that.”

## 5. Let the client do part of the work

Luxury service does not mean continuous talking. Create a moment for the client to look, touch where policy permits, try on, and compare.

Use prompts such as:

- “Would you like to see it at the shorter and longer lengths?”
- “Which one feels more like your existing collection?”
- “What are you responding to first: the color, the scale, or the texture?”
- “Would you prefer to compare the diamond size or the setting profile next?”

Silence after a good question is productive. The client is deciding, not rejecting you.

## 6. Disclosure is part of the presentation

Disclosure is not a disclaimer to rush through at checkout. It is information the client needs to make an informed choice. Follow your company’s approved disclosure, care, and product-record process.

When relevant, address material composition, treatment, special-care needs, metal quality, gemstone description, warranty or service terms, and any limitations exactly as approved. The FTC warns against misrepresenting material aspects of jewelry, including treatment, durability, origin, and value. [FTC: In the Loupe](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/loupe-advertising-diamond-gemstones-pearls)

A clean transition sounds like this:

> “Before we compare the two, I want to make sure you have the care information that belongs with this stone. I will show you the approved product details, then we can decide whether the design still fits the everyday wear you described.”

If the answer is unknown, pause. “I want to give you an accurate answer, so I am going to check the product record rather than guess.” Accuracy is better service than fluency.

## 7. Recover when a client pushes back

| Client response | Unhelpful reaction | Better response |
|---|---|---|
| “Why is this one more?” | “It is just better quality.” | “Let’s identify the documented differences in material, stone, construction, and design.” |
| “Will it hold its value?” | “Absolutely—it is an investment.” | “I cannot predict future value. I can explain the piece, its current price, and the services included.” |
| “Is that stone untreated?” | Guessing from appearance | “Let me check the product documentation so I can answer accurately.” |
| “I like it, but it may be too much.” | “You will get used to it.” | “Is it the scale, sparkle, color, or profile that feels like too much?” |

## 8. Practice: the 60-second presentation

Choose one approved item and one discovery brief. In 60 seconds:

1. Recall one client preference.
2. Identify one observable or documented feature.
3. Translate it into a conditional client benefit.
4. Invite a try-on or comparison.
5. Ask one checking question.
6. Name one piece of information that must be verified in the product record before you say it.

Partner feedback: Did the presentation sound like a speech, or did it leave room for the client? Was every factual claim supported by what was visible or documented?

## Self-check

- Did I connect this piece to something the client actually said?
- Did I distinguish an observable feature from a claim that needs documentation?
- Did I avoid promises about durability, resale, future value, or origin?
- Did I invite the client to experience the piece and give an opinion?
- Did I make relevant disclosure and care information understandable?
- Did I identify the next comparison instead of overwhelming the client?
