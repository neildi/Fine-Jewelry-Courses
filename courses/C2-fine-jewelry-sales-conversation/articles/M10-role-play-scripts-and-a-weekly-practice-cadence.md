## What happens to training by next Tuesday

Psychologist Hermann Ebbinghaus's forgetting curve, a foundational and widely cited finding in learning science, describes something every sales manager has watched happen in real life: people forget a large share of new information within a day or two of learning it, unless it is deliberately revisited. Exact percentages vary across the many sources that cite this research, but the direction is not in dispute, and it applies directly to the nine modules before this one. Reading a script about the assumptive close once is not the same as being able to deliver it, calmly and confidently, to a real client under real pressure. The gap between those two things is practice, specifically the kind that's repeated and spaced out, not crammed into a single orientation day and never revisited.

This module doesn't teach new sales technique. It gives you the scripts and the calendar to make Modules 1 through 9 actually stick.

## Why role-play specifically, not just re-reading

Retail sales consultant Bob Phibbs, writing for the Washington Retail Association, makes the case for role-play directly: it's how a manager puts an employee into an imaginary sales situation with a hypothetical shopper to see, in a low-stakes setting, how well they can actually apply what they've learned. RetailDoc's own guidance on this technique frames the payoff plainly: the more role-play scenarios a team has genuinely practiced, the less likely associates are to freeze on an objection, fall back to discounting instead of demonstrating value, or skip a close entirely, precisely the failure patterns Modules 5, 6, and 9 warn against.

The learning-science principle underneath this is straightforward: rehearsing a specific skill out loud, with feedback, under conditions that resemble the real moment, builds fluency in a way that silent reading does not. You already know the content. Role-play is where you find out whether you can actually use it.

## Rules for running it well

Phibbs's guidance, drawn from real retail-floor experience, includes several practical rules worth following exactly, because getting role-play wrong is worse than not doing it at all:

- **Never do it in front of customers.** Practice happens off the floor or during a quiet moment, not while a real client can overhear or feel like a training exercise.
- **Watch for "kill the leader."** When a group starts arguing about whether a specific line or technique was said correctly instead of practicing, stop that thread and simply start a new round rather than letting the exercise derail into debate.
- **The customer role should challenge, not sabotage.** Whoever plays the client should respond the way a real client might, including realistic hesitation or objections, but shouldn't try to deliberately trip up the associate or simply recite a script verbatim. The associate needs to practice finding the answer using the actual sales process, not memorizing a fixed exchange.
- **Do it more than once.** A single role-play session run during onboarding and never repeated is, in practical terms, the same as not training at all once a few weeks have passed. Role-play only works as an ongoing habit, not a one-time checkbox.

## The script bank: four scenarios from Modules 1-9

Each scenario below pulls its scoring criteria directly from the module it practices. Assign roles (associate, client, observer), run each for three to five minutes, then debrief using the criteria listed.

**Scenario 1 — The opener and the brush-off (Module 1).** Client role: browse the bridal case, respond to the first opener with "just looking." Associate goal: avoid "Can I help you?" entirely, use a statement or non-business opener, then respond to "just looking" with a validating, open-ended follow-up. Observer checks: did the associate avoid both banned phrases, and did the "just looking" response invite more than a one-word answer?

**Scenario 2 — Buying signal into price objection (Modules 4 and 5).** Client role: ask a clear buying-signal question ("Can you size this down?"), then, once the associate moves to close, add "but that's more than I wanted to spend." Associate goal: recognize the signal, avoid re-explaining features, then diagnose the price objection ("Compared to what?" or "Is it the price, or more than you wanted to spend?") before responding. Observer checks: did the associate stop talking after the signal appeared, and did they diagnose before reacting to the price pushback?

**Scenario 3 — The phone on the counter (Module 7).** Client role: pull up a lower price on a phone and ask the associate to match it. Associate goal: ask to see the listing, verify carat/cut/clarity/certifying lab before responding, and use either a side-by-side reframe or an honest acknowledgment if a genuine match isn't possible. Observer checks: did the associate verify before reacting, and did they avoid an immediate, unverified discount?

**Scenario 4 — The close (Module 9).** Client role: express clear interest but hesitate slightly ("I really like this one..."). Associate goal: choose and deliver one of the three closing techniques (assumptive, alternate choice, or reflexive) confidently, matched to the moment. Observer checks: did the associate actually ask for the sale, and did their tone match the words?

## Running a 15-minute practice huddle

A widely used structure for short, frequent skill practice breaks a session into three parts: a brief warm-up to set intention, a focused core drill, and a short reflection. Adapted for a jewelry sales floor huddle, that looks like roughly 3 minutes reviewing which scenario and skill you're drilling today, 8 minutes actually running the role-play (one or two reps through the scenario), and 4 minutes of specific feedback using the observer checks above, not a general "good job." Specific, immediate feedback on an observable behavior (did you ask the diagnostic question, did you stop talking after the signal) works better than a vague impression, because it gives the next attempt something concrete to correct.

## The weekly and monthly cadence

Spaced repetition, the practice of revisiting a skill at increasing intervals rather than cramming it once, is well supported across learning-science and sales-training sources as producing more durable skill than a single intensive session. Applied to this course's own nine modules, a simple, sustainable cadence looks like this:

- **Monday huddle (15 minutes):** Run one script-bank scenario tied to this week's focus module.
- **Wednesday micro-check (5 minutes):** A quick verbal recall check: "What are the three verbal buying signals from Module 4?" or "Name the three ways to ask for the sale."
- **Friday review (15 minutes):** Re-run a scenario from two or three weeks ago, not just this week's, so earlier skills get revisited before they fade rather than only ever being drilled once.
- **Monthly:** Rotate back through all four script-bank scenarios once, regardless of which module the team is currently focused on, so the earliest skills (openers, buying signals) stay fresh alongside the newest ones (closing).

## Objections, mistakes and edge cases

| Situation | The trap | Better move |
|---|---|---|
| Team ran role-play once during onboarding | Assuming the training is "done" | Treat role-play as an ongoing weekly habit, not a one-time event |
| A role-play session turns into arguing about exact wording | Letting the group debate instead of practicing | Stop the argument, start a fresh round; debate belongs in a separate conversation |
| The person playing the client tries to trip up the associate | Turning practice into a "gotcha" exercise | The client role should challenge realistically, not sabotage or recite a fixed script |
| Feedback after a role-play is vague ("good job," "needs work") | The associate has nothing concrete to improve next time | Use the specific observer checks tied to each scenario's source module |
| Practice only covers this week's newest module | Earlier skills quietly fade from disuse | Rotate older scenarios back in on a spaced schedule, not just the newest topic |
| Role-play happens where customers can see or hear it | Practice feels like a performance, and customers may be confused | Always practice off the floor or during a genuinely quiet moment |
| No practice happens at all after initial reading | Content from Modules 1-9 fades within days per the forgetting curve | Commit to the weekly cadence above, even in a short, 15-minute form |

## On the floor: applying it this week

- **Monday:** Run Scenario 1 (the opener and the brush-off) as a 15-minute huddle, using the observer checks from Module 1.
- **Tuesday:** Practice one closing technique from Module 9 out loud, solo or with a colleague, for five minutes.
- **Wednesday:** Run a 5-minute micro-check: quiz a colleague on the four verbal buying signals from Module 4.
- **Thursday:** Run Scenario 3 (the phone on the counter) as a 15-minute huddle, using the observer checks from Module 7.
- **Friday:** Re-run Scenario 2 (buying signal into price objection) even though it isn't this week's newest topic, to keep it from fading.
- **All week and beyond:** Keep the Monday/Wednesday/Friday cadence running permanently, rotating which scenario gets deep practice, so no module from this course is ever practiced only once.
