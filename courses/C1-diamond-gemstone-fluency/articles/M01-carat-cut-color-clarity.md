# Carat, Cut, Color, Clarity: What the Client Actually Sees

## The sale you lose by knowing the answer

It is a Saturday afternoon and you are three weeks into the job. A couple stops at the bridal case and points at two round diamonds sitting side by side on the pad. They are within a few hundred dollars of each other. The question is the simplest one in the business: "So what's the difference between these two?"

You know this. You say the one on the left is a G colour, VS2 clarity, Excellent cut, and the one on the right is an H, VS1, Very Good. You explain that colour runs from D to Z and clarity from Flawless to I3. Every word is accurate. The couple nods politely. Her partner says they will look at one more place and think about it. They leave in under four minutes, and on Monday you hear they bought somewhere else.

Nothing you said was wrong. The problem is that you answered a question about two objects on the pad by describing a system of letters. They asked what the difference is. They meant "show me." You had two stones, a case light, and a loupe within reach, and used none of them.

This article is about closing that gap. By the end of it you will be able to walk up to your own case, pick two stones, and demonstrate all four Cs in a way a client can verify with their own eyes.

## Why this matters

Almost every entry-level fine jewelry posting is written around a sales number. Kay Jewelers asks consultants to "meet individual and team sales goals" and to "provide information regarding extended service plans and financing options." Effy asks associates to "meet or exceed sales targets." Neither posting requires a GIA credential at this tier, and Blue Nile states that "jewelry experience [is] a plus but not required." You were hired expecting to learn the product on the job.

The metrics that follow are the ones store leaders watch: closing ratio, average transaction value, conversion. A diamond inquiry that ends in "we'll think about it" costs you on all three. Product fluency converts an inquiry into a decision, but only the demonstrable kind. A client who has been told about a lettering system has been given homework, and homework gets done at a competitor's counter. One bridal employer names the skill directly: "make complicated jewelry concepts feel simple and approachable."

## Carat is weight, not size

Carat weight is the easiest C to state and the easiest to mislead with. It is a measure of mass. It is not a measure of how large the stone looks when it is on a hand. Two stones of identical carat weight can present very differently face-up, because weight can be distributed downward into a deep pavilion instead of outward across the table. GIA accounts for this within cut grading through what it calls "weight ratio," the description of a diamond's overall weight in relation to its diameter. A stone carrying excess weight below the girdle is heavier without looking bigger, and the client is paying for carats they cannot see. So "how big is it" and "how heavy is it" are different questions, and the client is almost always asking the first.

Client: "We're trying to stay at one carat. Is this one a full carat?"

Associate: "It's just under. Before we settle on the weight, can I show you something? Let me put this one and this one next to each other face-up. Tell me which looks larger to you."

Client: "The left one, easily."

Associate: "The left one is the lighter of the two. It spreads wider across the top, so more of its weight is doing visible work. If what matters is how it reads on your hand, we should be shopping for that, and the number on the tag becomes a budget question rather than a size question."

The practical move: get a millimetre gauge, measure the face-up diameter of the round stones in your case, and write them on your own reference card. Your inventory is what you have to sell, and knowing its actual spread beats a memorised conversion chart.

## Cut is the only C a human made

Colour, clarity and carat weight are geology. Cut is a decision someone made at a wheel, and it is the C that most changes whether a stone looks alive in the case. Cut describes how a diamond interacts with light: the arrangement and proportions of its facets, not its outline. Shape and cut are different words. Oval is a shape. Cut is the overall design.

One limit to know before you speak: GIA cut grades are only assigned to standard round brilliant diamonds, the only cut with standardised facets. Everything else (marquise, emerald, pear, oval, heart, triangle) is a fancy shape and carries no cut grade. New associates routinely promise an "Excellent cut oval" and have to walk it back when the report arrives.

The anatomy is worth memorising because you will point at it from top to bottom: crown, girdle and pavilion. The large flat facet on top is the table. Every round brilliant has 57 or 58 facets, the 58th being the tiny flat facet at the bottom of the pavilion called the culet.

GIA's cut grading system runs from Excellent to Poor and assesses three things together:

| What is assessed | Components | What the client can see |
|---|---|---|
| Face-up appearance | Brightness (total internal and external reflection of white light), fire (dispersion into spectral colours), scintillation (the pattern of light and dark, and sparkle when moved) | All of it, immediately, if you move the stone |
| Design | Weight ratio (weight relative to diameter), durability (risk of damage inherent in the proportions) | Indirectly: spread, and where the stone will be worn |
| Craftsmanship | Polish (condition of facet surfaces), symmetry (exactness of facet shape and placement) | Under a loupe, with guidance |

Brightness, fire and scintillation are the three words worth teaching a client. Each is a separate thing to look for, and none requires trust.

Client: "They both sparkle. I honestly can't tell them apart."

Associate: "They do, but they sparkle differently. Hold this one still under the light, that flat white return is brightness. Now rock it slowly, maybe an inch. See those flashes of red and blue? That's fire, light being split into colours. Now do the same with the other one."

Client: "The second one goes a bit grey in the middle when I tilt it."

Associate: "You just read a cut difference without a report. That dark centre is light leaking out of the bottom instead of coming back to your eye."

Every grade in GIA's own cut-grade video is assigned under tightly controlled lighting against a reference set. Your case light is not a controlled environment, which is exactly why the report and the stone in the client's hand tell two different stories, and why you need both. Each grade covers a range of proportion sets, not a single ideal. That is why two Excellent-cut stones can look different from each other, and why personal preference still has room inside a grade.

## Color is grading the absence of colour

Most people picture a diamond with little or no hue. So grading colour in the normal range really means grading how successfully colour is absent. The GIA D-to-Z Color Scale runs from D, colorless, to Z, light yellow, light brown or light gray. GIA introduced it in 1953, and it deliberately starts at D rather than A to avoid confusion with the inconsistent systems that came before it: letters, numbers, Roman numerals, and vague terms like "blue white."

The part that most changes how you sell colour: it is graded against a set of masterstones under controlled lighting, face-down, not face-up in a showroom under LED spots. So "this is honest, not a euphemism": adjacent grades are extremely hard to separate without that comparison. The difference becomes legible across several steps, not one.

Notice how far apart two stones have to be on that scale before the difference is obvious. That is the visual argument for spending a client's budget where their eye will reward it.

One thing to keep straight: Fancy color means a diamond outside the normal colour range, more colour than a Z masterstone, or a hue other than yellow, gray or brown. There are no universal guidelines for the term, so treat any Fancy stone in your case as a conversation with your manager and the report, not a scale you can recite.

Client: "My sister said never go below G..."

Associate: "That is common advice and it is not wrong, it just skips a step. Let me show you a G and an I, face-up, in the setting you like. If you can see the difference, we buy the G with confidence. If you cannot, you have found budget that could go into cut or size instead."

## Clarity is birthmarks, not defects

Diamonds form under extreme heat and pressure, and small crystals get trapped as they grow. Internal and surface-reaching characteristics are inclusions; external features are blemishes. A diamond with none is extremely rare. Clarity is graded at 10x magnification on five factors, and knowing all five is what lets you answer "why is this one a VS1 and that one an SI1" without guessing.

| Factor | What the grader assesses |
|---|---|
| Size | The larger the inclusion relative to the diamond, the lower the grade |
| Number | The more characteristics visible face-up at 10x, the lower the grade |
| Location | The closer to the centre of the table, the greater the effect on the grade |
| Relief | How much the inclusion contrasts in brightness, darkness or colour against the diamond |
| Nature | The type of characteristic, and whether it affects durability; a chip or feather weighs more than a pinpoint |

Location is the one that surprises people. The same feather can produce two different grades depending on whether it sits under the table or near a prong.

GIA's clarity scale has six categories and 11 specific grades:

| Category | Grades | Definition |
|---|---|---|
| Flawless | FL | No inclusions and no blemishes visible |
| Internally Flawless | IF | No inclusions visible, but blemishes present |
| Very, Very Slightly Included | VVS1, VVS2 | Inclusions so slight they are difficult for a skilled grader |
| Very Slightly Included | VS1, VS2 | Inclusions observed with effort, characterised as minor |
| Slightly Included | SI1, SI2 | Inclusions are noticeable |
| Included | I1, I2, I3 | Inclusions are obvious and may affect transparency, brilliance and durability |

Most diamonds in jewellery stores fall between VS and SI. That is not a compromise band, it is the market: say it as a normal fact rather than an apology. The most valuable reframe here: inclusions are a byproduct of formation, and they help gemologists separate natural diamonds from synthetics and lookalikes. A stone's inclusions are unique enough that the plot on the report identifies that particular stone. An inclusion is an identity document, not a flaw to be excused.

GIA photographs two 1.00 ct, G colour, Excellent cut round brilliants side by side, one VVS1 and one VS2, and asks whether you can tell them apart. Work down that reference to the grade-by-grade photographs: GIA marks the grade-setting inclusion with a red box on each stone, and the caption explains why the same feather produces a VVS2 in one stone and a VS1 in another; in the VVS2 it sits where a prong will hide it, and in the VS1 it sits under the table where it cannot be hidden. Location, not just size, is what moves the grade, and that is the sentence that answers most client questions about clarity.

Learn six inclusion types by sight: feather, crystal, cloud, pinpoint, needle and internal graining. When you can name what a client is looking at through the loupe, an inclusion stops being a defect and becomes a feature you are identifying for them. GIA's own caution applies: these are magnified, so do not expect every stone to look exactly like the pictures.

Graders also check for evidence that a stone was treated to improve clarity, and any treatment is noted on the report. Treatment disclosure is its own module (Module 9); for now, the report is where you look, and you never guess.

## Building your two-stone demonstration

This is the deliverable: not a script, a routine you can run at your own case.

1. Pick your pair. Two round brilliants, similar carat weight, that differ clearly on one C. Start with cut, because it is the most visible without magnification.
2. Pull both reports. Read them before you ever show them. Know every grade on both stones.
3. Set your staging. Same pad, same distance from the case light, tweezers clean, loupe within reach.
4. Lead with the eye, follow with the paper. Client looks first, names a difference in their own words, then you show them where that difference is written down.
5. Give them the verb. "Hold it still" for brightness. "Rock it slowly" for fire and scintillation. "Look face-up from a normal distance" for colour and eye-cleanliness.
6. Name only the C you are demonstrating. One C per look. Four Cs in one breath is how the Saturday sale was lost.
7. Close the loop. "Which of those differences is worth paying for, to you?" That question turns a lesson into a decision.

Rehearse it out loud before you use it on a client. Ten minutes before opening, with a colleague playing the client, is enough.

## Objections, mistakes and edge cases

| Situation | The trap | Better move |
|---|---|---|
| "Is it flawless?" said about a VS2 | Saying "practically flawless" | "It is VS2. Let me show you what that means at 10x, then face-up, where you'll actually see it." |
| Client fixated on one carat | Selling weight you cannot see | Compare spread face-up; let the eye set the target |
| Fancy-shape client asks for a cut grade | Promising an "Excellent cut oval" | "Cut grades are assigned to round brilliants only. For an oval, here's how we judge it instead: brightness, symmetry, and whether you like the bow-tie." |
| "Why is this one cheaper?" | Guessing | Put both reports side by side and read the differing lines aloud |
| Client saw a lower price online | Defending your price defensively | Compare the two reports line by line; if you cannot see the other report, say so |
| Inclusion visible under the loupe | Apologising | "That is what makes this stone identifiable as this stone. Here it is on the plot. Now look face-up, tell me if you can find it." |
| Client asks if a stone was treated | Guessing to keep momentum | The report notes clarity treatments. Read it, or get your manager. Never estimate. |
| Two stones, same grades, different price | Assuming an error | Cut quality varies within a grade range; check polish, symmetry and spread, then ask your manager |

The most common mistake is not factual. It is talking through the demonstration instead of pausing to let the client look. Silence while someone examines a stone is working time, not dead air.

## Self-check

1. Can you name the parts of a round brilliant from top to bottom, and say how many facets it has?
2. Can you define brightness, fire and scintillation in one sentence each, without notes?
3. Can you explain why an oval has no cut grade, without sounding apologetic?
4. Can you state the range of the D-to-Z scale and explain what a masterstone is?
5. Can you list the six clarity categories in order and say how many specific grades there are?
6. Can you say where most jewellery-store diamonds fall on the clarity scale?
7. Can you explain why an inclusion is evidence of a natural diamond?
8. Can you run the two-stone demonstration end to end, one C at a time, in under five minutes?

If you missed more than two, work the resources below before asking for the observed demonstration.
