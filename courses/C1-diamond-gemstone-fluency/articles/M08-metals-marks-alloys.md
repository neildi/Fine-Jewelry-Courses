# Metals, Marks and Alloys

## The stamp nobody looks at until something goes wrong

A client returns a ring convinced it is "not real gold" because it bent slightly after a year of daily wear. You look inside the band and find the quality mark: 14K, alongside a maker's trademark. The ring is exactly what it was sold as. What was missing was an explanation, at the point of sale, of what 14K actually means and why a softer, more wearable alloy was the right choice for a daily ring in the first place. This module gives you that explanation.

## Why this matters

Metal-quality questions come up in nearly every sale, and clients frequently do not know what a karat number means, what platinum's purity requirements are, or why a hallmark exists at all. An associate who can read a quality mark fluently and explain the trade-offs between metals closes more sales and prevents the kind of confused, distrustful return described above. This also has a compliance dimension: quality marks are governed by federal regulation in the US and by law in the UK and other markets, and getting the vocabulary wrong is not just unpolished, it can misstate a legal disclosure.

## Gold karat purity, in one table

Karat measures the proportion of pure gold in an alloy, out of 24 parts. Pure gold is 24 karat; everything below that is gold mixed with other metals for durability, color, or cost.

| Karat | Fraction pure gold | Approximate percentage | Millesimal fineness mark |
|---|---|---|---|
| 24K | 24/24 | 100% | 999 |
| 22K | 22/24 | 91.6% | 916 |
| 18K | 18/24 | 75.0% | 750 |
| 14K | 14/24 | 58.3% | 583 |
| 10K | 10/24 | 41.7% | 417 |
| 9K | 9/24 | 37.5% | 375 |

The FTC's own framing is the clearest way to explain this to a client: an 18K piece is 18 parts gold mixed with six parts of another metal, and a 14K piece is 14 parts gold mixed with 10 parts of another metal. Higher karat means more pure gold and a richer color, but also a softer, more easily scratched metal. Lower karat trades some of that richness for durability and, generally, a lower price.

## Why lower karat is not a lesser choice

A common misconception is that lower karat gold is a compromise or a downgrade. In practice, 14K is often the better choice for an everyday ring precisely because the additional alloy content makes it more scratch- and dent-resistant than 18K, which is why many engagement ring bands are sold in 14K even at price points where 18K would be affordable. Frame this as a durability trade-off, not a quality hierarchy.

Client: "Is 14K gold worse than 18K?"

Associate: "Not worse, just different. 18K has more pure gold, so the color is a little richer, but it's also softer. 14K has more of the other metals mixed in, which makes it more resistant to scratches and dents. For a ring you'll wear every day, a lot of people actually prefer 14K for exactly that reason."

## Platinum and palladium: purity rules are different

Platinum and palladium are marked differently than gold, and the purity thresholds for calling something simply "platinum" or "palladium" are much higher. To carry an unqualified platinum quality mark, an item generally must be at least 95% pure platinum, or an alloy that is at least 95% platinum combined specifically with iridium or ruthenium. Lower-purity platinum alloys are marked with their exact composition instead, for example 950 Plat. for 95% platinum, 850 Plat. for 85% platinum, or 800 Pt. 200 Pall. for 80% platinum and 20% palladium. Palladium follows a similar logic: an unqualified palladium mark requires at least 95% pure palladium, or an alloy of at least 90% palladium combined with a short list of specified metals including platinum, iridium, ruthenium, rhodium, osmium, or gold.

## The trademark requirement

A karat or platinum mark alone is not the full picture. The FTC's own guidance is direct: near the karat quality mark, a client should also see the name or registered trademark of the company standing behind that mark, and if a piece of gold jewelry has a karat mark but no accompanying trademark, that is a signal not to buy it. Knowing this rule protects you as much as it protects the client, since it is a concrete, checkable standard you can point to rather than a vague reassurance.

## Hallmarks: a stricter system outside the US

Outside the US, particularly in the UK and other Commonwealth markets, hallmarking is a legal requirement, not an optional quality mark. A full UK hallmark consists of three compulsory elements: the sponsor's or maker's mark identifying who is responsible for the article, the metal and fineness mark showing the precious metal type and purity as a millesimal number, and the assay office mark identifying which independent office tested and marked the item. This is a meaningfully stricter system than the US's trademark-plus-karat-stamp approach, since every hallmarked item has been independently tested by a government-recognized assay office, not just self-certified by the maker.

## On the floor: applying it this week

- **Monday:** Find the quality mark on five pieces in your case and read them aloud, converting each karat number to its approximate percentage.
- **Tuesday:** Locate the accompanying trademark next to each karat mark and confirm you can find it without help.
- **Wednesday:** Practice the 14K-vs-18K durability conversation with a colleague until it sounds like guidance, not a downgrade pitch.
- **Thursday:** If your store carries platinum or palladium pieces, read their exact marks (950 Plat., for example) and confirm what percentage that represents.
- **Friday:** Ask your manager whether your store sources any hallmarked international pieces, and if so, review what a full hallmark looks like on one of them.

## Objections, mistakes and edge cases

| Situation | The trap | Better move |
|---|---|---|
| Client assumes 18K is always better than 14K | Not correcting the assumption | Explain the purity-versus-durability trade-off honestly, and recommend based on how the piece will be worn |
| Client finds a karat stamp with no trademark nearby | Not knowing this is a red flag | Explain that a legitimate US gold piece should carry a trademark alongside the karat mark |
| Client confuses platinum and white gold | Treating them as the same metal | Platinum is a distinct, denser, naturally white metal with its own purity marking system; white gold is a gold alloy plated or mixed to appear white |
| Client sees an unfamiliar mark like "800 Pt. 200 Pall." | Guessing at the meaning | Read it literally: this indicates 80% platinum and 20% palladium in the alloy |
| Client asks why a UK-made piece has more stamps than a US piece | Not knowing hallmarking is a legal requirement abroad | Explain that hallmarking is legally mandatory in the UK and involves independent third-party testing, unlike the US system |

## Self-check

1. Can you convert 10K, 14K, 18K, 22K, and 24K to their approximate gold percentages without a reference?
2. Can you explain why a lower karat is not necessarily a lower-quality choice?
3. Can you state the minimum platinum percentage required for an unqualified platinum mark?
4. Can you decode a mixed platinum-palladium mark like "800 Pt. 200 Pall."?
5. Can you explain the FTC's trademark-accompaniment rule for karat marks?
6. Can you name the three compulsory elements of a UK hallmark?
7. Can you explain the practical difference between platinum and white gold to a client?

If you missed more than two, review the FTC and Competition Bureau resources before your next metal-quality conversation.
