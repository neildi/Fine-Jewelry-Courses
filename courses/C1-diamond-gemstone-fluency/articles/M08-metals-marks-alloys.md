## The stamp nobody looks at until something goes wrong

A client returns a ring convinced it is "not real gold" because it bent slightly after a year of daily wear. You look inside the band and find the quality mark: 14K, alongside a maker's trademark. The ring is exactly what it was sold as. What was missing was an explanation, at the point of sale, of what 14K actually means and why a softer, more wearable alloy was the right choice for a daily ring in the first place. This module gives you that explanation, along with the adjacent terminology (vermeil, gold-filled, rhodium plating) that increasingly comes up as clients shop across a wider range of price points and metal types.

## Why this matters

Metal-quality questions come up in nearly every sale, and clients frequently do not know what a karat number means, what platinum's purity requirements are, or why a hallmark exists at all. An associate who can read a quality mark fluently and explain the trade-offs between metals closes more sales and prevents the kind of confused, distrustful return described above. This also has a compliance dimension: quality marks are governed by federal regulation in the US and by law in the UK and other markets, and getting the vocabulary wrong is not just unpolished, it can misstate a legal disclosure.

## Gold karat purity, in one table

Karat measures the proportion of pure gold in an alloy, out of 24 parts. Pure gold is 24 karat; everything below that is gold mixed with other metals for durability, color, or cost.

| Karat | Fraction pure gold | Approximate percentage | Millesimal fineness mark |
|---|---|---|---|
| 24K | 24/24 | 100% | 999 |
| 22K | 22/24 | 91.6% | 916 |
| 18K | 18/24 | 75.0% | 750 |
| 14K | 14/24 | 58.3% | 583 |
| 10K | 10/24 | 41.7% | 417 |
| 9K | 9/24 | 37.5% | 375 |

The FTC's own framing is the clearest way to explain this to a client: an 18K piece is 18 parts gold mixed with six parts of another metal, and a 14K piece is 14 parts gold mixed with 10 parts of another metal. Higher karat means more pure gold and a richer color, but also a softer, more easily scratched metal. Lower karat trades some of that richness for durability and, generally, a lower price.

## Why lower karat is not a lesser choice

A common misconception is that lower karat gold is a compromise or a downgrade. In practice, 14K is often the better choice for an everyday ring precisely because the additional alloy content makes it more scratch- and dent-resistant than 18K, which is why many engagement ring bands are sold in 14K even at price points where 18K would be affordable. Frame this as a durability trade-off, not a quality hierarchy.

Client: "Is 14K gold worse than 18K?"

Associate: "Not worse, just different. 18K has more pure gold, so the color is a little richer, but it's also softer. 14K has more of the other metals mixed in, which makes it more resistant to scratches and dents. For a ring you'll wear every day, a lot of people actually prefer 14K for exactly that reason."

## Why white gold needs rhodium plating

A detail that surprises many clients: white gold is not naturally, purely white. Gold in its pure form is yellow, and mixing it with white alloy metals like palladium, nickel, or silver produces an alloy that is closer to a light, creamy off-white or pale yellow, not the bright, cool white most clients picture. To achieve that bright white, reflective look, jewelers apply a thin layer of rhodium, a member of the platinum family of metals, through an electroplating process.

Rhodium plating serves two purposes at once: it gives white gold its characteristic bright white color, and it adds a hard, protective, scratch-resistant surface layer, which also happens to act as a barrier between the wearer's skin and any nickel present in the underlying alloy, a point that matters for clients with nickel sensitivity. Because this plating is a thin surface layer, not something baked into the metal itself, it wears down gradually with everyday contact, friction from hands, tools, and surfaces, and needs to be periodically reapplied, typically referred to in the trade as a "rhodium re-plate" or "re-rhodium" service.

Client: "Why did my white gold ring start looking a little yellow after a couple of years?"

Associate: "That's actually really common, and it's not a defect. White gold on its own is a pale, slightly warm off-white, not bright white, so jewelers add a thin coating of a metal called rhodium to get that crisp white look. That coating wears down gradually with everyday wear, which is why the natural color underneath starts to show through over time. A simple re-plating service brings back the bright white finish, and most jewelers recommend doing it every year or two depending on how the piece is worn."

## Vermeil, gold-filled, and gold-plated: knowing the difference

As clients shop across a wider range of price points, especially in fashion and everyday jewelry categories, three terms come up that are frequently confused with each other and with solid karat gold: vermeil, gold-filled, and gold-plated. Each describes a different construction, with different durability and different legal requirements.

| Term | Legal definition (US, per FTC standards) | Typical construction | Approximate durability |
|---|---|---|---|
| Gold vermeil | Base metal must be 925 sterling silver; gold layer must be at least 10 karat; gold layer must measure at least 2.5 microns thick | Gold electroplated onto sterling silver at a legally defined minimum thickness | Moderate; can wear thin over years of heavy use, but the sterling silver base is itself a precious metal |
| Gold-filled | No single universal legal minimum thickness standard cited here, but a specific gold layer (commonly around 5 percent of total weight) is mechanically bonded to a base metal (commonly brass) using heat and pressure | A thick layer of gold sheet mechanically bonded to a base metal core, not electroplated | Generally the most durable of the three non-solid options; commonly cited to last 10-20 years with normal wear |
| Gold-plated | No standardized minimum thickness; can be extremely thin (a small fraction of a percent of total weight) | Gold electroplated onto a base metal, commonly brass or copper, often with a nickel or copper underlayer first | Least durable; can wear through relatively quickly with regular contact and friction |

The key distinction between vermeil and plain gold-plated jewelry is the base metal and a legally enforced minimum thickness: vermeil must use sterling silver underneath and meet a specific gold-layer-thickness standard, while ordinary gold-plated jewelry has no such requirement and commonly uses a much thinner gold layer over a non-precious base metal. Gold-filled sits apart from both, using a mechanically bonded (not electroplated) thicker gold layer, which is why it tends to outlast standard gold plating significantly.

Client: "What's the actual difference between vermeil and gold-plated? They both sound like a thin layer of gold."

Associate: "The base metal and the amount of gold are both different. Vermeil is specifically gold over sterling silver, with a legally required minimum thickness, so there's an actual regulated standard behind the term. Gold-plated usually means a much thinner layer of gold over a base metal like brass, with no legal minimum thickness required. Gold-filled is different still, it's a thicker layer of gold mechanically bonded to a base metal, which tends to hold up longer than either of the electroplated options."

## Platinum and palladium: purity rules are different

Platinum and palladium are marked differently than gold, and the purity thresholds for calling something simply "platinum" or "palladium" are much higher. To carry an unqualified platinum quality mark, an item generally must be at least 95% pure platinum, or an alloy that is at least 95% platinum combined specifically with iridium or ruthenium. Lower-purity platinum alloys are marked with their exact composition instead, for example 950 Plat. for 95% platinum, 850 Plat. for 85% platinum, or 800 Pt. 200 Pall. for 80% platinum and 20% palladium. Palladium follows a similar logic: an unqualified palladium mark requires at least 95% pure palladium, or an alloy of at least 90% palladium combined with a short list of specified metals including platinum, iridium, ruthenium, rhodium, osmium, or gold.

## Nickel allergy: a real client concern connected to white gold alloys

Some white gold alloys, particularly older or lower-cost formulations, use nickel as part of the alloy mix to achieve the pale color base gold needs before rhodium plating. Nickel is one of the most common contact allergens, and a genuine subset of clients will react to nickel-containing jewelry with skin irritation, redness, or a rash, particularly with prolonged skin contact.

This is not just a US anecdotal concern; it is directly regulated in the European Union. The EU's Nickel Directive, first established in 1994 and now incorporated into the broader REACH chemical regulation framework, restricts the rate of nickel release from items intended for direct and prolonged skin contact, including earrings, necklaces, bracelets, rings, and watch cases. The regulation sets a release limit of 0.5 micrograms per square centimeter per week for most jewelry items, and a stricter limit of 0.2 micrograms per square centimeter per week specifically for posts inserted into piercings, reflecting the more sensitive, prolonged contact of pierced jewelry.

While the US does not have an identical federal nickel release standard, being aware that this regulation exists in the EU is useful if you serve international clients or work in a market with EU ties, and it also explains why many modern white gold alloys use palladium instead of nickel specifically to reduce allergy risk, and why rhodium plating (discussed above) provides an additional barrier layer between the skin and any nickel in the base alloy.

Client: "I think I might be allergic to nickel. Is white gold safe for me?"

Associate: "It depends on the specific alloy. Some white gold uses nickel as part of the mix, and if you have a nickel sensitivity, that can cause irritation with prolonged contact. Many modern white gold pieces use palladium instead of nickel specifically to avoid this, and the rhodium plating on top also acts as a barrier layer. I'd recommend we check the specific alloy in a piece you're interested in, or consider platinum or a nickel-free white gold alternative if you know you're sensitive."

## The trademark requirement

A karat or platinum mark alone is not the full picture. The FTC's own guidance is direct: near the karat quality mark, a client should also see the name or registered trademark of the company standing behind that mark, and if a piece of gold jewelry has a karat mark but no accompanying trademark, that is a signal not to buy it. Knowing this rule protects you as much as it protects the client, since it is a concrete, checkable standard you can point to rather than a vague reassurance.

## Hallmarks: a stricter system outside the US

Outside the US, particularly in the UK and other Commonwealth markets, hallmarking is a legal requirement, not an optional quality mark. A full UK hallmark consists of three compulsory elements: the sponsor's or maker's mark identifying who is responsible for the article, the metal and fineness mark showing the precious metal type and purity as a millesimal number, and the assay office mark identifying which independent office tested and marked the item. This is a meaningfully stricter system than the US's trademark-plus-karat-stamp approach, since every hallmarked item has been independently tested by a government-recognized assay office, not just self-certified by the maker.

## Regional variation: metal preferences and terminology by market

- **United States:** 14K is the most common karat for everyday fine jewelry, with 18K reserved for higher-end pieces; vermeil and gold-filled terminology is increasingly common in the growing fashion/demi-fine jewelry segment.
- **United Kingdom and Europe:** 18K and 9K are both common (9K is the UK's traditional minimum legal karat for a "gold" hallmark), and hallmarking is a legal requirement, unlike the US system.
- **India and the Gulf:** 22K and 18K gold dominate, with purity verification and hallmarking (BIS hallmarking in India specifically) carrying significant cultural and regulatory weight; karat expectations in these markets run meaningfully higher than typical US 14K norms.
- **Cruise and travel retail:** Platinum and higher-karat gold pieces often perform well as premium, memorable purchases in these settings, while duty-free positioning can make metal-purity education an important trust-building step in a compressed sales window.

## Objections, mistakes and edge cases

| Situation | The trap | Better move |
|---|---|---|
| Client assumes 18K is always better than 14K | Not correcting the assumption | Explain the purity-versus-durability trade-off honestly, and recommend based on how the piece will be worn |
| Client finds a karat stamp with no trademark nearby | Not knowing this is a red flag | Explain that a legitimate US gold piece should carry a trademark alongside the karat mark |
| Client confuses platinum and white gold | Treating them as the same metal | Platinum is a distinct, denser, naturally white metal with its own purity marking system; white gold is a gold alloy plated to appear white |
| Client sees an unfamiliar mark like "800 Pt. 200 Pall." | Guessing at the meaning | Read it literally: this indicates 80% platinum and 20% palladium in the alloy |

| Client's white gold ring looks yellowish after a couple years | Assuming it is defective or low quality | Explain rhodium plating wear and offer a re-plating service, framing it as normal maintenance |
