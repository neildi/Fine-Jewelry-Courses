# Module 1 Self-Assessment Quiz: Carat, Cut, Color, Clarity

10 multiple-choice questions testing the terminal outcome: demonstrating the 4Cs using two stones from the case.

**1. A client asks which of two same-weight diamonds "looks bigger." What should you do first?**
A. Tell them the millimeter measurements from the report
B. Compare the two stones face-up and let them judge visually
C. Explain that carat weight determines size
D. Recommend the heavier stone automatically
**Answer: B** - Two stones of identical weight can spread differently face-up; showing them side by side lets the client judge actual visual size rather than relying on the weight number alone.

**2. Which shapes receive an official GIA cut grade?**
A. All fancy shapes including oval and pear
B. Only standard round brilliant diamonds
C. Round brilliants and emerald cuts only
D. Any shape with 57 or more facets
**Answer: B** - GIA only assigns cut grades to standard round brilliants, the only shape with standardized facet arrangement; fancy shapes are judged differently (Module 1 covers how to explain this).

**3. What are the three components of face-up cut appearance?**
A. Table, crown, pavilion
B. Brightness, fire, scintillation
C. Polish, symmetry, weight ratio
D. Depth, table percentage, girdle thickness
**Answer: B** - These are the three visible effects of light interacting with the stone; polish/symmetry and weight ratio are craftsmanship and design factors, not face-up appearance itself.

**4. A client says "never go below G color." How should you respond?**
A. Agree and steer them only toward G or better
B. Tell them that advice is wrong
C. Show them a G and an I face-up in the intended setting and let their eye decide
D. Explain the D-to-Z scale numerically
**Answer: C** - The goal is to let the client's own eye set the budget target rather than following generic advice blindly or dismissing it outright.

**5. Why does the GIA D-to-Z color scale start at D instead of A?**
A. D stands for "Diamond"
B. To avoid confusion with earlier inconsistent grading systems that used letters, numbers, and vague terms
C. Grades A through C were reserved for fancy colors
D. It is alphabetically arbitrary
**Answer: B** - GIA introduced the D-to-Z scale in 1953 specifically to replace inconsistent prior systems, starting at D to signal a clean break from those older scales.

**6. What does "eye-clean" mean?**
A. An official GIA clarity grade
B. A stone with no inclusions at all under 10x magnification
C. A trade-common term meaning inclusions aren't visible to the unaided eye at normal viewing distance
D. Any stone graded VS2 or better
**Answer: C** - Eye-clean is not an official grade; it must be verified on the specific stone in front of you, since even some VS2 stones fail this test depending on inclusion location.

**7. A client's stone is graded VS1, and a "matching" competitor stone is SI1 but cheaper. What is the best move?**
A. Assume the competitor made a pricing error
B. Disparage the competitor's grading standards
C. Compare both reports field by field, including polish, symmetry, and fluorescence
D. Match the competitor's price immediately
**Answer: C** - Two stones with different clarity grades and prices should be compared systematically across all report fields, not assumed to be equivalent or dismissed without checking.

**8. Why can two Excellent-cut diamonds look slightly different from each other?**
A. One of them must have a grading error
B. The Excellent grade covers a range of proportion combinations, not one single ideal shape
C. Excellent cut only applies to stones over 1 carat
D. Fluorescence causes the difference
**Answer: B** - GIA's cut grade bands allow for a range of table percentages, crown angles, and pavilion angles, all producing an Excellent grade, which is why two such stones are not visually identical.

**9. Why might a jeweler recommend a lower color grade (I or J) for a diamond going into a yellow gold setting?**
A. Lower grades are always cheaper regardless of setting
B. Yellow gold's warm tone visually complements a warmer-graded stone, so the color difference is less noticeable
C. Yellow gold makes all diamonds look colorless
D. GIA grades differently based on setting metal
**Answer: B** - The setting metal's reflected color affects perceived body color; warm-toned yellow gold can make a slightly warmer diamond appear whiter than the same stone would look in a white metal setting.

**10. What is the single most important framing for talking about inclusions with a client?**
A. Apologize for any visible inclusion
B. Avoid mentioning inclusions unless asked directly
C. Frame inclusions as identifying, naturally occurring features rather than defects
D. Tell the client all diamonds have the same number of inclusions
**Answer: C** - Reframing inclusions as identity markers (unique to that specific stone, and evidence of natural formation) is the core technique for turning a technical clarity conversation into a trust-building moment rather than a defensive one.
