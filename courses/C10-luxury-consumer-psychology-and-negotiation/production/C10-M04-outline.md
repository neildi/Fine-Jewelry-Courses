# C10-M04 Outline: Anchoring and the Decoy Effect: Presenting Price and Options Responsibly

## Module Metadata
- Course: C10 - Luxury Consumer Psychology & Negotiation
- Module: 4 of 8
- Persona: P2
- Working title: Anchoring and the Decoy Effect: Presenting Price and Options Responsibly
- Learning objective: Associates can explain the anchoring effect and its ethical implications for price sequencing, explain the decoy effect including its important real-world replication caveat, distinguish legitimate multi-option presentation from manipulative decoy engineering, and apply a genuine-option verifiability test to any pricing presentation.
- Terminal outcome tie-in: "Apply anchoring and related pricing-psychology principles responsibly when presenting price and options."

## Section Map

1. **Why This Module Matters** — Frames price presentation as changing perception independent of actual value, and sets up the module's dual goal of accuracy and ethics.
2. **Anchoring: The First Number Sets the Frame** — Tversky and Kahneman's foundational research; the ethical distinction between real and artificially inflated anchors.
3. **The Decoy Effect: A Famous Finding With a Real Asterisk** — Huber, Payne, and Puto's original research; the important 2014 replication-failure caveat missing from most popular sales training.
4. **Three Genuine Reasons to Show Multiple Options (Distinct From Engineering a Decoy)** — Legitimate range presentation vs. manipulative decoy engineering.
5. **The Ethical Line for Both Effects** — A genuine-option verifiability test adapted from Module 3's verifiability framework.
6. **A Worked Scenario: Two Ways to Show Three Rings** — Illustrative synthesis contrasting honest range presentation against manufactured decoy engineering.
7. **Practice Exercise** — Presenting real store price points honestly and practicing a specific, honest answer to "why does this cost more?"
8. **Key Takeaways** — Five-point recap.

## Production Notes
- Anchoring's foundational research lineage (Tversky and Kahneman's 1974 "Judgment under Uncertainty: Heuristics and Biases" paper, and the resulting "anchor-and-adjust" hypothesis) is sourced from The Decision Lab's "Anchoring Bias" reference article and Wikipedia's "Anchoring effect" article, both independently describing the same foundational lineage. The pricing-specific application (the first price a client encounters becomes their mental reference point) is sourced from themindreader.ai's "Mastering Pricing Psychology for High-Ticket Luxury Sales" article and a peer-reviewed Frontiers in Psychology / PMC academic study, "An Experimental Study on Anchoring Effect of Consumers...", both independently confirming the anchoring mechanism in a consumer-pricing context. The specific luxury-retail application (releasing an ultra-expensive item to make other high-priced items seem more reasonable by comparison) is sourced from iiad.edu.in's "The Psychology of Pricing" article.
- The decoy effect's foundational research (Huber, Payne, and Puto's 1982 original documentation of asymmetric dominance) is sourced from Wikipedia's "Decoy effect" article and The Decision Lab's "Decoy Effect" reference article, both independently describing the same foundational research and the "asymmetrically dominated" terminology.
- IMPORTANT AND SPECIFICALLY VALUABLE SOURCING NOTE: the claim that two large 2014 replication efforts found the decoy effect "mostly disappeared once you used realistic stimuli" is sourced directly from atticusli.com's "The Decoy Effect: The Pricing-Page Tactic That Doesn't..." article, which explicitly and specifically addresses the replication crisis literature on this effect ("Real in the original paradigm... adding an asymmetrically dominated option to a set of abstract two-attribute numeric choices does, on average, increase the share of the option that dominates the decoy. This has been replicated and is not in doubt" — but the effect's robustness with realistic stimuli is separately and specifically called into question). This is a single-source claim within this research pass, though the source explicitly engages with and cites the specific 2014 replication literature by name; this nuance is not found in, and is not contradicted by, the other decoy-effect sources consulted (Wikipedia, The Decision Lab, yukaichou.com), which describe the classic finding without addressing its replication history. Given the significance of this caveat for the module's ethical argument, it should be flagged for independent verification against the primary 2014 replication studies themselves before being treated as fully settled.
- The three genuine (non-manipulative) reasons for showing multiple price points, and the genuine-option verifiability test, are this module's own original ethical framework, directly extending Module 3's previously-established verifiability-test logic (see C10-M03) rather than being drawn from a new external source.
- The worked scenario and practice exercise are ORIGINAL illustrative syntheses, not externally sourced cases.
