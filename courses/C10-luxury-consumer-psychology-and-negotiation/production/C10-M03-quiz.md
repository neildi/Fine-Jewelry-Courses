**1. What did Robert Cialdini's own research find about the scarcity tactic's effect on desire and compliance, regardless of whether the underlying scarcity was genuine or fabricated?**
A) The tactic only works when the scarcity is genuinely real; fabricated scarcity has no psychological effect
B) The tactic's effect on increasing desire and compliance remained potent whether the scarcity was genuine or perceived, meaning the psychological mechanism doesn't distinguish between real and fabricated scarcity
C) Fabricated scarcity is always more effective than genuine scarcity
D) Cialdini's research found no relationship between scarcity and consumer desire

**Correct answer: B**
Explanation: This finding is exactly why fabricating scarcity is both tempting (it works) and specifically unethical (it exploits a psychological vulnerability rather than informing a genuine decision).

**2. What psychological mechanism explains why scarcity increases desire, according to this module?**
A) Scarcity has no identifiable psychological mechanism behind it
B) Psychological reactance — when a person perceives their freedom to choose or acquire something is being restricted, they experience a strong desire to reassert that freedom by wanting the restricted thing even more
C) Scarcity works purely through logical, rational evaluation of supply and demand
D) Reactance only applies to inexpensive purchases, never to luxury goods

**Correct answer: B**
Explanation: Reactance is the specific mechanism behind scarcity's power, and it's why a genuinely limited piece creates real urgency without needing any embellishment.

**3. What is the "exclusivity paradox" in luxury marketing, as described in this module?**
A) There is no real tension in luxury marketing between growth and exclusivity
B) A luxury brand needs to grow and generate revenue, but doing so by making itself more widely available directly threatens the very exclusivity that made it desirable in the first place
C) Exclusivity always increases in direct proportion to a brand's revenue growth
D) The exclusivity paradox only applies to mass-market brands, never to luxury goods

**Correct answer: B**
Explanation: This documented tension in luxury marketing research plays out at the level of individual sales conversations, not just brand strategy.

**4. According to research cited in this module, when can social proof appeals ("this is our most popular design") actually backfire?**
A) Social proof never backfires under any circumstances
B) Social proof can backfire and produce reactance specifically among clients who see themselves as having high expertise or a non-conformist identity
C) Social proof only works with luxury clients and never with any other type of client
D) Social proof appeals are equally effective with every single client regardless of their self-perception

**Correct answer: B**
Explanation: This is a genuinely important caution for luxury retail specifically, since a meaningful share of serious luxury clients may identify as having expertise or valuing distinctiveness over conformity.

**5. What is the practical "verifiability test" this module recommends applying to any scarcity, exclusivity, or social-proof claim?**
A) Whether the claim sounds impressive regardless of whether it's true
B) Whether the specific claim being made could survive the client independently checking it
C) Whether the claim can be delivered with a confident tone of voice
D) Whether the claim includes a specific dollar amount

**Correct answer: B**
Explanation: A genuinely limited piece survives this test; a vague, unverifiable claim usually doesn't, which is exactly what separates ethical use from manipulation.

**6. In the worked scenario, why was the first associate's statement ("we only have three of these left, and the designer isn't making more") considered ethical scarcity?**

Explanation: Specificity and verifiability are exactly what distinguish this honest scarcity claim from the vague, unverifiable pressure used in the second example.

**7. Why does this module argue that fabricating scarcity, exclusivity, or social proof is not just risky but specifically unethical, beyond simply being a bad long-term business practice?**
A) These tactics are illegal in all retail contexts
B) It relies on exploiting a real psychological vulnerability (reactance, herd-following) against a client's genuine interests, rather than informing a decision with true, verifiable information
C) There is no meaningful ethical distinction between honest and fabricated versions of these claims

**Correct answer: B**
Explanation: The distinction between informing a decision and bypassing a client's own reasoning through exploited vulnerability is the core ethical line this module draws.
