# C10-M04 Fact-Check: Anchoring and the Decoy Effect: Presenting Price and Options Responsibly

## Verification Status: Anchoring's foundational research and pricing application well-corroborated across multiple academic and trade sources; the decoy effect's original finding is well-corroborated, but the module's important replication-failure caveat rests on a single source and should be independently verified against the primary 2014 studies

## Claims Reviewed

**1. Anchoring effect foundational research (Tversky and Kahneman, 1974, "Judgment under Uncertainty: Heuristics and Biases"; the anchor-and-adjust hypothesis).**
Status: CONFIRMED CONSISTENT across The Decision Lab's "Anchoring Bias" reference article and Wikipedia's "Anchoring effect" article, both independently describing the same 1974 foundational paper and the anchor-and-adjust hypothesis.

**2. Anchoring's pricing-specific application (the first price a client encounters becomes their mental reference point for subsequent evaluations).**
Status: Sourced from themindreader.ai's "Mastering Pricing Psychology for High-Ticket Luxury Sales" article and a peer-reviewed Frontiers in Psychology / PMC academic study, "An Experimental Study on Anchoring Effect of Consumers..." (an actual experimental study on consumer anchoring, not just a trade-press description), both independently confirming this mechanism in a consumer-pricing context.

**3. The specific luxury-retail application (releasing an ultra-expensive item to make other high-priced items seem more reasonable by comparison).**
Status: Sourced from iiad.edu.in's "The Psychology of Pricing: How Brands Engineer Value Perception" article, which explicitly describes this exact luxury-retail anchoring tactic.

**4. Decoy effect foundational research (Huber, Payne, and Puto's 1982 original documentation of asymmetric dominance).**
Status: CONFIRMED CONSISTENT across Wikipedia's "Decoy effect" article and The Decision Lab's "Decoy Effect" reference article, both independently describing the same foundational 1982 research and "asymmetrically dominated" terminology, and further corroborated by yukaichou.com's "The Decoy Effect" article and a LinkedIn business-strategy article both independently citing the same lineage (including Ariely and Wallsten's related 1995 work).

**5. The claim that two large 2014 replication efforts found the decoy effect largely disappeared with realistic stimuli, as opposed to the abstract laboratory paradigm where it was originally documented.**
Status: IMPORTANT SOURCING NOTE — sourced directly and specifically from atticusli.com's "The Decoy Effect: The Pricing-Page Tactic That Doesn't..." article, which explicitly engages with and names the 2014 replication literature ("In 2014, two large replication efforts found the effect mostly disappeared once you used realistic stimuli"). This is the module's single most consequential sourcing caveat: while this source explicitly and specifically addresses the replication-crisis literature by name, and the claim is not contradicted by the other decoy-effect sources consulted (which simply do not address replication history one way or the other), this claim currently rests on one source within this research pass. Given how directly this caveat shapes the module's ethical argument against relying on engineered decoys, it should be independently verified against the primary 2014 replication studies themselves (rather than this secondary summary) before being treated as fully settled in staff-facing training materials.

**6. The three genuine reasons for showing multiple price points and the genuine-option verifiability test.**
Status: This is this module's own original ethical framework, directly extending Module 3's previously-established and independently fact-checked verifiability-test logic (see C10-M03 factcheck) to the pricing-presentation context. Not a new externally-sourced claim requiring independent verification here.

**7. Worked scenario and practice exercise.**
