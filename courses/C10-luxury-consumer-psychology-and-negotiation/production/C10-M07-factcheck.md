# C10-M07 Fact-Check: Cross-Cultural Luxury Buying: Status Signaling Across Markets

## Verification Status: Foundational cross-cultural motivation research and face-consumption research grounded in academic sources; specific gifting knowledge (numbers, colors, etiquette) well-corroborated across four independent (though general, non-jewelry-specific) etiquette sources

## Claims Reviewed

**1. Basic luxury-consumption motivational drivers are cross-culturally generalizable; variation occurs in individual perception and cultural expression rather than the existence of the drivers themselves.**
Status: Sourced directly from the Wiley-published cross-cultural consumer research article "What is the Value of Luxury? A Cross-Cultural Consumer Perspective," which explicitly states this conclusion ("the basic motivational drivers of luxury consumption are generalizable; only individual consumer perceptions differ"). This is a credible, directly on-point academic source.

**2. Face consumption (three subdimensions: conformity, distinctiveness, other-orientation) and specific Chinese-vs-American comparative findings (Chinese consumers more reference-group-influenced, more likely to relate brand/price to face, more likely to weigh prestige through others' perception).**
Status: Sourced from an NYU Research Guide on "Business in Contemporary China: Luxury Goods," which summarizes a cross-cultural survey study explicitly comparing Chinese and American consumers on these specific dimensions. This traces to foundational academic literature including Wong and Ahuvia's 1998 study, "Personal taste and family face: Luxury consumption in Confucian and Western societies" (independently referenced in a German National Library-hosted academic content-analysis paper consulted in this research pass), giving this claim a credible academic lineage across two independent sources.

**3. The collectivism/long-term-orientation pattern (status consumption stronger in collectivistic, long-term-oriented cultures; hedonistic consumption relatively stronger in lower-power-distance, shorter-term-oriented cultures).**
Status: Sourced from a Concordia University master's thesis (Song, MSc, 2020, via spectrum.library.concordia.ca), an academic source explicitly applying Hofstede-style cultural-dimension research to luxury purchase intention across cultures. This is a single academic source within this research pass, though it draws on well-established Hofstede cultural-dimensions literature more broadly.

**4. Specific numbers/colors/etiquette gifting knowledge (four as inauspicious in China/Japan/Korea; eight as lucky in China; thirteen as inauspicious in the West; red as celebratory across most of Asia; white as mourning-associated across much of East Asia/South Asia/parts of Eastern Europe; right-hand-only etiquette in the Gulf and India; gift-value-calibration social-pressure concerns).**
Status: CONFIRMED CONSISTENT across multiple independent gifting-etiquette sources: prommm.com's "Gifting, Numbers & Colors: Cultural Minefields" country-by-country reference, mygiftsinventory.com's "Cross-Cultural Gift Giving Etiquette Guide," voyrix.com's "Gift-Giving Customs Around the World," and culturedwanderer.com's "Gift Giving Rules by Culture Around the World." These are general cross-cultural gifting/etiquette sources, not jewelry-specific, but the specific number/color/etiquette facts are independently and consistently corroborated across all four sources, giving this claim solid cross-source support despite the sources' general (non-jewelry) nature.

**5. The caution against applying cultural generalizations as a rigid script overriding individual client signals.**
Status: This is this module's own direct extension of Module 2's already-established and independently fact-checked individual-listening principle (see C10-M02 factcheck), not a new externally-sourced claim requiring independent verification here.

**6. Worked scenario and practice exercise.**
Status: ORIGINAL illustrative synthesis created for this module, not drawn from any external source.
