# C10-M01 Fact-Check: Why People Really Buy Luxury: Status, Identity, and Emotion Over Utility

## Verification Status: Emotion-motivates/reason-justifies pattern and personal-vs-social motivation distinction well-corroborated across multiple academic and trade sources; self-esteem-compensation nuance rests on a single (peer-reviewed) academic source not yet cross-corroborated in this research pass

## Claims Reviewed

**1. The two-stage purchase pattern: emotional factors provide initial motivation, rational factors function afterward as justification.**
Status: CONFIRMED CONSISTENT across multiple independent sources: the IJFMR academic paper "The Psychology Behind Purchasing Luxury Goods" (describing emotional factors symbolizing "personal success, social status, and self-identity" alongside rational factors like quality/durability/investment value), stylerave.com's "Premium Purchases Psychology" article (explicitly: "Emotional factors... provide the initial motivation to consider a luxury purchase. Rational factors... then help justify the decision"), and Psychology Today's "The Emotions of Luxury" by consumer psychologist Kit Yarrow ("the decisions to purchase a luxury product are overwhelmingly emotional"). This is a well-corroborated pattern spanning an academic paper, a consumer-psychology-expert source, and trade press.

**2. Personal-oriented vs. social-oriented motivation categories; Veblen's conspicuous consumption theory as the basis for social-oriented/status-signaling motivation.**
Status: Sourced from multiple peer-reviewed and academic sources: the PMC article "Going (in)conspicuous: antecedents and moderators of luxury consumption" (explicitly citing Veblen 1899/1992 on status-signaling via visible expensive goods to approach a higher social hierarchical position), the Wiley meta-analysis "Luxury value perceptions and consumer outcomes" (distinguishing status consumption, per Eastman et al. 1999, as purchasing to "experience and showcase prestige for personal and social endeavors"), and the City University London open-access paper "Explaining Variation in Luxury Consumption." This is strong, multi-source academic corroboration of a well-established consumer-behavior distinction.

**3. Self-completion theory (acquiring/displaying identity-associated symbols to achieve a complete sense of self in a chosen area).**
Status: Sourced directly from the City University London open-access academic paper, which explicitly introduces "a theory of self-completion" and describes "a basic drive... to achieve a complete identity in a chosen area, by means of displaying material prestige symbols associated with the desired identity." This traces to the established academic self-completion theory literature (Wicklund & Gollwitzer) as cited within that paper.

**4. Status-seeking as compensation for low social self-esteem, rather than a straightforward expression of confidence.**
Status: Sourced directly from the PMC peer-reviewed article "Social class, social self-esteem, and conspicuous consumption," which explicitly reports that "individuals in a high social class become conducive to conspicuous consumption when their social self-esteem is low" as a compensatory, self-presentational strategy. IMPORTANT SOURCING NOTE: this specific nuance rests on a single peer-reviewed academic source within this research pass; while credible (peer-reviewed, published in PMC), it has not been independently cross-corroborated by a second source here, and should be understood as a genuine but singly-sourced research finding rather than a broadly established consensus claim.
