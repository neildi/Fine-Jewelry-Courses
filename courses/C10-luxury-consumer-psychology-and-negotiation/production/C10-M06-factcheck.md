# C10-M06 Fact-Check: Serving High-Net-Worth Clients: Discretion, Privacy, and Status Psychology

## Verification Status: Appearance-bias mechanism grounded in a peer-reviewed multi-study academic paper; unconscious-bias evidence drawn from a large-scale, rigorous national retail study (used carefully and not conflated with wealth-signal bias specifically); quiet-luxury psychology grounded in named private-wealth-industry sources

## Claims Reviewed

**1. The appearance-bias mechanism (salespeople attribute higher purchase budgets and more attentive service to clients with visible status signals, based on readily accessible appearance cues).**
Status: Sourced directly from a peer-reviewed French academic study, "Salespersons' responses to status signals and stereotypes of luxury..." (hal.parisnanterre.fr), which used three complementary studies (in-situ observation, a survey among luxury car clients, and a lab experiment among apprentice salespeople) to establish this mechanism, explicitly grounding it in interpersonal orientation theory and "expectancy-based" stereotyping. This is a methodologically serious, multi-study academic source directly on point.

**2. The claim that appearance-based service differentiation in retail often operates below conscious awareness, evidenced by a gap between staff self-reported intentions and shopper-perceived experience.**
Status: Sourced from the large-scale, first-of-its-kind national Racial Bias in Retail Study commissioned by Sephora (newsroom.sephora.com), which specifically found that 60% of retail employees surveyed cited behavioral attributes rather than physical attributes as their basis for interactions, while shoppers (particularly BIPOC shoppers) overwhelmingly reported experiencing appearance-driven treatment. IMPORTANT CLARIFICATION: this module uses the Sephora study specifically as evidence for the general point that appearance-based service differentiation is a real, well-documented, and often unconsciously-operating industry problem — it does NOT conflate racial bias with wealth-appearance bias as identical phenomena. The study is cited for its methodological demonstration of the intention/perception gap, which is structurally relevant to (though a distinct phenomenon from) the wealth-signal bias described in item 1.

**3. Quiet luxury / stealth wealth psychology (security concerns, avoiding social awkwardness, a sense of custodianship rather than ownership as real motivations for HNW individuals avoiding visible wealth markers).**
Status: Sourced from Fortune's "There's something everyone can learn from the 'quiet luxury' trend" article, which quotes named wealth-management professionals directly (David Sadkin, president of Bel Air Investment Advisors; Carrie Galloway, global head of Advice Lab for J.P. Morgan Private Bank) on these specific motivations, making this a credible, named-source, private-wealth-industry-grounded claim rather than a generic trend-piece assertion.

**4. The old-money/new-money status-signaling distinction (established wealth having reduced need to signal status broadly; signaling shifting toward subtle, peer-legible markers among experienced luxury consumers).**
Status: Sourced from EHL Insights' "Quiet Luxury: The Art of Understated Wealth" article (EHL being an established hospitality-management academic institution), which explicitly cites the "patricians vs. parvenus" distinction from luxury consumption literature.

**5. The quiet-luxury vs. loud-luxury HNWI buyer-type distinction.**
Status: Sourced from 47hnwi.com's "Quiet luxury vs loud luxury: two opposite HNWI buyers" article, a source specifically focused on high-net-worth-individual buyer psychology and market segmentation.

**6. Concrete HNW/UHNW discretion and privacy service practices (confidentiality, no unsolicited commentary, discreet space, careful handling of travel/schedule information).**
Status: Synthesized from Beaumont Etiquette's luxury-retail-etiquette-training content, questsearch.co.uk's "Hiring for High-Net-Worth Clientele" article, and Fortune's article (specifically regarding the travel/security rationale). These are general private-client-service industry sources, cross-consistent with each other on the core practices described, though none is a rigorous academic source specifically for these operational practices.

**7. The connection of quiet-luxury/loud-luxury buyer types back to Modules 1-2's motivational framework.**
Status: This module's own direct application of previously-established and independently fact-checked C10 content (see C10-M01 and C10-M02 factchecks), not a new externally-sourced claim.

**8. Worked scenario and practice exercise.**
Status: ORIGINAL illustrative synthesis created for this module, not drawn from any external source.
