# C10-M07 Outline: Cross-Cultural Luxury Buying: Status Signaling Across Markets

## Module Metadata
- Course: C10 - Luxury Consumer Psychology & Negotiation
- Module: 7 of 8
- Persona: P2
- Working title: Cross-Cultural Luxury Buying: Status Signaling Across Markets
- Learning objective: Associates can explain that core luxury motivations are cross-culturally generalizable while their expression varies, explain face consumption as a culturally specific variant of social-oriented motivation, apply specific number/color/etiquette knowledge relevant to jewelry gifting across major cultural contexts, and use this knowledge as background awareness rather than a rigid script that overrides individual client signals.
- Terminal outcome tie-in: "Navigate cross-cultural differences in luxury buying and status signaling."

## Section Map

1. **Why This Module Matters** — Frames cross-cultural competence as building directly on, not replacing, Modules 1-2's motivational framework.
2. **The Core Motivations Are Universal; the Expression Isn't** — Foundational cross-cultural luxury consumption research establishing generalizable motivational drivers with culturally variable expression.
3. **Face Consumption: A Specific Cultural Variant of Social-Oriented Motivation** — Face consumption's three subdimensions; Chinese-American comparative research; the collectivism/individualism and long-term/short-term orientation patterns.
4. **Gifting-Specific Cultural Knowledge: Numbers, Colors, and Etiquette** — Concrete, well-documented associations (four/eight in East Asia, red/white across cultures, right-hand etiquette, gift-value calibration).
5. **Applying This Without Stereotyping Any Individual Client** — The explicit caution against rigid scripts; individual client signals (Module 2) take precedence.
6. **A Worked Scenario: The Gift Box That Almost Went Wrong** — Illustrative synthesis showing a specific, avoidable cultural misstep caught and corrected.
7. **Practice Exercise** — Reviewing store packaging/presentation practices against the module's cultural knowledge.
8. **Key Takeaways** — Five-point recap.

## Production Notes
- The foundational claim that basic luxury-consumption motivational drivers are cross-culturally generalizable, with variation occurring in individual perception and cultural expression rather than the existence of the drivers themselves, is sourced directly from the Wiley-published cross-cultural consumer research article "What is the Value of Luxury? A Cross-Cultural Consumer Perspective," which explicitly states this conclusion.
- Face consumption (three subdimensions: conformity, distinctiveness, other-orientation) and the specific Chinese-vs-American comparative findings (Chinese consumers more reference-group-influenced, more likely to relate brand/price to face, more likely to weigh prestige through others' perception) are sourced from an NYU Research Guide on "Business in Contemporary China: Luxury Goods," which summarizes a cross-cultural survey study explicitly comparing Chinese and American consumers on these specific dimensions, tracing to foundational academic work including Wong and Ahuvia's 1998 study on "Personal taste and family face: Luxury consumption in Confucian and Western societies" (cited in a related German National Library-hosted academic paper).
- The collectivism/long-term-orientation and status-consumption-strength pattern, and the corresponding hedonistic-consumption/lower-power-distance pattern, are sourced from a Concordia University master's thesis (Song, MSc, 2020, via spectrum.library.concordia.ca), an academic source explicitly applying Hofstede-style cultural-dimension research to luxury purchase intention across cultures.
- The specific numbers/colors/etiquette gifting knowledge (four as inauspicious in China/Japan/Korea; eight as lucky in Chinese culture; thirteen as inauspicious in Western contexts; red as celebratory across most of Asia vs. more mixed Western associations; white as mourning-associated across much of East Asia, South Asia, and parts of Eastern Europe vs. purity/bridal associations in the West; right-hand-only passing/receiving in the Gulf and India; gift-value-calibration concerns about creating social pressure) is CONFIRMED CONSISTENT across multiple independent gifting-etiquette sources: prommm.com's "Gifting, Numbers & Colors: Cultural Minefields" country-by-country reference, mygiftsinventory.com's "Cross-Cultural Gift Giving Etiquette Guide," voyrix.com's "Gift-Giving Customs Around the World," and culturedwanderer.com's "Gift Giving Rules by Culture Around the World." These are general cross-cultural gifting/etiquette sources (not jewelry-specific), but the specific number/color/etiquette facts are independently and consistently corroborated across all four sources.
- The explicit caution against applying cultural generalizations as a rigid script overriding individual client signals is this module's own direct extension of Module 2's already-established and independently fact-checked individual-listening principle, not a new externally-sourced claim.
- The worked scenario and practice exercise are ORIGINAL illustrative syntheses, not externally sourced cases.
