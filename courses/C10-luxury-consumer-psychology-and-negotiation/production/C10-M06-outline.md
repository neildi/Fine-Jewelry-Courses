# C10-M06 Outline: Serving High-Net-Worth Clients: Discretion, Privacy, and Status Psychology

## Module Metadata
- Course: C10 - Luxury Consumer Psychology & Negotiation
- Module: 6 of 8
- Persona: P2
- Working title: Serving High-Net-Worth Clients: Discretion, Privacy, and Status Psychology
- Learning objective: Associates can explain the documented appearance-bias mechanism in retail sales behavior, explain quiet luxury/stealth wealth psychology and why it makes appearance an unreliable signal specifically for HNW clients, distinguish quiet-luxury from loud-luxury client expectations, and apply consistent discretion/privacy practices to every client regardless of appearance.
- Terminal outcome tie-in: "Serve high-net-worth clients with appropriate discretion, privacy awareness, and sensitivity to the psychology of status-driven behavior."

## Section Map

1. **Why This Module Matters** — Frames appearance-based judgment as the single most consequential, often-unconscious mistake in HNW client service.
2. **The Appearance-Bias Problem Is Real and Measured** — Academic research on status-signal-driven service differentiation; the Sephora racial-bias study's self-report/perception gap as evidence the bias often operates unconsciously.
3. **Quiet Luxury: Why Genuine Wealth Often Looks Unremarkable** — Stealth-wealth psychology (security, social awkwardness, custodianship); old-money vs. new-money signaling research.
4. **Two Different Kinds of Wealthy Client, Two Different Expectations** — Quiet-luxury vs. loud-luxury buyer psychology; connection to Modules 1-2's motivational framework.
5. **What Discretion and Privacy Actually Require in Practice** — Concrete HNW/UHNW service practices (confidentiality, no unsolicited commentary, discreet space, careful handling of travel/schedule information).
6. **A Worked Scenario: The Quiet Question That Almost Got Missed** — Illustrative synthesis showing appearance-based assumption nearly costing a major relationship.
7. **Practice Exercise** — Discussing a real/hypothetical appearance-bias instance and practicing behavior-based attention criteria.
8. **Key Takeaways** — Five-point recap.

## Production Notes
- The appearance-bias mechanism in retail sales (salespeople attributing higher purchase budgets and providing more attentive service to clients with visible status signals, based on readily accessible appearance cues) is sourced directly from a peer-reviewed French academic study, "Salespersons' responses to status signals and stereotypes of luxury..." (hal.parisnanterre.fr), which used three complementary studies (in-situ observation, a survey among luxury car clients, and a lab experiment among apprentice salespeople) to establish this mechanism, explicitly framing it via interpersonal orientation theory and "expectancy-based" stereotyping.
- The claim that this kind of appearance-based bias often operates below conscious awareness, with a gap between self-reported staff intentions and shoppers' actual perceived experience, is sourced from the large-scale, first-of-its-kind national Racial Bias in Retail Study commissioned by Sephora (newsroom.sephora.com), which specifically found that "60% of retail employees surveyed cited behavioral attributes rather than physical attributes" as their basis for interactions, while shoppers (especially BIPOC shoppers) overwhelmingly reported experiencing appearance-driven treatment. This is a rigorous, large-scale, methodologically serious retail-industry study, used here specifically as evidence that appearance-bias in retail service is a real, well-documented, and often unconscious phenomenon (distinct from, though structurally related to, the status-signal bias described in item 1) — this module does not conflate racial bias and wealth-appearance bias as identical phenomena, but uses the Sephora study specifically to support the general point that appearance-based service differentiation is a documented industry problem operating partly below conscious awareness.
- Quiet luxury / stealth wealth psychology (security concerns, avoiding social awkwardness, a sense of custodianship rather than ownership) is sourced from Fortune's "There's something everyone can learn from the 'quiet luxury' trend" article, which quotes named wealth-management professionals (David Sadkin of Bel Air Investment Advisors; Carrie Galloway, global head of Advice Lab for J.P. Morgan Private Bank) directly on these specific motivations, making this a credible, named-source, private-wealth-industry-grounded claim.
- The old-money/new-money status-signaling distinction (established wealth having reduced need to signal status; status signaling among experienced luxury consumers shifting toward subtle, peer-legible markers) is sourced from EHL Insights' "Quiet Luxury: The Art of Understated Wealth" article, an academic-hospitality-institution source explicitly citing the "patricians vs. parvenus" distinction from luxury consumption literature.
- The quiet-luxury vs. loud-luxury buyer-type distinction is sourced from 47hnwi.com's "Quiet luxury vs loud luxury: two opposite HNWI buyers" article, a source specifically focused on high-net-worth-individual buyer psychology.
- The concrete HNW/UHNW discretion and privacy service practices (confidentiality, no unsolicited commentary, discreet space, careful handling of travel/schedule information) are synthesized from Beaumont Etiquette's luxury-retail-etiquette-training content, questsearch.co.uk's "Hiring for High-Net-Worth Clientele" article, and Fortune's article (specifically regarding the travel/security rationale for information discretion). These are general private-client-service industry sources, cross-consistent with each other on the core practices described.
- The connection of quiet-luxury/loud-luxury buyer types back to Modules 1-2's personal-vs-social motivational framework is this module's own direct application of previously-established and independently fact-checked C10 content, not a new externally-sourced claim.
- The worked scenario and practice exercise are ORIGINAL illustrative syntheses, not externally sourced cases.
