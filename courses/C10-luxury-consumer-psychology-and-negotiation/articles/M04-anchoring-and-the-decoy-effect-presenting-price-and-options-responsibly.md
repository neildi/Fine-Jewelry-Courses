# Anchoring and the Decoy Effect: Presenting Price and Options Responsibly

## Why This Module Matters

How a price is presented changes how it's perceived, independent of whether the underlying value changed at all. Two of the most famous findings in this area — anchoring and the decoy effect — are genuinely well-documented psychological phenomena, and they are also easy to misuse in ways that manipulate rather than inform. This module covers what these effects actually are, what the evidence genuinely supports (including an important caveat about one of them that most sales training doesn't mention), and how to use price presentation responsibly rather than as a trick.

## Anchoring: The First Number Sets the Frame

Anchoring, first documented by Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman in their foundational 1974 research on judgment under uncertainty, describes a robust and well-replicated cognitive bias: people rely heavily on the first piece of information they receive about something — the "anchor" — and subsequent judgments get evaluated relative to that anchor, even when the anchor itself is arbitrary or irrelevant. In a pricing context, the first number a client sees becomes their mental reference point, and everything shown afterward gets evaluated as more or less expensive relative to that reference point, not in some absolute sense. Luxury retail applies this directly and openly: showing an ultra-high-price piece first can make everything shown afterward feel more reasonable by comparison, purely as a function of sequencing, independent of any change in the actual pieces or their value.

The ethical distinction here is about sequencing intent, not about avoiding anchoring altogether — anchoring happens whether an associate intends it or not, since the first number shown always becomes a reference point. The honest version of this is a genuine range presentation: showing a client the real breadth of what's actually available (including honestly higher and lower options) so they can locate their own comfort zone within real choices, rather than deliberately opening with an inflated, rarely-actually-sold price specifically engineered to make a target price look artificially reasonable by comparison.

## The Decoy Effect: A Famous Finding With a Real Asterisk

The decoy effect (also called the asymmetric dominance effect), first systematically documented by Huber, Payne, and Puto in 1982, describes how adding a third, deliberately inferior option to a choice between two alternatives can shift preference toward one of the original two — the decoy is "asymmetrically dominated," meaning it's worse than the target option on every relevant attribute, but only worse than the competing option on some attributes. This is one of the most frequently cited findings in pricing psychology and behavioral economics writing.

Here is the part that matters for using it responsibly, and that most sales training conveniently leaves out: two large, well-designed replication efforts in 2014 found that the decoy effect largely disappeared once researchers moved from abstract, artificial laboratory choices to more realistic purchasing scenarios. The effect is genuinely real and reliably replicated in the original, narrow lab paradigm (abstract two-attribute numeric choices); it is considerably less reliable and less well-supported as something that reliably steers real purchasing decisions with real products and real prices. This matters directly for this module's ethical stance: deliberately engineering a "decoy" tier specifically to manipulate a client toward a target purchase is relying on a psychological effect whose real-world robustness is genuinely contested, not a guaranteed lever — on top of the more basic ethical problem, discussed below, of deliberately including an option no one is meant to actually choose.

## Three Genuine Reasons to Show Multiple Options (Distinct From Engineering a Decoy)

Presenting several pieces or price points to a client is not, by itself, manipulative — it becomes manipulative specifically when one option is included purely to make another look better, with no genuine intention that anyone ever chooses it. There are legitimate, non-manipulative reasons to show a range:

- **Genuine choice across a real range** that reflects the actual breadth of what's available, letting a client find where their own preferences and budget genuinely land.
- **Comparison that provides real information** — showing a client two similar pieces at different price points because the price difference reflects a real quality or design difference the client should know about, not because one is a fake decoy.
- **Letting a client self-select their own comfort level** without an associate guessing at it, since a genuine range respects that different clients on different budgets are equally legitimate, rather than funneling everyone toward one predetermined target.

## The Ethical Line for Both Effects

The same verifiability-style test from Module 3 applies here, adapted for pricing presentation specifically: is every option shown something a client could genuinely choose and be well-served by, or does the presentation include an option that exists purely to manipulate perception of another? Anchoring is unavoidable and ethically neutral in itself — the question is whether the anchor reflects something real (a genuinely available, genuinely comparable piece) or something artificially inflated purely to create a contrast effect. A deliberately-included decoy that nobody is meant to actually buy fails this test regardless of how well the underlying psychological effect actually replicates in real-world settings.

## A Worked Scenario: Two Ways to Show Three Rings

A client is deciding on an anniversary ring, and an associate has three real options to show: a $4,200 piece, a $6,800 piece, and a $9,500 piece, each genuinely available and each representing an honest step up in stone size and craftsmanship. Presenting all three, explaining honestly what changes at each price point, and letting the client choose based on real preferences and budget is a legitimate use of range and anchoring — the client's own sense of what's reasonable will naturally be shaped by seeing the full range, but every option shown is real and genuinely purchasable. A different approach would be adding a fourth "option" — a poorly-designed $7,900 piece with a smaller stone than the $6,800 piece, included purely to make the $6,800 piece look like the obvious smart choice by comparison, with no real intention that any client would ever actually buy the $7,900 piece. The first approach uses real anchoring across real choices; the second manufactures a decoy specifically to manipulate, and does so relying on a psychological effect whose robustness with real products is genuinely contested.

## Practice Exercise

Review three actual price points currently available in your store within a single client's likely consideration set. Practice presenting all three honestly, describing what real difference justifies the price gap at each step, and let a colleague playing the client ask you directly why one piece costs more than another — practice having a genuine, specific answer ready rather than a vague deflection.

## Key Takeaways

- Anchoring is a robust, well-documented cognitive bias (Tversky and Kahneman's foundational 1974 research) in which the first number a client sees becomes their reference point for everything shown afterward; it happens regardless of intent, so the ethical question is whether the anchor itself reflects something real.
- The decoy effect is a famous, frequently cited finding, but two large 2014 replication efforts found it largely disappeared outside the narrow, abstract laboratory paradigm where it was first documented — a genuinely important caveat missing from most popular sales-training content on this effect.
- Presenting multiple real options across an honest price range is a legitimate, non-manipulative practice; it becomes manipulative specifically when an option is included purely to make another look better, with no genuine intention that anyone would choose it.
- The ethical test for any pricing presentation: is every option shown something a client could genuinely choose and be well-served by, or does the presentation include an artificial element purely to manipulate perception?
- Being ready to explain, specifically and honestly, what real difference justifies a price gap between two pieces is the practical skill this module builds toward, and it protects against both the ethical risk and the practical risk of relying on a psychological effect that may not reliably work the way popular sales training claims it does.
