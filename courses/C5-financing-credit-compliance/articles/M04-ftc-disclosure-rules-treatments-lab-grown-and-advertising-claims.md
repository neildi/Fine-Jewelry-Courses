# FTC Disclosure Rules: Treatments, Lab-Grown, and Advertising Claims

## Why This Module Matters

Everything covered so far in this course governs how a customer pays. This module covers something different but equally important: what you're legally allowed to say about the piece itself while you're selling it — especially when that piece is being financed, since a financed sale often comes with a longer relationship and more room for a claim to be scrutinized later than a same-day cash sale. Getting product claims wrong isn't just a sales-ethics issue; it can trigger real regulatory exposure under the FTC's rules governing jewelry advertising and disclosure.

## The FTC Jewelry Guides: The Regulatory Backbone

The Federal Trade Commission maintains guidance specifically governing how jewelry, precious metals, and gemstones can be described, marketed, and sold — commonly referred to in the trade as the FTC Jewelry Guides. The Jewelers Vigilance Committee (JVC), the trade organization that specializes in legal compliance for the jewelry industry, describes its own guidance on "Understanding the FTC Guidelines" as covering fair product representation, disclosure of treatments and lab-grown status, and advertising claims generally — explicitly framing this as "the legal frame around what you can promise a financed client." JVC's broader Essential Guides series also addresses memo/consignment transactions, UCC-1 filings, platinum marking law, and irradiated gemstone disclosure as related compliance topics.

## Treatment Disclosure: Say What Was Done to the Stone

Many gemstones sold in retail jewelry have been treated in some way to improve their color, clarity, or durability — heat treatment, irradiation, fracture filling, and other processes are common and legitimate parts of the trade. The core disclosure principle is straightforward: if a stone has been treated, that treatment must be disclosed to the customer, not left for them to discover later or assume doesn't apply to their specific piece. This isn't about treated stones being lesser — many treatments are standard, accepted trade practice — it's about the customer's right to know what they're actually buying before they decide whether the price and the piece match their expectations.

## Natural vs. Lab-Grown: A Distinction That Must Be Stated, Not Implied

The distinction between a natural and a lab-grown (sometimes called "cultured" or "created") diamond or gemstone must be clearly and affirmatively disclosed — not left ambiguous through vague language, and not something a customer is expected to infer from price alone. A lab-grown diamond is a real diamond with genuine gemological properties, and it can be an entirely appropriate, honestly-marketed product — but calling it simply "a diamond" without qualification, when the customer reasonably understands "diamond" to mean natural unless stated otherwise, risks creating a false impression even without an explicit false statement.

This matters especially in a financing context: a customer who finances a piece over 12-24 months is making a longer-term financial commitment based partly on their understanding of what they're buying. If that understanding turns out to be based on an unclear or misleading description, the dispute doesn't just damage trust — it can escalate into a chargeback, a complaint to the financing partner, or a regulatory complaint, any of which is a far worse outcome than a slightly less impressive-sounding but fully accurate description at the time of sale.

## Advertising Claims: Don't Promise What You Can't Support

The same disclosure discipline extends to advertising and marketing claims generally — anything printed, posted, or said about a piece needs to be something you can actually support, not aspirational language that oversells what the piece or the deal actually is. This includes claims about value ("this is worth double what you're paying"), claims about quality grading, and claims about savings or promotional pricing. If you can't point to a specific, verifiable basis for a claim, don't make it — and if you're not sure whether a specific claim is supportable, that's exactly the kind of question to bring to a manager before repeating it to a customer, rather than assuming it's fine because you've heard other associates say it.

## Why This Connects Back to Financing

This module sits in a financing-focused course specifically because the stakes of a disclosure problem compound when a sale is financed rather than paid in full upfront. A cash sale that goes wrong is a single bad transaction; a financed sale that goes wrong is a bad transaction plus an ongoing credit relationship, plus a financing partner who may now be fielding a customer complaint about a product claim the store made, not the financing partner. Treat accurate disclosure as part of what makes a financed sale sustainable, not a separate compliance topic bolted onto the sales conversation.

## A Worked Scenario: The Ambiguous Lab-Grown Description

A customer is looking at a $3,500 solitaire ring and asks, "So this is a real diamond?" The stone is lab-grown.

**Incorrect (technically true but misleading by omission):** "Yes, it's a real diamond — same hardness, same sparkle as anything else in the case."

**Correct (accurate and complete):** "Yes, it's a genuine diamond — lab-grown rather than mined, which is why it's priced where it is. It has the same chemical and physical properties as a natural diamond; the difference is entirely in where and how it formed. I want to make sure that's clear since it's an important part of what you're deciding between."

The second version is both more honest and, in most cases, a better sales conversation — it gives the customer a real reason for the price point rather than leaving them to wonder later why a "diamond" cost less than they expected, which is exactly the kind of gap that turns into a complaint months into a financing term.

## Practice Exercise

Think of how you currently describe treated stones or lab-grown pieces in your own sales conversations. Say your typical explanation out loud. Does it clearly and affirmatively state the treatment or origin, or does it rely on the customer not asking a follow-up question? If you're not confident your current phrasing would hold up under scrutiny, revise it using the correct-version structure above: state the fact plainly, then explain why it matters to their decision.

## Key Takeaways

- The FTC Jewelry Guides govern fair product representation, treatment disclosure, and advertising claims — described by JVC as "the legal frame around what you can promise a financed client."
- Any gemstone treatment (heat, irradiation, fracture filling, etc.) must be disclosed to the customer, not left for them to discover or assume doesn't apply.
- Natural vs. lab-grown status must be clearly and affirmatively stated, not left ambiguous or implied through price or vague language alone.
- Advertising and verbal claims about value, quality, or savings must be genuinely supportable — if you can't point to a specific basis for a claim, don't make it, and ask a manager if you're unsure.
- Disclosure problems compound in a financed sale, since a longer credit relationship gives more time and more channels (chargebacks, financing-partner complaints) for an inaccurate claim to surface and cause real damage.
