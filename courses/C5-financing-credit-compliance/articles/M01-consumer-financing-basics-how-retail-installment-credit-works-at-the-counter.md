# Consumer Financing Basics: How Retail Installment Credit Works at the Counter

## Why This Module Matters

Financing is one of the most common tools you'll use to make a high-ticket piece achievable for a client who can't or doesn't want to pay the full price at once. But financing is also a regulated consumer-credit product, not just a sales feature — get the mechanics wrong, and you create real compliance and customer-experience problems, not just an awkward conversation. This module covers what retail installment credit actually is, how it differs from a straightforward credit-card sale, and the specific rules that govern how and when you can charge a financed client's account.

## What a Retail Installment Contract Actually Is

The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB) defines a retail installment sales contract (sometimes called a retail installment agreement) as a credit agreement in which a buyer purchases goods and agrees to pay for them over time in installments, with the seller (or a financing partner acting on the seller's behalf) extending the credit. This is distinct from a general-purpose credit card in an important way: a retail installment contract is typically tied to a specific purchase and a specific merchant program, rather than being a revolving line the customer can use anywhere.

In jewelry retail, this usually takes the form of a store-branded or partner-branded financing program — a "Jewelry Credit Card" or similar product issued through a financing partner (Synchrony is one commonly used example in this space) rather than the store extending credit directly itself.

## The Application Flow

A typical financing application at the point of sale follows a defined flow: the associate offers financing as an option, the consumer completes an application through one of several possible channels — an online platform, a link on the retailer's website or social media, or a paperless in-store "Direct to Device" option completed right at the counter. Regardless of which channel is used, one requirement applies universally: the credit agreement must be in the consumer's hands before the sale proceeds. This isn't a formality — a customer needs to actually see and have access to the terms they're agreeing to before you complete the transaction, not receive them afterward as a follow-up.

## Two Compliance Rules That Matter at the Register

Beyond the general application flow, two specific rules govern what you can actually charge to a financed account, and getting these wrong is one of the most common register-level compliance mistakes:

1. **You can only charge for products and services that the customer is receiving at the time of sale.** If the customer is walking out with the piece today, charging the account today is correct. If they're not receiving the item today, this rule is where the second rule comes in.

2. **Future delivery must be transacted using Authorization Only and Forced Purchase processes.** This is the specific mechanism used for custom orders, special orders, and anything where the customer won't receive the physical item on the day of the transaction. An "Authorization Only" holds the approved credit amount without charging it immediately; a "Forced Purchase" is used to actually complete the charge once the item is ready and delivered. Skipping this two-step process and simply charging the full amount at the time of a custom-order deposit, before the customer has anything in hand, is exactly the kind of mistake this rule is designed to prevent.

## Why This Distinction Matters Beyond Compliance

This isn't only a regulatory technicality — it protects the customer and the store in a very practical way. If a custom order is charged in full immediately and something goes wrong in production (a delay, a design issue, a piece that needs to be remade), the customer has already paid for something they don't yet have, and unwinding that charge creates friction, a refund process, and a worse customer experience than if the charge had never been forced through prematurely. Following the Authorization Only → Forced Purchase sequence keeps the charge timeline matched to when the customer actually receives value.

## What Reporting Looks Like on the Retailer Side

Financing partners typically provide retailer-facing reporting that associates and managers should know exists, even if they don't personally run these reports daily: an Application Summary, a Business Dashboard summarizing application and credit-limit approvals, an Authorizations Report, a Credit Card Transaction Report used for reconciliation, a Daily Funding Report, a Monthly Statement, and a customer list showing existing customers' available credit. Associates are commonly expected to process sales, returns, layaways, and financing applications accurately at the point of sale, and to verify credit information for prospective customers as part of the standard sales workflow — this is a stated duty in real job postings, not an optional add-on skill.

## An Important Scope Note: Luxury Maisons Often Skip Financing Entirely

It's worth knowing that consumer financing isn't universal across the fine jewelry industry. Luxury maison postings (from brands like Cartier, Tiffany, Van Cleef & Arpels, and Bulgari) generally do not mention financing or credit at all in their associate job descriptions. This reflects a genuine segment difference: chain and independent retailers commonly rely on financing as a core sales tool, while ultra-luxury maisons more often expect outright purchase or their own house-specific payment arrangements. If you move between segments of the industry, don't assume the financing skills covered in this module transfer identically everywhere — confirm what payment tools your specific employer actually offers.

## A Worked Scenario: The Custom Engagement Ring Deposit

A customer wants to finance a $6,000 custom engagement ring that will take four weeks to produce. They're approved for financing and want to put the charge through today so they don't have to think about payment again before the ring is ready.

The correct sequence: process an Authorization Only for the approved amount today — this holds the credit without charging it. The customer leaves with a clear record of what's been authorized, but no charge has actually posted yet. Four weeks later, when the ring is finished and the customer takes delivery, you process the Forced Purchase to complete the actual charge, matched to the moment they receive the piece.

Compare this to what would happen if you simply ran a full charge today instead: if the design needs to change, if a stone needs to be swapped, or if the customer needs to cancel for any reason before the ring is finished, you're now unwinding a completed charge on a financed account for a piece that was never delivered — a materially harder problem than simply canceling an authorization that was never converted to an actual purchase.

## Practice Exercise

Think through the last few financed sales you've been part of, or imagine three common scenarios your store handles: an in-stock ring bought and worn out same-day, a custom order with a multi-week production timeline, and a special order where an existing piece is being sourced from another location. For each, identify which of the two compliance rules applies and what the correct charge sequence should be. If you're not confident about any of the three, that's a good discussion topic for your next team meeting.

## Key Takeaways

- A retail installment contract is credit tied to a specific purchase through a merchant financing program, not a general-purpose revolving credit line — and the credit agreement must be in the customer's hands before the sale proceeds.
- You can only charge a financed account for what the customer receives at the time of sale; anything delivered later (custom orders, special orders) must go through Authorization Only, then Forced Purchase once the item is actually ready.
- Skipping the two-step future-delivery process and charging in full immediately creates real risk if something goes wrong before delivery — the rule exists to protect both the customer's money and the store's exposure.
- Financing-partner reporting (application summaries, funding reports, credit-availability lookups) is a standard part of the retailer-side workflow, and processing financing accurately at the point of sale is a stated job duty at many employers.
- Financing isn't universal — luxury maisons often skip it entirely, so confirm what payment tools are actually available in your specific segment of the industry before assuming this module's tools apply everywhere.
