# C8-M08 Outline: Capstone: Managing the Repair and Custom Design Pipeline

## Module Metadata
- Course: C8 - Custom Design & Repair Workflow
- Module: 8 of 8 (Capstone)
- Persona: P3
- Working title: Capstone: Managing the Repair and Custom Design Pipeline
- Learning objective: Associates/managers can organize a mixed batch of simultaneous repair and custom jobs into a shared, visible pipeline structure, prioritize using multiple real factors (due date, blocking status, technician skill match, comeback precedence) rather than pure FCFS, plan capacity with appropriate buffer, and maintain proactive client communication at scale across the full pipeline.
- Terminal outcome tie-in: "Manage a full repair and custom-design pipeline across multiple simultaneous jobs at varying complexity and stage."

## Section Map

1. **Why This Module Matters** — Frames the capstone as bringing every prior single-job module together into multi-job pipeline management.
2. **The Pipeline as a Real Structure, Not a Pile** — Shared, visible pipeline stages (backlog/intake, scheduled, in progress with blocked sub-states, QC, ready for pickup).
3. **Prioritization: Not Just First-In, First-Out** — Due date, blocking status, complexity/skill match, comeback precedence.
4. **Capacity: Don't Schedule Every Available Hour** — 10-20% buffer guidance; daily check-in against emerging reality.
5. **Communication Across the Pipeline** — Extending Module 2's status-transparency commitment to pipeline scale.
6. **A Worked Scenario: A Full Bench on a Monday Morning** — Illustrative synthesis of a twelve-job morning triage applying every prior section's principles together.
7. **Practice Exercise** — The capstone assessment: sorting the worked scenario's job batch into stages, identifying blocked vs. queued jobs, drafting communications.
8. **Key Takeaways** — Five-point recap.

## Production Notes
- The specific pipeline-stage structure (intake/backlog → scheduled/to-do → in progress with blocked sub-states → QC → ready for pickup) is synthesized from several real jewelry-repair-tracking software product descriptions that independently describe highly similar stage structures: Shelvdon's explicit 4-stage Kanban pipeline (Backlog, To Do, In Progress, Done), PIRO Retail's jewelry repair software workflow (received, at the bench, waiting on parts, polishing, QC, ready), Gem Logic's repair-management workflow (drop-off, photograph/label, workshop progress board, done/notified, pickup), WJewel's repair-tracking buyer's guide (intake, approval, assign, track, QC/complete, pickup), and Jewelia's production-management feature page (intake, design/CAD, casting/assembly, setting/finishing, QC, delivery). This is a well-corroborated, product-market-confirmed pipeline structure specifically from jewelry-industry software vendors, not a generic adaptation.
- The "flag blocked jobs as a distinct state rather than repeatedly revisiting them" and general prioritization principles (weighing due date, blocking status, and skill match rather than pure FCFS) draw on general job-shop and repair-shop scheduling literature: a formal job-shop prioritization scheme (bottleneck identification plus FCFS for non-bottleneck operations) and applied repair-shop scheduling guidance from RedAppy's auto-repair-shop resource-allocation and scheduling-optimization articles and Heavy Duty Journal's shop-bay-management guide. IMPORTANT NOTE: the RedAppy and Heavy Duty Journal sources are written for the auto-repair industry, not jewelry repair specifically — the underlying scheduling/prioritization principles (bottleneck visibility, priority criteria beyond arrival order, comeback/priority categories) are domain-general shop-operations concepts and are presented in this module as reasoned adaptations to a jewelry bench context, not as jewelry-specific findings.
- The 10-20% capacity buffer guidance is sourced directly from Automotive Business Guide's repair-shop-downtime-reduction article ("if your real productivity averages 110%, you can plan around ~35 billable hours—not 45") and Mechanics.app's auto-repair-scheduling guide ("build in 10-15% buffer for unexpected issues"), both from the auto-repair-shop domain; presented here as a reasoned adaptation to jewelry bench capacity planning, with the same domain-adaptation caveat as above.
- The daily-check-in-against-reality practice is sourced from RedAppy's resource-allocation-optimization article's described daily cadence ("night before... morning huddle... live check-ins"), again from the auto-repair domain and adapted here.
- The pipeline-wide client-communication practice is this module's own direct extension of Module 2's already-established, previously fact-checked status-transparency commitment, combined with the explicit "send updates when work is approved, delayed, or ready" pattern independently confirmed in both PIRO Retail's and WJewel's jewelry-specific repair-software descriptions — this specific claim IS confirmed directly from jewelry-industry sources, unlike the general scheduling-principle claims above.
- The worked scenario and practice exercise are ORIGINAL illustrative syntheses, not externally sourced cases.

## Assessment Alignment
- This module's practice exercise IS the course's stated capstone assessment ("a capstone pipeline-management simulation across a mixed batch of jobs" per the C8 README), and should be scored by a manager against: correct stage assignment for every job, correct identification of genuinely blocked vs. simply queued jobs, sound prioritization reasoning (not pure FCFS), and appropriately drafted client communications for each status change.
- Quiz items should test: correctly distinguishing blocked from queued job states, applying multi-factor prioritization over pure FCFS, understanding why capacity buffer matters, and recognizing when a status change should trigger client communication.
- Huddle card should focus on the "flag blocked jobs, don't keep revisiting them" and "leave capacity buffer" principles as the two most operationally actionable takeaways for daily pipeline management.
- Factcheck should clearly distinguish jewelry-specific sourcing (pipeline stage structure, communication triggers) from general/adapted auto-repair-domain sourcing (prioritization scheme, capacity buffer percentages, daily check-in cadence), flagging the latter as reasoned domain adaptations rather than jewelry-specific findings.
