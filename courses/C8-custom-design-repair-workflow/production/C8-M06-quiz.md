# C8-M06 Quiz: Redesign Consultations

**Instructions:** Answer all questions. This module's quiz emphasizes correctly applying the discovery, assessment, and disclosure discipline, not memorizing exact recutting percentages.

---

**1. What is the single non-negotiable first step before any cleaning, disassembly, or stone removal on an heirloom piece?**
A) Immediately begin design work to keep the client excited
B) Create a complete "before" record — photographs of every side, marks, condition, and provenance — since many next steps are genuinely irreversible
C) Melt the metal down immediately to see what alloy it is
D) Skip documentation for pieces that appear to be in good condition

**Correct answer: B**
Explanation: Once a stone is removed from old prongs or gold is melted, there is no going back to compare against a "before" state, making complete documentation essential before any irreversible step.

---

**2. Why should an associate confirm who has decision-making authority over an heirloom piece before design work begins?**
A) Decision-making authority is never relevant to jewelry redesign
B) Heirloom jewelry often practically and emotionally belongs to more than one person, so confirming authority upfront prevents a painful dispute after work is already underway
C) Only the store's own staff should ever make decisions about a redesign
D) Decision-making authority only matters for pieces worth over $10,000

**Correct answer: B**
Explanation: Multiple family members or contributors may have a stake in an heirloom piece, and clarifying this before starting work avoids conflict once design or physical changes are already in progress.

---

**3. Why does this module emphasize asking what specifically needs to be preserved, rather than assuming it's the stone or original setting?**
A) Because clients always want everything about a piece preserved exactly as-is
B) Because clients sometimes assume they need to keep a specific physical element to honor a memory, when what they actually want preserved is something else entirely (like the stone itself, an engraving, or simply the memory) — surfacing this can open up design options they didn't realize existed
C) Because preservation questions are irrelevant to a successful redesign
D) Because the setting is always the only meaningful element in an heirloom piece

**Correct answer: B**
Explanation: Directly asking this question, rather than assuming, often reveals more design flexibility than the client initially thought was available.

---

**4. What is the default, lowest-risk path for most old mine and old European cut stones in a redesign project?**
A) Recutting the stone into a modern shape immediately
B) Simply resetting the stone into a new mounting with no alteration to the stone itself, when the stone's condition supports it
C) Melting the stone down along with the metal
D) Always replacing the original stone with a new one

**Correct answer: B**
Explanation: A reset-as-is approach avoids the irreversible risks associated with recutting and should be the default option whenever the stone's condition allows it.

---

**5. Approximately how much carat weight can be lost when recutting an old mine or old European cut diamond to a modern round brilliant?**
A) No weight is ever lost during recutting
B) Roughly 15% for a round-to-round recut, and as much as 40% when going from a more unusual outline (like a cushion-shaped old mine cut) to a true round shape
C) Exactly 50% in every single case, with no variation
D) Weight loss only occurs if the stone breaks during cutting

**Correct answer: B**
Explanation: These are real, quantifiable, and substantial losses that should be explained concretely to a client considering recutting, not glossed over.

---

**6. Besides the intentional weight loss from reshaping, what additional risk exists during the recutting process itself?**
A) There is no additional risk beyond the expected weight loss
B) A real risk of the stone breaking or fracturing on the cutting wheel, a risk that exists in addition to, not instead of, the expected weight reduction
C) Recutting is a risk-free, fully predictable process
D) The only risk is a minor delay in delivery time

**Correct answer: B**
Explanation: This is a distinct, additional risk beyond the anticipated weight loss, and it should be part of an honest disclosure conversation before a client commits to recutting.

---

**7. Why does recutting typically reduce, rather than increase, an antique stone's value to the market that most prizes it?**
A) Recutting always increases value with no exceptions
B) Antique-cut-focused collectors and buyers specifically pay a premium for a stone's original, historic character, so recutting into a modern shape permanently destroys the exact feature that market values most
C) Market value has no relationship to a stone's cut style
D) Antique stones have no distinct market or buyer base

**Correct answer: B**
Explanation: A stone "modernized" through recutting can become less valuable to the specific collector market that would have paid the most for its original antique character.

---

**8. What is an alternative to melting gold when combining multiple heirloom pieces into one new design?**
A) There is no alternative; melting is always required
B) Hand-disassembling existing pieces (rather than melting) and mechanically joining preserved elements using new connecting metal — sometimes called "the art of assembly" — which preserves physical continuity with the original pieces
C) Simply wearing all the original pieces together with no redesign at all
D) Always replacing every original element with entirely new materials

**Correct answer: B**
Explanation: This hand-assembly approach is worth mentioning specifically when a client is attached to the physical metal itself, not just the stones, since melting eliminates that specific continuity.

---

**Answer Key:** 1-B, 2-B, 3-B, 4-B, 5-B, 6-B, 7-B, 8-B
