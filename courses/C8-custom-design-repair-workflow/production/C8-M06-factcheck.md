# C8-M06 Fact-Check: Redesign Consultations

## Verification Status: Documentation/discovery process sourced from a rigorous preservation-first guide and cross-corroborated across four independent redesign-service providers; recutting risk figures sourced from a specialist antique-diamond dealer's own published commentary

## Claims Reviewed

**1. Document-before-touching, decision-making-authority-first, preservation-vs-redesign-comparison discovery process.**
Status: Sourced directly and in detail from Leyloon's "Redesigning Heirloom Jewelry: Questions, Constraints, and Documentation" guide, a detailed, preservation-first guide explicitly covering provenance, appraisal purpose, condition, family authority, and documentation "without promising alteration." This is a rigorous, structured source and was used as the primary reference for this module's documentation and authority-confirmation sections.

**2. The specific discovery-question set (history/meaning, what to preserve, style/lifestyle, design concepts, timeline/pricing, combining heirlooms).**
Status: CONFIRMED CONSISTENT across four independent sources: Leon Diamond's "Incorporating Family Heirlooms into New Designs" (explicitly listing history, preservation priorities, style/lifestyle, design concepts, and timeline/pricing as discussion topics), Legacy Jewellery's "Ultimate Guide to Redesigning Inherited Jewellery" (explicitly asking "what's the most meaningful part of this piece?" and covering combining multiple heirlooms), D&H Jewelers' heirloom-redesign service description (consultation-first process explicitly discussing sentimental story before design), and Hingham Jewelers' heirloom-redesign service page (consultation assessing existing pieces and most meaningful elements). All four independently describe a highly similar consultation-first, history-and-preservation-focused process.

**3. Stone condition inspection practices for antique/heirloom stones (magnified inspection for chips, girdle wear, fractures, prior repair/repolishing evidence).**
Status: Sourced from Imperial Cushions' "Antique Diamond Condition Guide" and "Recut or Preserve an Old Mine Diamond?" guide, both detailed, gemologically rigorous sources with specific inspection protocols; cross-consistent with Your Diamond Guys' vintage-diamond guide and Peter Indorf's appraiser-evaluation guide, both independently describing matching inspection considerations (chips, girdle wear, fractures, evidence of prior repolishing).

**4. Recutting weight-loss figures (approximately 15% for an old round recut to modern round; up to 40% for an old cushion recut to round) and specific risk factors (weight-loss expense plus possible breakage during faceting).**
Status: Sourced directly from Michael Goldstein's "Antique Diamonds" article, published via the DCGIA (a gemological association context) — Goldstein is described as a 30+-year antique-cut-diamond specialist who publishes his own price guide for this specific market, making this a credible, directly-on-point primary trade source specifically focused on this topic. Cross-consistent with Imperial Cushions' recut-vs-preserve framework (which independently describes weight loss and outline change as key trade-offs of a full recut), Gem Society's "Recutting Diamonds and Colored Gemstones" guide, and JCK's "Appraising 'Old-Cut' Gemstones" article (a trade-press source discussing recutting formulas specifically for appraisal purposes). The exact percentage figures should be understood as commonly-cited approximate ranges reflecting real variation by specific stone, not universal constants — this module presents them as such.

**5. Recutting typically reducing rather than increasing an antique stone's market value, since antique-cut-focused buyers pay a premium for original character.**
Status: Sourced from Imperial Cushions' guide (which frames recutting as destroying "original outline, facet relationships, culet and crown profile" — the exact features collectors value) and Uniglo Diamonds' diamond-resale-value guide (which explicitly advises comparing a recut proposal against an offer for the stone in its existing condition before proceeding). This is consistent with the general antique-jewelry-market logic already established in this course's C7 sibling content on estate/antique valuation.

**6. Melting vs. hand-disassembly/mechanical-reassembly ("the art of assembly") as a genuine alternative for combining multiple heirloom pieces.**
Status: Sourced directly from Gewels by Mona's "The Art of Assembly" guide, a specialist practitioner's detailed, first-person description of this exact technique (hand-dismantling pieces, identifying focal elements, using new connecting metal/reinforcement). Independently corroborated by a LinkedIn Pulse article specifically titled "Should You Melt Down Old Jewellery for a Redesign?" which makes the same core point ("you can merge them into one meaningful creation without melting anything down") from a different author/source.

**7. Worked scenario and practice exercise.**
Status: ORIGINAL illustrative synthesis created for this module, not drawn from any external source.
