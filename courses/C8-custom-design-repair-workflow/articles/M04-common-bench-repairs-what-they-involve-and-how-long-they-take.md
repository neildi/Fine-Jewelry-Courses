# Common Bench Repairs: What They Involve and How Long They Take

## Why This Module Matters

Ring sizing, prong work, and chain repair make up the overwhelming majority of day-to-day bench traffic, and giving a client an accurate, credible timeline for each depends on actually knowing what the process involves — not a rounded-off guess. This module covers the specific mechanics and realistic timeframes for the repairs that come across the counter most often.

## Ring Sizing: Up, Down, and Everything That Changes the Timeline

Ring sizing is the single most common bench job, and its timeline depends on more variables than most clients realize:

- **Sizing down** (making a ring smaller) is generally faster: a jeweler cuts a small section out of the band, brings the ends together, solders the seam, and reshapes and polishes the ring on a mandrel. For a plain gold band with no stones, this commonly takes 2-4 business days; a simple gold band with no stones can sometimes even be done same-day or within 24-48 hours at a shop with an on-site bench jeweler.
- **Sizing up** (making a ring larger) generally takes longer: the jeweler cuts the band, inserts a matching piece of new metal into the gap (sourcing an alloy that matches precisely), solders and welds it in place, then reshapes and polishes. This commonly takes 7-14 days for changes of roughly 1.5 sizes or more, since sourcing matching metal and achieving seamless grain alignment takes real, additional time beyond a simple down-size.
- **Metal type changes the timeline substantially**: gold and sterling silver are the easiest and fastest to resize; platinum is denser and requires higher temperatures and more specialized skill, often extending the timeline and sometimes requiring referral to a specialist bench jeweler; tungsten carbide generally cannot be resized at all, and titanium/stainless steel resizing is limited and not offered by every shop.
- **Stones add real time and complexity**: a solitaire is the simplest stone configuration to resize around; side stones, pavé, or a halo require careful, gradual heating so stones don't crack or become loose, plus a full prong check and tightening afterward — this commonly extends a job to 5-14 days. A full eternity band (stones set continuously around the shank) is frequently not resizable through standard methods at all, a point this course's C7 sibling content on estate/antique work covers in more depth.
- **Design details also add time**: engravings or patterns that need to be preserved or recreated after cutting and soldering, filigree or milgrain requiring more delicate handling, and two-tone metal combinations (different solders and temperatures for each metal) all extend a straightforward resize into a more careful, slower job.

## Prong Retipping and Rebuilding

Prong work is the second most common bench job, and the distinction between retipping and rebuilding matters for both timeline and cost:

- **Retipping** adds new metal (via solder or, increasingly, precision laser welding) to an existing prong that's worn thin at the tip, then reshapes and polishes it to match the original. A standard retipping job commonly takes 1-5 business days at most repair shops, with some quoting as fast as same-day to 1-2 days for straightforward cases, and others quoting up to a week depending on shop workflow and how many prongs need work.
- **Prong replacement or rebuilding** (needed when a prong has broken off or worn down beyond what retipping can restore) is a more involved structural repair, generally quoted in the same broad 3-5 business day range as retipping at most shops, though it can extend further for complex or custom settings.
- The general process, regardless of retip vs. rebuild, follows a consistent sequence: assess the prongs and determine which approach is needed, clean and prepare the surface for the new metal to bond properly, apply the new metal precisely, finish and polish to blend seamlessly with the original design, and perform a final inspection confirming the stone is held securely.

## Chain and Clasp Repair

Chain and clasp work is typically the fastest category of common bench repair, since it rarely involves stone-setting or structural reshaping:

- **A single-link re-solder** (repairing a broken link in a box, curb, or Figaro chain) commonly takes only 15-25 minutes of actual bench time.
- **Multi-link replacement** (replacing 3-5 damaged links and re-soldering) takes somewhat longer, commonly 45-75 minutes, and requires matching the original wire gauge precisely so the repaired section doesn't stand out or weaken the chain.
- **Clasp replacement** (lobster clasp, spring ring, toggle) commonly takes 20-40 minutes, and similarly requires matching the new clasp's size to the chain's thickness so the repair looks and functions correctly.

These short bench-time estimates are specifically for the hands-on work itself; total turnaround (including queue time, if the shop is busy, or shipping time for mail-in service) is often longer than the raw bench-time figure, which is worth clarifying explicitly with a client so "20 minutes of work" doesn't get misheard as "ready in 20 minutes."

## Why "Same-Day" Isn't Always Honest, Even When It's Technically Possible

A consistent theme across every repair category above is worth naming directly: any work involving heat, metal manipulation, or stone removal needs careful, deliberate handling, and rushing it is a documented, real cause of damaged stones and failed repairs. Same-day service is genuinely possible for certain simple, stone-free jobs (a plain band resize, a clasp swap) when a shop has an on-site bench jeweler and isn't backed up — but promising same-day turnaround on a job involving stones, structural rebuilding, or platinum simply to satisfy an eager client sets up exactly the kind of rushed work this module's sourced timelines specifically warn against.

## A Worked Scenario: The Client Who Wants It "By Friday"

A client bringing in an engagement ring with a halo setting for resizing asks if it can be ready by Friday, four days away. Rather than agreeing to satisfy the request, the honest response draws directly on this module's content: "I want to give you an answer I know we can actually deliver. Because this ring has stones set close to where the resize happens, we need to heat that area carefully so nothing cracks, and every stone near the resized section gets checked and re-tightened afterward — that's typically a 5-to-14-day job, not a same-day one. If Friday is a hard deadline for something specific, let's talk about what's realistic, but I don't want to promise Friday and then either rush the work or let you down." This applies the module's core distinction (stone-adjacent work takes longer, and rushing it is a real risk) directly to a live scheduling pressure.

## Key Takeaways

- Ring sizing timelines depend on direction (down is faster than up), metal type (gold/silver fastest, platinum slower, some hard metals not resizable), and the presence/placement of stones (solitaires are simplest; pavé/halo/eternity settings add real time and complexity).
- Prong retipping (adding metal to a worn tip) commonly takes 1-5 business days; prong rebuilding (replacing a broken or severely worn prong) runs a similar but sometimes longer range depending on setting complexity.
- Chain and clasp repairs are the fastest common bench category, with actual hands-on bench time often just 15-75 minutes, though total turnaround including queue or shipping time should be communicated separately from raw bench time.
- Any repair involving heat, metal manipulation, or stone removal genuinely requires careful, deliberate handling; rushing these steps is a documented cause of cracked or lost stones, which is why "same-day" promises should be reserved for jobs that are actually simple and stone-free.
- Giving a client a specific, factor-based explanation for a repair timeline (rather than a vague estimate or an overpromised deadline) is both more honest and more likely to be actually deliverable.
