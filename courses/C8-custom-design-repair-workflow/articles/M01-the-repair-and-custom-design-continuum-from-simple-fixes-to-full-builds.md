# The Repair and Custom Design Continuum: From Simple Fixes to Full Builds

## Why This Module Matters

"Repair" and "custom design" get talked about as if they're two separate departments, but in most jewelry stores they sit on a single continuum, sharing the same intake discipline, the same job-tracking infrastructure, and often the same bench jeweler. This module establishes that continuum and the vocabulary this course uses throughout — the foundation for intake (Module 2), the CAD-to-casting workflow (Module 3), bench repair specifics (Module 4), pricing (Module 5), redesign consultations (Module 6), quality control (Module 7), and the capstone pipeline-management module (Module 8).

## The Continuum, From Simple to Complex

Work coming across the counter spans a genuinely wide range of complexity, and recognizing where a specific job sits on this continuum shapes almost everything else — timeline, pricing, who handles it, and what the client conversation needs to cover:

- **Simple bench repairs**: ring sizing, prong retipping, clasp replacement, chain soldering — typically same-day to a few days, handled directly by a bench jeweler with no design consultation needed.
- **Moderate repairs and restoration**: re-shanking a worn band, replacing a stone, restoring an antique piece's function without altering its design — these draw directly on this course's C7 sibling content (Module 2's construction knowledge, Module 6's authentication skills) when the piece has any age or attribution complexity.
- **Redesign and remounting**: taking existing materials (often heirloom stones) and reworking them into a substantially different piece — this sits in the middle of the continuum, since it uses design consultation and sometimes CAD, but starts from existing rather than newly sourced materials. Module 6 covers this specifically.
- **Fully custom design**: building an entirely new piece from a client's concept, using CAD modeling, casting, and hand-finishing from the ground up — the most complex, highest-touch end of the continuum, covered in depth in Module 3.

## Why the Same Infrastructure Serves the Whole Continuum

A well-run store uses one consistent operational system across this entire range, and understanding why matters before diving into any specific stage:

**Every job, regardless of complexity, needs the same core intake discipline**: a unique job ticket or work order, a clear scope of work, photographic documentation of starting condition, and a tracked status that both staff and the client can check. Modern jewelry-specific repair and job-tracking software (purpose-built platforms distinct from generic retail POS systems) typically moves a job through a consistent status sequence — something like intake, awaiting approval, in progress, quality check, and ready for pickup — regardless of whether the underlying job is a five-minute ring sizing or a six-week custom build. This consistency is what prevents jobs from getting lost, missed, or miscommunicated, and it's the foundation Module 2 builds on directly.

**The same person (or the same small team) often handles work across the whole spectrum.** A bench jeweler's role genuinely spans this entire continuum: performing routine repairs, assessing structural integrity and stone security, and — increasingly, using CAD and 3D printing alongside traditional hand skills — translating a client's custom-design concept into a producible piece. This means the skills this course covers aren't siloed by job type; a strong bench jeweler moves fluidly across the continuum within a single day.

**Every job needs a consistent client-communication cadence**, regardless of where it sits on the complexity spectrum: an intake confirmation, an approval step when scope or pricing needs client sign-off, updates if scope or timing changes, a completion notification, and a pickup or shipping confirmation. A five-day ring sizing and a ten-week custom build both deserve this same basic communication rhythm, scaled appropriately to the job's actual timeline.

## Chain of Custody: Why This Matters Across the Whole Continuum

Because jewelry work involves handing valuable client property across multiple people and sometimes multiple locations (in-house bench, an outside specialist casting house, a repair partner), a clear, unbroken chain-of-custody record matters at every point on this continuum, not just for complex custom builds. Every handoff — counter to secure storage, store to an outbound shipment or specialist pickup, specialist back to the store, store to the client — should be logged against the same unique job ticket that started at intake. This isn't bureaucratic overhead; it's the specific mechanism that lets a store answer, confidently and immediately, "where is my ring right now?" at any point in a repair or custom-build timeline, and it directly protects the store if a piece's condition or whereabouts is ever questioned.

## A Worked Scenario: The Job That Changes Complexity Mid-Stream

A client brings in a ring for what seems like a simple prong repair. On closer inspection at intake, the bench jeweler discovers the shank is significantly worn and would benefit from a full re-shank rather than a spot repair — moving this job from "simple bench repair" to "moderate repair" on the continuum. Rather than proceeding on the original assumption, the correct response is to pause, document the new finding with photos, and return to the client with an updated scope and price before any further work begins: "When we looked closely, we found the band itself is worn thin enough that a full re-shank will actually serve you much better long-term than just fixing the prong — here's what that changes about the timeline and cost, and I want your sign-off before we proceed." This demonstrates exactly the kind of intake-to-approval discipline (covered in depth in Module 2) that has to hold regardless of where a job starts or ends up on the complexity continuum.

## Key Takeaways

- Repair and custom design work exist on a single continuum from simple bench repairs through moderate restoration, redesign/remounting, and fully custom builds — not as separate, unrelated departments.
- The same core intake discipline (unique job ticket, documented condition, tracked status) and client-communication cadence (intake confirmation, approval, updates, completion, pickup) should apply consistently across the entire continuum, scaled to each job's actual complexity and timeline.
- Bench jewelers typically work fluidly across this entire spectrum within a single day, combining traditional hand skills with CAD and 3D printing as the job calls for it.
- A clear, unbroken chain-of-custody record, logged against a single unique job ticket, matters at every point on the continuum — not just for complex custom builds — since it's what lets a store confidently answer "where is my piece right now?" at any moment.
- Recognizing when a job's actual complexity differs from its initial assumption (and pausing for a documented, approved scope change) is a core discipline that applies regardless of where on the continuum a job started.
