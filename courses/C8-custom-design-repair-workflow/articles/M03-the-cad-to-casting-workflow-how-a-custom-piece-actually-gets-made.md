# The CAD-to-Casting Workflow: How a Custom Piece Actually Gets Made

## Why This Module Matters

Every custom piece follows a physical process most clients never see, and understanding it well enough to explain it builds real client confidence — while also explaining exactly why certain timelines, costs, and design constraints are non-negotiable physical realities, not arbitrary store policy. This module walks through the full CAD-to-casting pipeline, generalizing the consultation-and-sign-off workflow this course's sibling bridal content already covers into the underlying manufacturing process itself.

## Stage One: From CAD File to Physical Pattern

Once a CAD design is approved (the sign-off discipline covered in this course's bridal-specific content applies here too — approval locks the specifications), the digital file needs to become a physical wax or resin pattern before any metal is involved. Two methods dominate:

- **3D-printed wax or resin patterns**: a printer builds the pattern directly from the CAD file, layer by layer, with no physical mold required. This is now the dominant method for one-off custom pieces specifically because it requires no tooling and can capture fine CAD detail precisely.
- **Hand-carved wax**: a model maker carves the pattern by hand from a wax block — the traditional method, still used for genuinely one-of-a-kind artistic work or when a physical, tactile design process suits the piece better than digital modeling.

Within 3D printing specifically, there's a real, technical distinction worth knowing: **direct wax printers** produce a true wax pattern ready for casting with no further processing, while **castable resin printers** produce a photopolymer resin pattern that requires UV post-curing and burns out somewhat less cleanly — direct wax printing is generally reported to have a meaningfully higher successful burnout rate than resin, precisely because resin can leave more residue or cause porosity if not handled carefully during burnout.

## Stage Two: Building the Investment Mold

The pattern (whether hand-carved or printed) gets a **sprue** attached — a thin wax rod that will later form the channel molten metal flows through. The pattern and sprue are placed inside a metal flask, and a plaster-like **investment** material (a gypsum-bonded plaster and silica mixture) is mixed and poured around it, filling the flask completely. Careful mixing and vibration during this step matter directly: trapped air bubbles in the investment become defects in the final casting, so removing them (through timed mixing and vibration on a dedicated table) before the investment sets is a genuinely important quality step, not a cosmetic one.

## Stage Three: Burnout

The filled flask goes into a burnout kiln and is heated gradually, on a controlled temperature ramp, up to roughly 1,350-1,400°F over a period commonly taking 8-12 hours. This gradual heating melts and vaporizes the wax pattern entirely, leaving a precise, empty cavity inside the hardened investment in the exact shape of the original pattern — this is the step that gives lost-wax casting its name, since the wax pattern is genuinely destroyed (lost) in the process of creating the mold cavity.

## Stage Four: Casting the Metal

Molten metal — gold, silver, platinum, or palladium alloy, heated to that specific metal's casting temperature (commonly cited in the range of 1,000-1,800°F depending on the alloy) — is introduced into the still-hot investment mold. This is done using centrifugal casting (spinning the flask rapidly to force molten metal outward into every cavity through centrifugal force) or vacuum-assisted casting (pulling a vacuum to draw the metal into fine detail), both methods designed specifically to force metal into thin, delicate areas like prongs and fine engraving before the metal solidifies — a casting method that doesn't fully fill these fine areas produces a piece with missing or incomplete detail, a real, physical failure mode this process is specifically engineered to prevent.

## Stage Five: Cooling, Breakout, and Finishing

Once the metal solidifies and cools somewhat, the flask is quenched in water, which serves two purposes simultaneously: further cooling the metal, and breaking apart the now-brittle investment material so it releases the metal casting. The piece (still attached to the metal sprue and, for batch work, to a full "tree" of multiple castings) is then pickled (cleaned in an acid solution to remove investment residue and surface oxidation), and the sprues are cut away with a jeweler's saw. From here, the piece moves into filing, stone-setting, and polishing — the hand-finishing work that turns a rough casting into a finished piece.

## Common Casting Defects and Why They Happen

Understanding what can go wrong in this process, and why, helps explain both realistic timelines and the genuine value of quality control (covered in depth in Module 7):

- **Porosity** (small internal or surface voids in the metal) most commonly results from trapped air in the investment, insufficient vacuum/vibration during investing, or improper metal temperature during the pour.
- **Incomplete fill** (missing or thin detail, especially in fine prongs) results from metal cooling too quickly before fully reaching every cavity, or insufficient centrifugal/vacuum force during the pour.
- **Investment inclusions** (bits of plaster embedded in the metal surface) result from a cracked or improperly cured investment shell breaking apart during the pour rather than staying intact until the metal solidifies.

None of these defects are visible from the outside of a piece before finishing — which is precisely why a systematic quality-control inspection (Module 7) after casting and before final finishing is a genuinely necessary step, not an optional one.

## A Worked Scenario: Explaining the Timeline Honestly

A client asks why a custom piece "can't just be done faster" when they've already approved the CAD file. Rather than a vague appeal to "it takes time," the physical process itself is the honest answer: "Once you approve the design, it still has to become a physical wax pattern, get invested in plaster, go through an 8-to-12-hour burnout in a kiln, get cast in molten metal, cool, and then go through hand-finishing and stone-setting. Several of those steps have to happen on their own physical timeline — the burnout kiln, for instance, needs that full ramp-up time regardless of how much anyone might want to speed it up, because rushing it risks a flawed casting. I want to give you a timeline I know we can actually hit, not one that sounds good today and causes a problem in three weeks." This directly ties the technical process to the timeline commitment, giving the client a real reason rather than a placating one.

## Key Takeaways

- Once a CAD file is approved, it becomes a physical pattern (3D-printed wax/resin or hand-carved wax), which is then invested in plaster, burned out in a kiln (typically 8-12 hours on a controlled temperature ramp), cast with molten metal, and finished by hand.
- Direct wax 3D printing generally achieves a higher successful burnout rate than castable resin printing, since resin can leave more residue or cause porosity if not carefully post-cured and burned out.
- Centrifugal and vacuum-assisted casting methods exist specifically to force molten metal into fine details like prongs and engraving before it solidifies — without this force, thin areas can be left incomplete.
- Common casting defects (porosity, incomplete fill, investment inclusions) each trace back to a specific point in the process (trapped air, insufficient casting force, a cracked investment shell), and none are visible until after finishing begins, which is exactly why systematic quality control matters.
- Explaining a custom piece's timeline in terms of the actual physical process (kiln ramp times, cooling requirements) gives clients an honest, credible reason for a timeline, rather than a vague appeal that erodes trust.
