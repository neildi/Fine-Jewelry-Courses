# Capstone: Managing the Repair and Custom Design Pipeline

## Why This Module Matters

Every prior module in this course covered a single job in isolation — intake, casting, a repair, pricing, a redesign consultation, a handoff. In real store operations, none of that happens one job at a time. A bench jeweler or store manager is tracking dozens of jobs simultaneously, at every stage from intake to pickup, at every complexity level from a quick clasp fix to a months-long custom build. This capstone module brings every earlier module together into the skill that actually determines whether a repair and custom program runs smoothly or constantly feels behind: managing the full pipeline at once.

## The Pipeline as a Real Structure, Not a Pile

The single biggest failure mode in multi-job repair operations is treating every job as its own isolated task with no shared structure. A functioning pipeline organizes every job into a small number of clear stages that reflect what's actually happening, not an idealized version of the process:

- **Backlog/intake queue** — received, not yet started, awaiting triage
- **Scheduled/to-do** — triaged and queued for the bench, with a target date
- **In progress** — actively being worked, including specific sub-states like "waiting on parts or stones," "waiting on customer approval," or "at an outside vendor," since a job sitting idle for a reason outside the store's control needs to be visible as a different state than a job that's simply stalled
- **Quality control** — finished at the bench, awaiting the Module 7 final inspection
- **Ready for pickup** — inspected, documented, awaiting the client

Every job should always sit in exactly one of these stages, visible to the whole team, not tracked only in one technician's head or a sticky note. This is what allows anyone on the team — not just whoever originally took the job in — to answer a client's "is it ready yet?" call without hunting someone down.

## Prioritization: Not Just First-In, First-Out

With many jobs stacked up, a purely first-come-first-served approach is a common but incomplete answer, because not every job carries the same cost of delay. A more useful prioritization approach considers several factors together:

- **Promised due date**, since the client commitment made at intake is the real constraint, not when the job happened to arrive
- **What's actually blocking progress**, since a job waiting on a special-order stone or an outside vendor can't move regardless of priority, and time spent trying to advance it is wasted — those jobs should be flagged and set aside rather than repeatedly revisited
- **Job complexity and required skill**, matching each job to whichever bench jeweler is fastest and most reliable at that specific type of work, rather than assigning purely by availability
- **"Comeback" or previously-delayed jobs**, which often deserve real priority precedence, since a client who's already waited past a promised date has a legitimately higher-cost delay than a job just entering the queue

The practical output of good prioritization isn't a single universal ranking — it's matching the right job to the right bench capacity at the right time, while keeping blocked jobs visibly separate from jobs that are simply next in line.

## Capacity: Don't Schedule Every Available Hour

A pipeline that looks efficient on paper because every hour is booked is often actually fragile in practice. Real capacity planning holds back a portion of total available bench time as buffer — commonly cited guidance suggests planning around roughly 10-20% less than theoretical full capacity — specifically to absorb the inspection findings, approval delays, and "while you're at it" additional work that inevitably shows up once a job is actually on the bench. A schedule with zero slack means a single delayed approval or a single piece that needs more repair than initially scoped cascades into every job behind it running late.

Similarly, a brief daily check-in — comparing the plan for the day against what's actually happening, since one late approval or one missing part can reorder the whole day's priorities — catches this drift early rather than after several jobs have already slipped.

## Communication Across the Pipeline

Module 2 introduced status transparency as an intake-time commitment, and it applies just as directly here at the pipeline level: every meaningful status change — job approved, job delayed, job ready — should trigger a client update, rather than leaving clients to call in and ask "is it done yet?" This matters more, not less, as job volume grows, since a larger pipeline makes it easier for any single job's status to go stale without anyone noticing unless updates are a structural habit rather than something remembered only when time allows.

## A Worked Scenario: A Full Bench on a Monday Morning

A store manager arrives Monday to find twelve active jobs: three ready for pickup, four in progress (one waiting on a special-order stone), two just intaken over the weekend and not yet triaged, two flagged from Friday as needing rework after failing final inspection, and one custom piece awaiting a client's CAD approval before casting can begin. Rather than working whichever job is easiest to start, the manager runs a quick triage: the stone-waiting job gets flagged and set aside since no bench time will move it forward today; the two rework jobs get real priority since they represent clients who've already waited past a promised date; the weekend intakes get triaged and slotted based on complexity and due date; and the CAD-approval job stays parked until the client responds, with a follow-up message sent proactively rather than the job simply sitting silent. This ten-minute morning triage, done consistently, is what actually prevents a full bench from turning into a chaotic, reactive day.

## Practice Exercise

Using the twelve-job scenario above (or a similar mixed batch provided by your manager), practice sorting every job into the correct pipeline stage, identifying which jobs are genuinely blocked versus simply next in queue, and drafting the specific client communication each job's status change should trigger. This is the capstone assessment for this course: demonstrate that you can hold the full pipeline in view and make prioritization decisions that reflect real constraints, not just whichever job is in front of you.

## Key Takeaways

- A functioning pipeline organizes every job into a small number of clearly defined, team-visible stages — including specifically flagging jobs that are blocked (waiting on parts, stones, an outside vendor, or client approval) as distinct from jobs simply queued and waiting.
- Prioritization should weigh promised due dates, what's actually blocking a job, matching job complexity to the right technician's skill, and giving real precedence to previously-delayed "comeback" jobs — not a simple first-come-first-served order.
- Planning bench capacity at roughly 10-20% below the theoretical maximum, rather than scheduling every available hour, absorbs the inevitable delays and additional work that show up once jobs are actually underway.
- A brief daily check-in comparing the day's plan against emerging reality catches schedule drift before it cascades into missed dates across multiple jobs.
- Every meaningful status change should proactively trigger a client update — this becomes more important, not less, as pipeline volume grows, since a larger pipeline makes silent, stale statuses easier to miss.
