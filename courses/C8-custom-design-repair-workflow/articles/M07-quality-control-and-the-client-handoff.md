# Quality Control and the Client Handoff

## Why This Module Matters

Every module in this course up to this point has been building toward a single moment: handing a finished piece back to a client with full confidence it is correct, secure, and exactly what was promised. A systematic final inspection — done the same way every time, regardless of how simple or complex the job — is what actually earns that confidence. This module covers what that inspection looks like, what a warranty realistically does and doesn't promise, and how to run the handoff itself so the client leaves informed and reassured rather than simply relieved to have their piece back.

## The Final Inspection: A Consistent, Systematic Sequence

A jewelry QC inspection before delivery should never depend on how busy the day is or how routine the job looked. The core sequence, adapted from wholesale/manufacturing quality-control practice to a retail bench-repair and custom-work context, covers four areas in order:

1. **Stone security.** Every stone gets gentle lateral pressure testing with a loupe or tweezers, not just a glance — this is the single check most directly tied to preventing a lost stone after pickup. Prong height, spacing, and finish should be uniform, and prong tips should be smooth with no sharp edge that could snag clothing or skin.
2. **Surface and finish, under 4x-10x magnification.** Check for porosity, tool marks, uneven polish, solder marks visible on a finished surface, and scratches or dents that weren't part of the original piece. This directly follows from Module 3's defect content — porosity and incomplete fill are exactly the kind of thing this inspection step is designed to catch before a piece ever leaves the bench.
3. **Function.** Clasps should open and close smoothly with adequate spring tension; hinges should move freely without binding; any moving part should be cycle-tested, not just checked once. A ring should be checked on a mandrel to confirm the actual size matches what was quoted.
4. **Specification match.** The finished piece is compared directly against the original intake photos and the agreed scope — does it match what was actually approved, not just what looks acceptable in isolation?

The specific, non-negotiable discipline behind this sequence: **do not skip steps because the first few checks looked fine.** A piece that passes stone security and surface inspection can still fail on function or spec match, and checking only what seems most likely to have a problem misses exactly the defects a systematic process is designed to catch.

## Comparing Against the Intake Record

Module 2 established a specific before/after photo-pairing discipline, and this is precisely where it pays off. The finished piece should be compared directly against the intake photos and condition notes: does the repair area match what was scoped, is anything that was documented as pre-existing still present and unchanged, and does the piece match the client's original description of what they wanted? This comparison is what actually demonstrates — to the store and, if ever needed, to the client — that the work performed was the work agreed to, and that nothing unrelated changed in the process.

## What a Warranty Actually Promises

Just as Module 2 covered what a liability waiver does and doesn't protect against, a repair or craftsmanship warranty needs the same precise understanding before it's explained to a client. Across real repair and jewelry warranty policies reviewed, a highly consistent pattern emerges:

- **Warranties typically cover defects in the specific workmanship performed** — a solder joint failing under normal wear shortly after a repair, or a clasp the store installed failing due to a manufacturing issue in that clasp. Warranty periods commonly range from 90 days for minor adjustments up to 6 months to a year for more substantial repair or setting work, with some stores offering longer or lifetime coverage on newly manufactured pieces specifically.
- **Warranties consistently exclude**: normal wear and tear (scratches, patina, loss of shine); damage from misuse, accidents, or impact; pre-existing conditions (worn prongs, thin shanks, cracked stones) that existed before the store's work; and — critically — any service performed by a third party during the warranty period, which typically voids it entirely.
- **"Lifetime" guarantees are frequently conditional**, not unconditional: several major retailers' lifetime diamond/gemstone guarantees specifically require documented professional inspection on a set schedule (commonly every six months) to remain valid — missing that inspection cadence can void coverage the client believed was permanent.
- **The remedy is typically limited**, not open-ended: most warranties cap the store's obligation at re-performing the specific defective work or refunding the original service fee, not an unlimited replacement guarantee.

Exactly as with the liability waiver in Module 2, overselling a warranty's protection — letting a client believe "lifetime warranty" means "covered no matter what happens" — creates a worse outcome than clearly explaining its real, specific scope at the point of handoff.

## The Handoff Itself: More Than Just Returning the Piece

The handoff is a genuine opportunity, not just a transaction to complete quickly. A strong handoff includes:

- **A direct comparison to the intake photos**, showing the client specifically what changed and confirming anything noted as pre-existing is unchanged — this closes the loop Module 2 opened and gives the client visible, concrete evidence the work was done as agreed.
- **Care guidance specific to the piece** — cleaning method, any material-specific caution (rhodium replating timeline for white gold, avoiding chemical exposure for certain stones), and the recommended future inspection cadence.
- **A clear, honest explanation of the warranty's actual scope** — what it covers, how long, and what would void it (including, if applicable, the inspection-cadence requirement for any "lifetime" component).
- **For custom or redesign pieces specifically, a moment of genuine presentation** rather than a rushed counter handoff — many clients, especially on emotionally significant redesign projects, are experiencing something closer to the actual "reveal" moment from Module 6's discovery conversation than a routine pickup, and treating it that way (a private area, unhurried pacing, letting the client actually look at and try on the piece before signing anything) respects the significance of what's happening.

## A Worked Scenario: The Rushed End of a Long Day

At the end of a busy day, a technician is tempted to give a finished custom redesign a quick visual check and move on to close out the register. The correct discipline holds regardless of the hour: run the full four-part sequence (stone security, surface/finish, function, spec match), compare against the original intake photos specifically for this piece, and only then prepare it for pickup. When the client arrives, rather than a rushed "here you go," the handoff still includes the comparison, the care guidance, and the warranty explanation — even if it means staying a few minutes past closing. A shortcut taken here to save ten minutes risks a stone loss, a warranty dispute, or a client who leaves without understanding what they actually own — all of which cost far more than the ten minutes saved.

## Practice Exercise

Practice running the four-part final inspection sequence out loud on a hypothetical finished ring resize, explicitly narrating what you're checking at each step and why. Then practice the full handoff conversation for a custom redesign piece, including the intake-photo comparison, care guidance, and an honest, specific explanation of what the warranty does and doesn't cover.

## Key Takeaways

- A systematic four-part final inspection — stone security, surface/finish under magnification, function, and specification match against the original scope — should be run the same way every time, regardless of job complexity or how busy the day is.
- Comparing the finished piece directly against Module 2's intake photos and condition notes is what actually demonstrates the agreed work was performed correctly and nothing unrelated changed.
- Warranties typically cover only the store's own specific workmanship defects, exclude normal wear/pre-existing conditions/third-party service, and "lifetime" guarantees are frequently conditioned on a documented inspection cadence — overselling this scope creates worse outcomes than explaining it precisely.
- The remedy under most warranties is capped at re-performing the defective work or refunding the service fee, not an open-ended replacement guarantee.
- The handoff is a genuine opportunity for a comparison walkthrough, specific care guidance, and honest warranty explanation — and for custom or redesign pieces, deserves unhurried, presentation-quality pacing rather than a routine counter transaction.
