# Appraisal, Insurance, and Aftercare: Protecting the Purchase for a Lifetime

## Why This Module Matters

A client who leaves the store without understanding how their purchase is actually protected is a client who may discover, years later and at the worst possible moment, that their engagement ring wasn't covered the way they assumed. This module covers the three pieces of that protection conversation: what an appraisal actually is and isn't, the real trade-offs between insurance options, and the maintenance cadence that keeps a ring both beautiful and structurally sound.

## Appraisals: Retail Replacement Value, Not Resale Value

The single most important thing to communicate clearly about an appraisal is what number it actually produces. The vast majority of appraisals written today use **retail replacement value (RRV)** — the cost to replace the item with an equivalent piece at a traditional retail jewelry store, accounting for materials, labor, craftsmanship, and standard retail markup. This is intentionally the highest of the standard valuation types, because insurance is designed to make a client whole by letting them walk into a store and buy an equivalent replacement, not to compensate them for what the item would fetch if sold.

This is genuinely worth explaining directly, because it prevents a common and understandable misunderstanding: if a client ever asked what their ring was "worth" in a resale sense, that number — fair market value, which reflects what a willing buyer would actually pay in an open resale market — is typically meaningfully lower than the retail replacement value on their insurance appraisal. Both figures are accurate; they just answer different questions. An independent, written appraisal (from a certified appraiser, ideally through the National Association of Jewelry Appraisers or American Gem Society) typically costs $50-200 and takes up to a week, and virtually every insurer requires one before writing a policy.

## The Three Insurance Paths, and What Each Actually Covers

There are three commonly discussed paths for insuring an engagement ring, and the differences between them are practical, not just theoretical:

1. **Standard homeowners/renters insurance, unmodified.** This is the option many clients assume covers their ring by default, and it's worth correcting that assumption directly: standard policies typically cap jewelry coverage at a sub-limit of roughly $1,000-2,500 total, regardless of the ring's actual appraised value, and often only cover theft specifically — not loss, mysterious disappearance, or accidental damage (a stone falling out, a ring going down a drain). For any ring with real value, this coverage alone is almost certainly insufficient.
2. **A scheduled rider (or floater) added to an existing homeowners/renters policy.** This raises the covered amount for that specific, itemized piece up to its full appraised value, removing the sub-limit. The trade-off worth flagging: some riders still exclude mysterious disappearance or accidental damage depending on the specific carrier, and a claim on this rider is typically reported to CLUE (a shared insurance claims history database), which can affect future homeowners premiums or underwriting.
3. **A standalone specialty jewelry policy** from a dedicated insurer (Jewelers Mutual, BriteCo, and similar companies are commonly cited examples). These are typically written on an all-risk (open-perils) basis, meaning the policy covers any loss not specifically excluded — broader than most riders by default — and importantly, claims on a standalone policy generally do not touch the client's homeowners CLUE record. Annual cost is commonly cited in the range of 0.5-1.5% of the ring's appraised value, comparable to or sometimes cheaper than a rider, with the added benefit of not affecting home insurance history.

There's no single "correct" answer for every client — someone who wants to consolidate policies and has a rider with strong terms may reasonably choose that path, while someone who wants the broadest coverage without touching their home insurance record may prefer a standalone policy. The value in this conversation isn't steering the client to one option, but making sure they understand what each path actually does and doesn't cover before they decide.

## Aftercare: A Cadence, Not a One-Time Conversation

The maintenance cadence for a daily-worn engagement ring is consistently described across sources, and it connects directly back to Module 5's setting-type content — prong wear specifically is the reason this cadence matters as much as it does:

- **Daily:** Wipe with a soft cloth to remove skin oils and lotion residue before they build into a film.
- **Weekly to monthly:** Clean with warm water, mild soap, and a soft brush to clear grime that collects behind the stone, where light needs to pass through for full brilliance.
- **Every six months:** Professional ultrasonic and steam cleaning plus a full prong and setting inspection — this is the consensus figure across essentially every source reviewed for a ring worn daily, and it's the single most important recurring touchpoint, since a prong wearing thin is, as multiple sources put it, "the cheapest repair and the most expensive failure" if missed.
- **Every one to two years (white gold specifically):** Rhodium replating, connecting directly back to Module 5's white-gold maintenance content.
- **Every five to ten years:** Prong retipping as part of normal wear, not a sign anything went wrong.
- **Every two to three years:** Insurance reappraisal, to make sure the policy's coverage amount still reflects current replacement cost (material prices, especially gold and diamond prices, shift over time).

Most reputable jewelers offer the six-month cleaning and inspection free of charge for pieces purchased in-store, which makes this an easy, low-friction touchpoint to build into a client relationship — and it connects directly to C3's clienteling cadence content, since this is a natural, low-pressure reason to bring a client back into the store twice a year.

## A Worked Scenario: The Protection Conversation at Checkout

A client purchasing a $12,000 engagement ring hasn't mentioned insurance at all. Rather than letting this pass, it's worth raising directly and without pressure: "One last thing before you go — I want to make sure you leave knowing how to protect this. Your homeowners policy alone likely only covers jewelry up to about $1,000 to $2,500, so for a ring this value, you'd want either a scheduled rider on that policy or a standalone jewelry policy — either works, they just have slightly different trade-offs. We'll provide a written appraisal today for exactly this purpose, and I'd also recommend coming back in six months for a complimentary cleaning and prong check, which is included with your purchase." This gives the client concrete, actionable information rather than a vague "you should probably get that insured" and sets up the first of many recurring, low-pressure touchpoints.

Then practice walking through all three insurance paths for a client with an existing homeowners policy, helping them think through the trade-offs rather than recommending a single option outright.

## Key Takeaways

- Appraisals for insurance purposes use retail replacement value, which is intentionally higher than fair market (resale) value — both are accurate, but they answer different questions, and explaining this prevents a common misunderstanding.
- Standard homeowners/renters insurance typically caps jewelry coverage at $1,000-2,500 and often covers theft only, which is insufficient for most engagement rings.
- A scheduled rider raises coverage to full appraised value but may still exclude mysterious disappearance/accidental damage and reports to CLUE; a standalone policy is typically broader (all-risk) and doesn't affect homeowners claims history, at a comparable cost of roughly 0.5-1.5% of appraised value annually.
- The professional maintenance consensus for a daily-worn ring is cleaning and inspection every six months, connecting directly to Module 5's setting-durability content — prong wear caught early is a minor repair; missed, it's a lost stone.
- Raise the insurance and aftercare conversation proactively at the point of sale, and use the free six-month cleaning/inspection as a natural, recurring touchpoint for ongoing clienteling.
