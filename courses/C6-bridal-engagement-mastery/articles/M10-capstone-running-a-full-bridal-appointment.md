# Capstone: Running a Full Bridal Appointment

## One appointment, nine skills, no seams

Here is the appointment this whole course has been building toward, in a single pass. A couple walks in, a little nervous. You greet them, book the follow-up properly, discover the journey they've been on, guide the shape and setting and metal without overwhelming them, answer the lab-grown question honestly, find the budget without breaking the romance, put the ring on the hand and go quiet at the right moment, read the two of them as one, set the size and the timeline honestly, and plant the band and the referral on the way out. Nine modules, one continuous performance — and the mark of mastery is that the couple never sees the seams.

This capstone is the assessment module. It holds the appointment together, gives you the full walkthrough to practice against, and points you to the two graded artifacts: the **role-play** and the **bridal conversion log**.

## Why this matters

The Prompt Kit's C6 spec ends here for a reason. The assessment for this course is not a quiz — it is a **role-play** (a full appointment, observed and scored) plus a **bridal conversion log** (a running record of real appointments, outcomes, and what was learned). The course exists to move three numbers — **bridal conversion, band attach, referral volume** — and the only honest test of those numbers is a live appointment, recorded and reflected on. The resource pairing the spec names — **JCK, GIA, and NDC** — is the reference shelf for this: JCK for the market and the conversation, GIA for the stones, NDC for the diamond story and ethics you tell. Keep all three close for the capstone, because the assessment draws on all of them.

## The full walkthrough, stage by stage

Here is the appointment as one continuous sequence, with the module that owns each stage. Practice it until the transitions are invisible.

1. **Book and prepare (M02).** The appointment starts before the couple arrives: the discovery you ran, the tray and room you set, the agenda you can state in one breath. "Here's the plan — I'll learn what you're looking for, show you shapes and settings, and we'll settle on a budget that works. Does that sound good?"
2. **Discover the journey (M01).** Which journey is this — solo researcher, couple deciding together, or family-in-tow? You already know what a modern bridal buyer does: researches online, then comes in to try on. Meet them where they are, and never assume.
3. **Guide shape → setting → metal (M03).** One question at a time. Brilliant or step-cut first; setting led by their lifestyle; metal with the honest trade-offs (platinum's patina, white gold's rhodium, 14k vs 18k). You are the guide, not the lecturer.
4. **The lab-grown question (M04).** If it comes — or if you raise it — keep it neutral: same material, different origin, three honest differences, and the disclosure discipline that keeps the trust.
5. **The budget conversation (M05).** Ask early and plainly; frame a range, not a number; triage the trade-offs (color against warm metal, eye-clean over flawless); and keep the romance while you keep it real.
6. **The try-on (M06).** Clean, light, scope ready. One observation, then silence. Read the reaction — especially the partner watching the wearer — and confirm quietly rather than closing hard.
7. **The couple dynamic (M07).** Bring the quiet partner in, reconcile the priorities, re-center the family advisor, and honor a genuine "think about it."
8. **The practical reality (M08).** Size it right, state the timeline honestly, restate the custom spec, and say the resizing limits before the sale.
9. **The second sale (M09).** Show the band with the ring, plant the upgrade, and make the referral easy — then follow up after the proposal, because that is where the next three sales are.

The capstone is not a new skill. It is the proof that the nine are one.

## The role-play: how it's scored and how to prepare

The role-play is a full appointment, observed. The scoring looks for the behaviors each module teaches, not for a memorized script. Prepare by running the appointment as a scene, three times, with a colleague playing the couple — once as a solo researcher, once as a couple with a disagreement, once with a parent in the room.

The behaviors that get scored, module by module:

- **M01/M02:** Did you discover the journey and state an agenda? Did you answer-then-bridge instead of listing times?
- **M03:** Did you ask one question at a time, and did the shape lead the setting and metal?
- **M04:** Did you keep the lab-grown conversation neutral and accurate, with no invented numbers?
- **M05:** Did you find the budget early and plainly, without judging and without killing the romance?
- **M06:** Did you clean and light the stone, make one observation, and hold the silence? Did you read the partner watching the wearer?
- **M07:** Did both people get heard? Did you reconcile rather than referee?
- **M08:** Did you size correctly and state an honest timeline with margin?
- **M09:** Did you show the band, plant the upgrade, and set up the follow-up?

Three things fail the role-play, and they are the same three things that fail the real appointment: talking through the try-on, taking a side in the couple's disagreement, and promising a timeline or a resizing that isn't true.

## The bridal conversion log

The second graded artifact is the log — a running record of real appointments. Each entry asks for the minimum that makes reflection honest:

| Field | What you record |
|---|---|
| Date and client | Who, and which journey (M01) |
| Outcome | Sold / follow-up booked / decided to think / lost — and the real reason |
| The moment | The one point where the appointment turned — the try-on, the budget ask, the band |
| What worked | The specific move that moved it |
| What to change | The specific move you'll do differently next time |

The log is where the numbers meet the craft. Conversion is the score; the "what to change" column is where the score improves. A log with honest "lost" entries and real reasons is worth more than a perfect column of "sold," because the losses are the only part that teaches.

## The performance standard, in one sentence

A bridal specialist at mastery is not the person who talks the most or closes the hardest. It is the person who runs an appointment so cleanly — discovery, guidance, honesty, silence, follow-up — that the couple feels they made the decision themselves, tells the story, and comes back for the band, the upgrade, and the next referral. That sentence is the rubric. Run the walkthrough until you can do it without thinking, then run it live and log it.

## Objections, mistakes and edge cases

| Situation | The trap | Better move |
|---|---|---|
| The appointment goes off-script | Panicking and forcing the plan | Hold the sequence's spirit, not the words; the skills, not the script |
| The role-play observer goes quiet | Filling the silence | It's the same as the try-on — let the silence work |
| The log shows a string of "lost" | Deleting the losses | Keep them; the losses are the only part that teaches |
| The couple asks something you don't know | Guessing to look fluent | Say you'll confirm with the gemologist and get back — that is M01's dignity-preserving move |
| The budget ask feels awkward in the scene | Skipping it | Do it anyway; the role-play scores the early, plain ask |
