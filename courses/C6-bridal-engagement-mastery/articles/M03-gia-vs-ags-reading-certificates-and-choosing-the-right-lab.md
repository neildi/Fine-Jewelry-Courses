# GIA vs. AGS: Reading Certificates and Choosing the Right Lab

## Why This Module Matters

Module 2 covered what cut quality actually is. This module covers how it gets measured and certified — specifically, the real and still-relevant difference between GIA and AGS grading methodology, even though AGS Laboratories itself closed as a standalone lab in 2022. Understanding this history and the current state of both systems lets you explain a certificate credibly, and — critically — helps you know when recommending an additional report genuinely serves the client rather than just adding cost.

## A Shared Origin, Then Two Different Missions

GIA (Gemological Institute of America) and AGS (American Gem Society) were both founded by the same person, gemologist Robert M. Shipley — GIA in 1931, AGS in 1934. But they were built for different purposes from the start: GIA became the trade's research and grading-standards institute, creating the diamond grading scales still used industry-wide; AGS was founded as a retailer association bound by a strict ethics code, not originally a grading lab at all.

AGS didn't open its own diamond-grading laboratory until 1996, and it did so specifically to fill a gap GIA hadn't addressed yet: a genuinely measured cut grade based on light performance, not just proportion categories. GIA didn't introduce its own cut grade — and only for round brilliants — until January 2006, a full decade after AGS Laboratories opened with exactly that as its founding purpose.

## The Core Methodological Difference: Proportion Buckets vs. Measured Light Performance

This is the single most important technical distinction in this module:

- **GIA's cut grade** is a categorical system: Excellent, Very Good, Good, Fair, or Poor. It's determined primarily through proportions (table percentage, crown angle, pavilion angle), polish, symmetry, and an observer-panel visual assessment of face-up appearance. It does not include a direct, numeric light-return measurement on the report.
- **AGS's system** measures light performance directly using ASET (Angular Spectrum Evaluation Tool) ray-tracing technology, scoring brightness, contrast, dispersion (fire), and light leakage individually, then combining them into a continuous 0-10 numeric scale (0 being the best, "AGS Ideal"). The AGS report includes an actual ASET image — a four-color light-zone map — showing exactly where and how light is entering and returning from the stone, not just a category label.

There is no clean, one-to-one conversion between the two systems. A GIA "Triple Excellent" (Excellent cut, polish, and symmetry) could correspond to an AGS grade anywhere from 0 to roughly 2, depending on what the ray-tracing actually shows — meaning two diamonds that both carry a GIA Triple Excellent designation can have genuinely different light performance if graded under the AGS system.

## What Happened in 2022: AGS Laboratories Closed, But the Methodology Didn't Disappear

In late 2022, GIA acquired AGS Laboratories' Las Vegas facility, its Performance Grading Standard intellectual property, its ASET ray-tracing methodology, and its research staff. AGS Laboratories stopped issuing its own standalone certificates at that point. But the methodology itself survived: starting in 2023, GIA began offering an "AGS Ideal Report" as a supplement that can be added to a qualifying GIA report for roughly $25 per stone. In practical terms, this means the AGS light-performance methodology is still available today — just as an add-on to a GIA report rather than as an independent competing certificate.

This is genuinely useful context to share with a client who's confused about why you'd recommend "getting AGS too" if AGS supposedly "doesn't exist anymore" — the lab closed, but the science it pioneered is still the exact same science, now delivered as a GIA supplement rather than a separate document.

## The Fancy-Shape Gap: Why This Matters More as Oval Rises

Here's a distinction with direct, practical relevance given Module 1's finding that oval is one of the fastest-rising 2026 bridal shapes: GIA has never offered an overall cut grade for fancy shapes — only for round brilliants. AGS, by contrast, extended its Performance Grading Standard to cover princess, emerald, oval, cushion, radiant, and eventually Asscher cuts, starting in the mid-2000s through the 2010s, and this fancy-shape coverage continues today through the AGS Ideal supplement.

This means for a round diamond, a GIA report alone already gives the client a genuine, if categorical, cut grade. But for an oval, cushion, or other fancy shape, a standard GIA report provides only measurements — no overall cut-quality verdict at all. The AGS Ideal supplement is, as of 2026, the only independently-measured cut credential available for these increasingly popular fancy shapes. Given that oval specifically is trending upward, this is a genuinely more relevant recommendation for a meaningfully larger share of your bridal clients than it would have been even five years ago.

## A Practical Spend-Based Guide for Recommending the Supplement

A reasonable, practical guideline for when the AGS Ideal supplement genuinely adds value versus when it's an unnecessary added cost:

- **Round natural diamonds under roughly $4,000:** A GIA Triple Excellent designation alone is generally sufficient; the added cost and complexity of the supplement isn't well-matched to the purchase size.
- **Round natural diamonds in the roughly $4,000-$15,000 range:** The supplement becomes genuinely worth recommending — the cut-quality difference is meaningful relative to the purchase price, and the AGS numeric grade can distinguish between stones that would otherwise all just say "Excellent."
- **Round diamonds above roughly $15,000, or any diamond marketed as "super ideal":** Strongly recommend the supplement and actually walk the client through the ASET image itself, not just the numeric grade — at this price point, the difference between an AGS 0 and an AGS 1 represents real money, and the image is what actually substantiates any "super ideal" marketing claim.
- **Any fancy-shape diamond, or a lab-grown round:** Recommend the supplement as the only available independent cut credential, since GIA doesn't grade fancy-shape cut at all, and (worth knowing) GIA changed its lab-grown reporting in October 2025 to no longer include the same granular 4Cs detail it provides for natural diamonds.

## What Stays the Same Between the Two Systems

It's worth clarifying to a client what does NOT differ between GIA and AGS reporting, so you're not overstating the distinction: both labs use essentially the same methodology and master stone sets for color and clarity grading, both use the same fluorescence scale, and both provide a proportion diagram and clarity plot on their full reports. The meaningful difference is specifically in cut/light-performance measurement — not in how color or clarity are assessed.

## A Worked Scenario: Explaining Why "AGS" Still Matters After the Lab Closed

A client researching online asks, "I read that AGS closed — so why are you telling me to get an AGS report?" The clear, honest answer: "You're right that AGS Laboratories stopped issuing its own separate certificates in 2022 — GIA actually bought their light-performance technology and research team. But the science itself didn't go away; GIA now offers it as an add-on called the AGS Ideal Report, right alongside your regular GIA certificate. So you're not getting a competing opinion from a different company anymore — you're getting the same trusted measurement technology, just delivered as part of the same document instead of a separate one. For a stone at this price point, I'd genuinely recommend it, because it's the only way to actually see how much light is coming back out of this specific diamond, not just which quality category it falls into."

## Key Takeaways

- GIA and AGS share a common founder (Robert Shipley) but were built for different original purposes — GIA as the trade's research/grading institute, AGS originally as a retailer-ethics association that later added its own diamond lab in 1996.
- GIA's cut grade is a categorical proportion-and-appearance system (Excellent through Poor); AGS's system directly measures light performance via ASET ray-tracing on a continuous 0-10 scale, with no clean one-to-one conversion between the two.
- AGS Laboratories closed as a standalone lab in 2022, but its methodology survives as GIA's "AGS Ideal Report" supplement (~$25), meaning the science didn't disappear — it changed how it's delivered.
- GIA has never offered an overall cut grade for fancy shapes (only round brilliants); AGS's methodology covers princess, emerald, oval, cushion, radiant, and Asscher — a genuinely more relevant gap to know given oval's current rise in popularity.
- Use a spend-based guideline for recommending the AGS Ideal supplement: skip it for modest round-diamond purchases, actively recommend it as spend increases, and treat it as the only available independent cut credential for fancy shapes or lab-grown rounds.
