# The Custom Design and CAD Workflow for Bridal

## Why This Module Matters

A custom engagement ring is often the single highest-emotion, highest-stakes purchase a client will make in the store, and it unfolds over weeks rather than minutes — which means the associate's job shifts from a single closing conversation to managing a multi-stage process where expectations, sign-offs, and timeline communication determine whether the client feels confident or anxious throughout. This module walks through that process stage by stage, with particular attention to the CAD approval step, where a genuinely important but easy-to-miss detail — that approval locks the file — needs to be communicated clearly.

## The Five-Stage Workflow

Custom bridal orders consistently follow the same core sequence across the sources reviewed, even though naming and exact timing vary somewhat by jeweler:

1. **Initial consultation and design brief** (typically 30 minutes to 2 hours): The associate captures the client's vision, budget, stone preferences, and any specific inspiration references, and this gets translated into a written design brief — center stone specs, metal type, band style, sizing, and any engraving. This brief becomes the reference point for everything that follows.
2. **Design and CAD rendering** (roughly 3 days to 2 weeks): A CAD designer builds a 3D digital model from the brief, typically producing both a technical wireframe (exact measurements, used to verify proportions and metal weight) and a photorealistic shaded render (used to help the client visualize the finished look). Some jewelers also provide an interactive 360-degree view.
3. **Revision rounds** (typically 3-7 days per round): The client reviews the render and requests changes — proportion adjustments, stone placement, band width, and so on. Most custom orders resolve within one to two revision rounds, though more complex or indecisive projects can take longer.
4. **Final sign-off and production** (casting through finishing typically takes 2-6 weeks): Once the client formally approves the file, it moves to casting, stone setting, and hand-finishing. Some jewelers include an optional wax or 3D-printed physical prototype for the client to hold and approve before metal casting begins — a genuinely valuable extra checkpoint for complex or expensive designs.
5. **Final inspection and delivery** (typically 2-7 days): The finished piece undergoes quality control — checking stone security, sizing accuracy, and overall finish — before the client is contacted for pickup.

## The Sign-Off Detail Worth Getting Right

Here's the part of this process that's easy to gloss over but genuinely important to communicate clearly: once a client formally approves the CAD file, that approval locks the specifications. Prong height, band profile, metal weight, and engraving placement all become fixed at that point, and any further changes typically require rework, added cost, or added time, rather than being a simple in-flight adjustment. This is why the CAD review itself deserves real attention rather than a quick glance — before presenting a file for approval, the file should be checked in a specific order: stone size and shape against the actual order (not a placeholder), overall proportions against the ring size, prong placement and count against the stone's shape, rendered metal weight against the quote, stone seating depth from a cross-section view, shank taper and gallery clearance from an underside view, and engraving space, before finally reviewing the face-up aesthetic view. Walking a client through this same sequence — rather than just asking "does it look good?" — helps them catch problems that a face-up-only view can hide, like an underweight-looking shank or a stone seated too shallow.

## Setting Timeline Expectations Honestly

The most commonly cited total range across sources is roughly four to twelve weeks from first consultation to finished ring, with four to six weeks being the figure most frequently cited as typical for a moderately complex custom engagement ring; simpler designs can move faster, and more elaborate designs with multiple revision rounds or hand-fabrication elements can extend well past two months. Fabrication (casting, setting, finishing) is consistently the most time-intensive single phase, generally running two to six weeks on its own. Given how often bridal purchases are tied to a specific date — an anniversary, a planned trip, a proposal deadline — setting this range honestly at the very first consultation, and explicitly building in buffer time for at least one revision round, is far better than promising an aggressive timeline that then creates pressure or disappointment later. This connects directly to Module 6's sizing content and will connect further to Module 8's proposal-logistics content: a custom order with a fixed deadline needs the sizing and CAD-approval steps to move efficiently precisely because there's less slack in the schedule.

## A Worked Scenario: Managing the Anxious Custom-Design Client

A client three weeks into a custom design process calls, anxious, because their proposal is in ten days and they haven't yet approved the CAD file. Rather than making vague reassurances, it's worth being specific and structured: "Let's look at exactly where we are — you've seen two rounds of revisions, and the current file addresses everything you flagged last time. If you can review and approve today, that gives production eight full days, which is enough time for casting and setting on this design since it doesn't need hand-fabrication. I'd recommend we get that sign-off locked in today rather than requesting another small tweak, since additional changes at this point would push past your date. Take twenty minutes now to look at it closely — I'll walk through the file with you on a call if that helps." This gives the client a clear, honest picture of exactly where the timeline stands and what needs to happen next, rather than either false reassurance or unstructured worry.

## Key Takeaways

- The custom bridal workflow moves through five consistent stages: consultation/brief, CAD design, revision rounds, sign-off/production, and final inspection/delivery.
- Most custom orders resolve within one to two revision rounds; more rounds are possible but extend the timeline meaningfully.
- CAD sign-off locks the file's specifications — communicate this clearly before the client approves, since post-approval changes typically mean added cost and time, not a quick fix.
- Review CAD files in a specific technical order (stone specs, proportions, prongs, metal weight, seating depth, shank/gallery, engraving) before the aesthetic face-up view, and walk clients through this same sequence rather than asking only "does it look good?"
- The realistic total timeline is roughly four to twelve weeks, with four to six weeks typical for moderate complexity; set this expectation honestly at the first consultation, especially when a client has a fixed target date.
