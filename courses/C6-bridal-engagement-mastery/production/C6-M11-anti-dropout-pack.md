### 3-Question Diagnostic Pre-Check

Instruct learners to answer honestly before viewing lesson content. Scoring key is revealed only after all three questions are answered.

**Question 1: The Silent Reaction**
A couple is looking at a $6,000 tennis bracelet. She says "I love it." He says nothing and just nods slightly while looking at the price tag.

- A) Address her directly: "It looks beautiful on you — should we go ahead and wrap this up?"
- B) Turn to him and ask: "What do you think — is this the one, or should we look at a couple more?"
- C) Say to both: "I can see you both are drawn to different things here — what's most important to each of you about this piece?"

**Question 2: The Verbal Disagreement**
She says, "This is exactly what I want." He says, "I think we should look at something smaller." Tension is visible.

- A) Side with her since she's the one who will wear it: "Well, it's her ring, so ultimately it's her choice."
- B) Try to smooth it over quickly: "No problem, let's look at a few more options in different sizes."
- C) Acknowledge both positions explicitly, then reframe around shared criteria: "It sounds like you both want this to feel special and to feel right long-term — let's find where those two things meet."
- D) Stay quiet and let them argue it out while you wait nearby.

**Question 3: The Divided Agenda**
He tells you privately at the counter, "I want to surprise her, don't tell her the price." Later she asks you directly, "How much is this?"

- A) Answer her honestly since she asked directly: state the price.
- B) Deflect vaguely: "Oh, it's a great value for what you're getting."
- C) Redirect without lying or breaking his trust: "That's a great question for the two of you to talk through together — let me give you two a moment."

## Section 2: The Dual-Buyer Alignment Protocol™ — 3 Branching Decision Scenarios

The Dual-Buyer Alignment Protocol™ is built on one core principle: in a two-person purchase decision, the associate's job is not to persuade the loudest voice — it is to surface, name, and align both parties' unspoken criteria into one shared decision. Each scenario below has three response paths.

### Scenario 1: The Silent Partner

**Setup:** She is actively engaged, trying on rings, asking questions. He stands slightly back, arms crossed, checking his phone occasionally, saying almost nothing.

**Option A — Amateur Execution**
Verbatim: "So what do you think — should we get this one for her?"
*Psychological Analysis:* This directly puts the silent partner on the spot in front of his partner, which triggers defensiveness rather than openness. Silence in a jewelry setting is rarely disengagement — it's usually financial calculation, social discomfort, or deference. Calling it out publicly forces a yes/no answer under social pressure, which often produces a stall ("let's think about it") rather than a real answer.

**Option B — Polite Stall**
Verbatim: "Take your time, no pressure at all — I'll just be right over here if you need me."
*Psychological Analysis:* This avoids the risk of confrontation but abandons the sale process entirely. It fails to gather any information about the silent partner's actual concerns (budget ceiling, ring style opinion, timing). Without intervention, the silent partner's unspoken objection (usually price-related) never surfaces, and the couple leaves to "think about it" — which converts at a very low rate.

**Option C — Master Execution**
Verbatim, delivered privately to him while she continues browsing: "I noticed you've been taking this in from over here — I do that too when I'm the one thinking through the practical side of things. Can I ask, is there something specific on your mind — budget, style, or timing — that would help me point you both toward the right piece?"
*Psychological Analysis:* This approach does three things simultaneously. It normalizes his silence rather than shaming it ("I do that too"), which lowers defensiveness. It gives him a low-cost, private channel to voice the real objection without losing face in front of his partner. And it offers three concrete categories (budget, style, timing) so he doesn't have to articulate a vague feeling — he just has to pick a lane. This typically surfaces the true blocker within 15-20 seconds.

### Scenario 2: Direct Conflict

**Setup:** She says, "I love the halo setting." He says, "I think the solitaire looks more classic — I don't love all the extra diamonds." Voices are calm but the disagreement is clearly unresolved.

**Option A — Amateur Execution**
Verbatim: "Well, she's the one who'll be wearing it every day, so her opinion probably matters most here."
*Psychological Analysis:* Taking a side — even the "obvious" one — makes the associate an adversary to whichever party they didn't back. It also removes the couple's autonomy over their own decision, which can create resentment even if it appears to speed up the sale. Deals closed this way have a disproportionately high return-and-refund rate once the sidelined partner reconsiders at home.

**Option B — Polite Stall**
Verbatim: "These are both beautiful — why don't you two talk it over and let me know what you land on?"
*Psychological Analysis:* This avoids taking sides but also avoids doing the associate's actual job: guiding a resolution. Left alone, disagreements often escalate quietly or the couple defaults to "let's come back later," which rarely happens. The associate has surrendered the one moment they had leverage to add value.

**Option C — Master Execution**
Verbatim: "It sounds like you're both after the same thing from a different angle — she wants something that feels a little more special day to day, and you want something that ages well and feels timeless. Those aren't actually opposites. What if we looked at a solitaire with a subtly detailed band, or a halo with a slightly lower profile? That might give you both what you're really after."
*Psychological Analysis:* This is a classic reframe-to-shared-values technique. Instead of treating the disagreement as style A versus style B, it names the underlying value each person is protecting (specialness vs. timelessness) and shows those values are compatible, not competing. This shifts the conversation from a win/lose negotiation between partners to a collaborative search — with the associate positioned as the ally solving the puzzle, not the tiebreaker choosing a winner.

### Scenario 3: The Split Agenda

**Setup:** He quietly tells you he wants to surprise her and asks you not to reveal the price. Minutes later, she turns to you directly and asks, "So how much is this one?"

**Option A — Amateur Execution**
Verbatim: "It's $4,800."
*Psychological Analysis:* Answering directly honors her request for transparency but breaks the private agreement made with him moments earlier, damaging trust with the person actually financing the purchase. Associates who default to "answer whoever asks" often lose repeat and referral business from the paying partner, who feels exposed or undermined in front of their significant other.

**Option B — Polite Stall**
Verbatim: "Oh, it's really competitively priced for the quality you're getting."
*Psychological Analysis:* This avoids breaking confidence but is transparently evasive — most customers immediately recognize a dodge, which erodes trust in the associate's honesty generally, not just on this one point. It also fails to actually solve the underlying tension, leaving both parties slightly unsatisfied.

**Option C — Master Execution**
Verbatim, said warmly to both: "That's actually a really good conversation for the two of you to have together — I'll step away for just a minute and let you two chat, then I'll come right back and answer anything you'd like."
*Psychological Analysis:* This resolves the conflict without lying to either party or betraying a confidence. It reframes the price question as a couple's decision rather than an associate's disclosure, which respects both the surprise-giver's intent and the direct-asker's right to information — she simply gets the answer from him, not from staff. Physically stepping away also removes the associate as the pressure point, letting the couple self-resolve while signaling discretion and professionalism.

## Section 3: The 1-Page Countertop Laminated Field Guide

**JEWELSWELL FIELD GUIDE — DUAL-BUYER ALIGNMENT™ (Laminate at counter height, front-facing to associate)**

**Triangular Eye Contact**
When speaking with a couple, mentally divide your attention across three points: her eyes, his eyes, and the piece itself. Rotate through all three roughly every 8-10 seconds during key moments (price reveal, style choice, closing). Never let one partner receive more than 60% of your direct eye contact during a shared decision — this visually signals you see this as a joint decision, not a one-on-one sale.

**Velvet Pad Placement**
Always place the velvet pad at the exact midpoint between the two customers, never closer to one side. Angle the piece on the pad so it is equally viewable from both positions, not tilted toward whoever is holding the wallet or whoever spoke first.

**5 Memorized Phrases**
- "What's most important to each of you about this piece?"
- "It sounds like you're both after the same thing from different angles."
- "That's a great conversation for the two of you to have together."
- "Take a moment — I want you to feel confident about this together."
- "Is there anything on your mind — budget, style, or timing — that would help me guide you both?"

**3 Banned Phrases**
- "Well, ultimately it's her/his choice."
- "Don't worry about the price."
- "Just trust me, this is the one."

**60-Second Recovery Protocol (use if tension escalates mid-sale)**
1. (0-10 sec) Pause. Do not speak. Set the piece down on the velvet pad, hands visibly open and away from it.
2. (10-25 sec) Name the shared goal aloud: "I can tell you both want to get this right."
3. (25-45 sec) Offer a graceful exit: "Why don't I give you two a minute, and I'll come right back."
4. (45-60 sec) Physically step back at least three feet and busy yourself briefly with a neutral task (polishing cloth, adjusting the case) before returning.

## Section 4: Post-Lesson Spaced Repetition Drip Cadence

### Day 2 — Retrieval Quiz (5 Questions)

**1. What is the primary function of the associate in a dual-buyer scenario?**
Answer: To surface and align both parties' unspoken criteria into one shared decision, not to persuade the loudest voice. *Explanation: This is the foundational principle of the entire protocol — siding with either party undermines the joint decision.*

**2. In the Silent Partner scenario, what does silence usually indicate?**
Answer: Financial calculation, social discomfort, or deference — not disengagement. *Explanation: Treating silence as disinterest leads associates to ignore the very person holding the real objection.*

**3. Name one of the three categories offered to a silent partner to help them voice a concern without losing face.**
Answer: Budget, style, or timing. *Explanation: Offering categories lowers the emotional cost of speaking up compared to asking an open-ended "what's wrong?"*

**4. In the Direct Conflict scenario, what technique reframes a style disagreement into a shared search?**
Answer: Naming the underlying value each partner is protecting (e.g., specialness vs. timelessness) and showing they're compatible. *Explanation: This moves the associate from tiebreaker to ally, avoiding the resentment that comes from picking a side.*

**5. In the Split Agenda scenario, what should an associate do when asked a question that would break a confidence shared by the other partner?**
Answer: Redirect the question back to the couple privately rather than answering directly or deflecting vaguely. *Explanation: This preserves trust with both parties without lying to either one.*
