## Why this course exists

GIA's Colored Stones course ships with a bibliography, not a textbook: a per-assignment list of *Gems & Gemology* articles ("optional; you will not be tested on it") that represents 40+ years of field gemology, treatment forensics and locality research — from Keller's 1981 field reports on Colombian emeralds and the 1980s Fritsch–Rossman color-science trilogy, to the 2003 beryllium-diffusion scandal, the 2009 Mozambique ruby discovery, the 2010s Ethiopian emerald and Madagascar sapphire rushes, and GIA's ongoing photomicrography of the Micro-World. Most students never read it. This course reads it for you — it fetches the flagship articles of each assignment, digests them, and teaches colored stones the way the research itself does it: formation and species first, value factors second, locality and treatment evidence third.

Where C1's M06 gave you a hardness/treatment/care table for the ten most-sold stones, C17 gives you the science behind those numbers — the geochemistry of why rubies from Mogok look different from those from Winza or Montepuez; why emerald oiling is graded F1/F2/F3 and what that means for durability; why beryllium diffusion requires disclosure; how pleochroism changes what a client sees in tanzanite and iolite; why a Kashmir sapphire's velvet color is tied to submicroscopic inclusions; and how the lab reads inclusions to tell natural from synthetic, heated from unheated, filled from clean.

## Terminal outcomes

The learner can:
- Narrate the geologic origin of each major colored stone species and connect locality to typical color, inclusions and value
- Identify and explain color-causing mechanisms (transition-metal ions, charge transfer, color centers, band gaps, physical phenomena) and their visible consequences, including pleochroism and phenomenal effects (asterism, chatoyancy, adularescence, play-of-color, iridescence, alexandrite effect)
- Read inclusions as fingerprints of origin and treatment and know when to refer a stone to a lab
- Explain every commercially significant colored-stone treatment — heat, diffusion (beryllium, cobalt, titanium), lead-glass filling, emerald oil/resin, irradiation, coating, dyeing, bleaching/polymer impregnation, sugar/acid — and apply FTC-style disclosure rules to a counter conversation
- Identify synthetics, imitations and assembled stones (flux/hydrothermal corundum and beryl, synthetic opal, synthetic quartz, doublets/triplets, glass/plastic imitations) using the detection features the G&G literature established
- Give species, source, typical treatment, care and a client-sayable story for each major colored stone: ruby, sapphire (blue and fancy), emerald, and the secondary species covered in C1 M06
- Explain pearl culture types, value factors, common treatments and how the lab distinguishes natural vs. cultured
- Explain the jadeite vs. nephrite distinction, the Type A/B/C treatment language, and the cultural and market context of jadeite in East Asia
- Read any future *Gems & Gemology* colored-stone article with the vocabulary to understand it, using the course bibliography as the entry point

## Production rules

Same six-step SOP as C1 and C16: outline → media research (verified real sources only, links never re-hosted) → full article → self-critique → fact-check table → quiz + huddle card. No AI-generated imagery or video; all media are real GIA/creator works verified live before inclusion. Flagged items use `[VERIFY]`.

**Depth policy:** C16-level gemological rigor (mechanism first, vocabulary second; cite the primary research paper; do not dumb down the science) paired with C1-style floor readiness — every module ends in "on the floor: applying it this week" scripts, an objections table, and a self-check that a manager can observe on the sales floor.

**Read-level honesty:** ★ entries in `references/annotated-bibliography.md` are sources whose text was read during production; unstarred annotations are title/abstract-level by design (context budget). Articles and fact-checks carry inline [VERIFY] flags for every such claim — work them before public publication.
