# Annotated Bibliography — GIA "Colored Stones" Further Reading

This is the complete reading list from GIA's official *For Further Reading — Colored Stones* handout (the PDF distributed with the GIA Colored Stones course, current version at `https://www.gia.edu/dam/jcr:405b84e3-6df0-40fb-858b-35f84ada7728/Colored-Stones.pdf`), re-set here with annotations. It is the spine of this course: every module cites from it, and every learner can follow the original sources — all of which GIA makes available free on gia.edu.

**How to use this file**
- **★ = digest-grade source.** Full text (or a substantial extract) will be fetched during course production and used in the corresponding module. Its annotation states what the article actually says.
- ⚠ = article whose URL is broken or redirected in GIA's current site; annotation is based on title, journal volume/issue and cross-references. Verify before quoting specifics.
- Unmarked entries: annotation is based on the citation's title, journal volume/issue, and assignment context. The article remains free to download from GIA; the annotation tells you what it covers and which module pairs with it.
- The handout itself notes: *"This reading is optional. You will not be tested on the content of these articles."* This course changes that — it treats the list as the core curriculum.

G&G = *Gems & Gemology*, GIA's quarterly peer-reviewed professional journal. All G&G articles are free to download at gia.edu/gems-gemology; Research & News articles live at gia.edu/gia-news-research.

## Assignment 2: Gemstone Formation and Mining → Course Modules 2–3

### Gems from volcanic rocks (basalt-hosted corundum, peridot, red beryl)
- ★ **Thuyet N.T.M. et al. (2016) Peridot from the Central Highlands of Vietnam: Properties, Origin, and Formation.** G&G 52:3 — https://www.gia.edu/gems-gemology/fall-2016-peridot-central-highlands-vietnam-properties-origin-formation — Peridot in lherzolite xenoliths from basalt; geologic context for a peridot source that supplies much of the Asian market.
- ★ **Shigley J.E. et al. (2003) Red Beryl from Utah: A Review and Update.** G&G 39:4 — https://www.gia.edu/gems-gemology/winter-2003-red-beryl-utah-shigley — The Wah Wah Mountains violet-red beryl (bixbite): one of the rarest gem species on Earth; rhyolite-hosted formation vs. the pegmatite-hosted emerald/aquamarine/morganite family.
- ★ **Levinson A.A., Cook F.A. (1994) Gem Corundum in Alkali Basalt: Origin and Occurrence.** G&G 30:4 — https://www.gia.edu/gems-gemology/winter-1994-corundum-occurence-levinson — THE reference for basalt-hosted sapphire (Thailand/Cambodia, Australia, Nigeria, China, Vietnam basalt fields); explains why basalt sapphires typically show darker blues and higher iron content than metamorphic sapphires.

### Gems from pegmatites
- **Proctor K. (1985) Gem Pegmatites of Minas Gerais, Brazil: The Tourmalines of the Governador Valadares District.** G&G 21:2 — https://www.gia.edu/gems-gemology/summer-1985-tourmaline-brazil-proctor — Classic pegmatite tourmaline localities; sets up Paraíba-type cuprian tourmaline discovery context.
- ★ **Shigley J.E., Kampf A.R. (1984) Gem-Bearing Pegmatites: A Review.** G&G 20:2 — https://www.gia.edu/gems-gemology/summer-1984-pegmatite-shigley — Pegmatite formation model: why late-stage granitic fluids yield tourmaline, kunzite, beryl (aquamarine, morganite, heliodor), topaz and spessartine garnet in sizeable gem crystals.

### Gems from hydrothermal deposits
- **Keller P.C. (1983) The Capão Topaz Deposit, Ouro Preto, Minas Gerais, Brazil.** G&G 19:1 — https://www.gia.edu/gems-gemology/spring-1983-topaz-brazil-keller — Imperial topaz from Ouro Preto: hydrothermal veins in itabirite (iron formation); why imperial topaz remains a single-source rarity alongside tanzanite.

### Gems from metamorphic rocks
- ★ **Smith C.P. et al. (1997) Rubies and Fancy-Color Sapphires from Nepal.** G&G 33:1 — https://www.gia.edu/gems-gemology/spring-1997-rubies-fancy-color-sapphires-nepal-smith — Marble-hosted ruby from the Himalayan orogeny; the metamorphic (marble) vs. basalt corundum dichotomy that explains why Burmese/Mogok and Kashmir/Vietnamese rubies differ chemically from Thai/Cambodian/Australian basalt sapphires.

### Gems from sedimentary rocks
- **Wise R.W. (1993) Queensland Boulder Opal.** G&G 29:1 — https://www.gia.edu/gems-gemology/spring-1993-queensland-boulder-opal-wise — Sedimentary (weathered Cretaceous sandstone) opal formation; how opal's play-of-color forms in ironstone concretions.

- ★ **Fritsch E., Rossman G.R. (1987/1988) An Update on Color in Gems, Parts 1, 2, and 3.** G&G 23:3, 24:1, 24:2 — https://www.gia.edu/gems-gemology/fall-1987-color-gems-fritsch ; https://www.gia.edu/gems-gemology/spring-1988-gem-color-fritsch ; https://www.gia.edu/gems-gemology/summer-1988-color-gems-fritsch — THE color-science trilogy for gemologists. Part 1: dispersed metal ions (Cr, Fe, Mn, V, Co, Ni, Cu) — idiochromatic vs. allochromatic species, ruby emerald alexandrite from Cr; Part 2: charge transfer, multiple atoms and color centers (sapphire Fe-TIntervalence, Maxixe-type beryl, smoky quartz); Part 3: band gaps and physical phenomena (diamond, blue diamond boron acceptor, play-of-color in opal, iridescence, asterism, chatoyancy, labradorescence). Anchor of M05.
