# M01 Quiz — Before the Grader

10 multiple-choice questions testing the terminal outcome. Answer key with one-sentence explanation at the end. Do not test trivia; test what changes behavior on the floor.

---

**1. Why is there no universal D-to-Z equivalent for colored-stone color grading?**
a) Colored stones are too rare to support a standardized system.
b) Color in colored stones is the primary value driver and is described per species using hue/tone/saturation, not letter grades against diamond masterstones.
c) GIA chose not to extend the D-to-Z scale to colored stones to avoid consumer confusion.
d) Colored stones cannot be color-graded objectively.

**2. Which of the following is NOT one of the four structural differences between colored-stone grading and diamond grading described in this module?**
a) Dozens of species instead of one mineral species.
b) Color is the primary value driver, not a demerit.
c) Treatments are the commercial norm for many species and must be disclosed.
d) Colored stones cannot be weighed in carats.

**3. A client asks if their emerald is "VVS1." What is the best response?**
a) "Yes, emeralds can be VVS1 just like diamonds."
b) "We don't use the VVS scale the same way for emeralds — emeralds are expected to have inclusions, and GIA reports describe clarity and clarity enhancement (F1/F2/F3) rather than VVS/VS."
c) "VVS1 means the emerald is oiled."
d) "If it has no inclusions visible to the eye, it is VVS1."

**4. Which GIA resource is the right first stop for a quick species question such as "how hard is peridot?"**
a) *Gems & Gemology* peer-reviewed archive.
b) GIA Research & News.
c) GIA Gem Encyclopedia.
d) GIA Library.

**5. Which GIA resource should you use to find detection features for a treatment such as beryllium diffusion?**
a) *Gems & Gemology* peer-reviewed research articles.
b) GIA Gem Encyclopedia.
c) GIA Library search only.
d) Instagram.

**6. What was the industry consequence of the 2001–2003 beryllium-diffusion "scandal" in corundum?**
a) Beryllium diffusion was banned worldwide.
b) All orange sapphires were removed from the market.
c) The treatment was identified technically and became a legitimate, disclosed trade practice, resetting colored-stone disclosure norms.
d) GIA stopped issuing origin reports for sapphire.

**7. Which three colored stones are traditionally called the "Big Three" in the gem trade?**
a) Diamond, ruby, sapphire.
b) Ruby, sapphire, emerald.
c) Emerald, jade, pearl.
d) Tanzanite, tsavorite, tourmaline.

**8. A client brings in a family heirloom her grandmother called a Kashmir sapphire. The best response is:**
a) "If Grandma said so, it must be Kashmir — those are extremely valuable."
b) "Kashmir sapphires haven't been mined in decades, so that's almost certainly not one."
c) "That's a wonderful family story. Origin is determined in a lab, not at the counter — I'd recommend an identification and origin report before speaking to value."
d) "Kashmir sapphires are all heat-treated, so the origin doesn't matter."

**9. If a direct *Gems & Gemology* article URL returns 404 after a GIA site migration, where should you look first?**
a) Google Images.
b) GIA Library (gia.edu/library).
c) A third-party gemology forum.
d) The GIA 4Cs site.

**10. The question "Is this stone natural?" is insufficient for a colored stone. What is the necessary follow-up?**
a) "Is it synthetic?"
b) "Has it been treated, and if so, by what method — and what is its geographic origin?"
c) "What is its carat weight?"
d) "Where did you buy it?"

---

## Answer key

1. **b** — Colored stones use a descriptive (hue/tone/saturation) system per species because color itself is the value.
2. **d** — Colored stones are weighed in carats, same as diamonds; the differences are species diversity, color-as-value, treatments as norm, and origin as price factor.
3. **b** — Emerald clarity is described against the stone's own expectation (with jardin typical) and clarity-enhancement grade (F1/F2/F3), not against the diamond clarity scale.
4. **c** — The Gem Encyclopedia is the two-minute first stop for basic species questions.
5. **a** — Treatment-detection features are published in peer-reviewed G&G articles (Emmett, McClure, etc.).
6. **c** — Beryllium diffusion is now a legitimate, disclosed practice rather than a banned one; the scandal was about non-disclosure.
7. **b** — Ruby, sapphire and emerald are the traditional "Big Three" colored stones.
8. **c** — Never confirm or deny a family origin story from the counter; refer to the lab for an origin report.
9. **b** — The GIA Library is the search portal for all GIA content, including articles whose direct URLs have been retired.
10. **b** — "Natural species" is not enough; treatment status (method, stability, disclosure) and origin (where applicable) are the additional pieces that actually matter to value and care.
