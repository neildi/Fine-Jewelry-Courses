## Title
**Color and Light: How Gems Get Their Color — and Why Lighting Changes Everything**

## Scenario
A client picks out a tanzanite in the store's halogen spotlights — a vivid violet-blue. At dinner in candlelight it looks purple-gray; at her office under fluorescent it looks flat and brownish. She brings it back and says "you sold me the wrong stone." You didn't — but no one showed her the stone in multiple lights before she bought it. This module is why.

## H2 outline
1. The tanzanite that changed color (hook ~200w)
2. Light is color: a 60-second physics reminder (~350w). Visible spectrum ~380–750 nm; white light mix; selective absorption (gem absorbs some wavelengths, transmits/reflects others; the mix we see is the gem's color); transmission vs. reflection; why "color is not in the stone — color is the light the stone does not absorb." Introduce the spectroscope concept (a gemologist's tool for seeing which wavelengths are absorbed) without overcomplicating.
3. The five color-causing mechanisms (~900w). Following Fritsch-Rossman trilogy structure:
   - (a) Idiochromatic vs allochromatic gems and transition-metal ions ("self-colored" — peridot from iron, malachite from copper, rhodochrosite from manganese — vs "other-colored," where trace impurities provide color: ruby/emerald from Cr, sapphire from Fe+Ti, tanzanite from vanadium, aquamarine from Fe, morganite from Mn, amethyst from iron color centers).
   - (b) Charge transfer (intervalence) — Fe²⁺+Ti⁴⁺ blue sapphire; other CT pairs.
   - (c) Color centers — irradiation-induced electron traps: Maxixe beryl, smoky quartz, irradiated blue topaz, some amethyst, some yellow sapphire.
   - (d) Band theory and semiconductor band gaps — diamond's conduction/valence gap; blue diamond from boron acceptor; colored diamond mechanisms (brief — diamonds are C16, but color in diamond briefly noted for cross-reference).
   - (e) Physical phenomena — dispersion ("fire"), scattering (moonstone adularescence, aventurescence), interference/diffraction (opal play-of-color, iridescence, labradorescence), inclusions producing asterism/chatoyancy (rutile silk).
4. The four optical effects that change what the client sees (~500w):
   - Pleochroism (Hughes 2014): dichroism (2 colors, uniaxial — ruby, sapphire, emerald, aquamarine, tourmaline, quartz, zircon) vs trichroism (3 colors, biaxial — tanzanite, iolite, andalusite, alexandrite chrysoberyl, kunzite, peridot). How pleochroism affects orientation at the cutter's wheel (tanzanite must be cut with the table perpendicular to the optic axis to show blue rather than purple/brown).
   - Alexandrite effect (color-change gemstones): chrysoberyl (classic alexandrite), color-change garnet (vanadium), color-change sapphire, color-change diaspore (zultanite) — different transmission windows cause the stone to preferentially transmit red (incandescent) vs green/blue (daylight).
   - Metamerism: two objects match under one light source but not another — why your showcase lighting matters.
   - Fluorescence (brief): Cr in ruby and red spinel causes red fluorescence under LW UV; fluorescence is why Burmese rubies "glow" in natural daylight vs Thai/Cambodian basalt rubies that don't.
5. Phenomenal stones: stars, cats, moonstone, opal, sunstone (~500w) — Bohannon 2016 anchor.
   - Asterism: rutile silk needles oriented in three directions (120° apart in basal plane) in corundum; en cabochon perpendicular to c-axis; 6-ray stars (most) vs 12-ray (from two needle sets in same stone); dual-color double stars (Schmetzer 2015 briefly).
   - Chatoyancy (cat's eye): parallel needles/tubes/fibers in chrysoberyl (the classic cat's-eye), tourmaline, quartz, apatite, scapolite; single bright band; orientation matters.
   - Adularescence (moonstone): alternating albite/orthoclase layers scatter blue/white light; schiller.
   - Aventurescence: platy inclusions reflect light — sunstone (copper platelets in Oregon feldspar; hematite in Indian sunstone); aventurine quartz (fuchsite mica).
   - Play-of-color (precious opal): silica spheres 150–300 nm diffract visible light; spheres uniform → red flash; small → blue flash; chaotic → no play.
   - Iridescence / labradorescence: thin-film interference in labradorite, iris agate, pearl nacre.
6. Describing color consistently: GIA hue/tone/saturation (Munsell-derived) (~350w). Hue (position on color wheel — red, orange, yellow, green, blue, violet plus intermediates), tone (light to dark), saturation (intensity/grayness). Why "cornflower blue" is metaphor, not a grade; GIA's colored-stone color descriptions use controlled vocabulary. Metamerism in action: two sapphires match in store but not in her car. Light source recommendation: show any colored stone in daylight equivalent (6500K) before closing, not just under warm halogen.
