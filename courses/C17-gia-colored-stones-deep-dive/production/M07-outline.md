## Title
**Cut: Orienting Color, Weight Retention, and Why Colored Stones Are Not Diamonds**

## Scenario
A client compares two 2 ct oval blue sapphires — one is bright and faces up well with a slight window at certain angles, the other is dark and lifeless-looking in daylight even though it has no visible window and has "better" proportions on paper. The darker stone has a better cut grade by diamond standards. You explain that colored-stone cut is judged by how the stone faces up for color, not by an ideal proportion chart.

## H2 outline
1. The two sapphire ovals (hook ~200w)
2. Colored-stone cut is not diamond cut (~400w). Diamonds are cut for brilliance/light return (ideal proportions maximize sparkle). Colored stones are cut to maximize **face-up color** — the best color is the objective, and proportion is sacrificed when it must be. Rough is expensive; cutters balance three competing goals: best face-up color, best weight retention (which heavily affects price), and reasonable symmetry/polish. Gilbertson (2013) "Optimizing Face-Up Appearance in Colored Gemstone Faceting" is the anchor.
3. Orientation: the cutter's biggest decision (~500w). Rough crystal shape, cleavage, pleochroism, color zoning, inclusions, and silk orientation all influence orientation. The cutter must decide where to place the table and culet, often sacrificing weight:
   - Pleochroic stones (tanzanite, iolite, andalusite, tourmaline, kunzite) are oriented with the table perpendicular to the optic axis to show the best color face-up.
   - Tourmaline has strong dichroism — dark/light along the c-axis; cutters typically orient the table slightly off the c-axis to avoid an overly dark face-up appearance (tourmaline is commonly cut "fatter" perpendicular to c).
   - Star/cat's-eye stones must be oriented with cabochon base perpendicular to c-axis to center the star/eye on the dome; cutting off-axis produces a sliding star.
   - Color-zoned stones (amethyst, ametrine, blue sapphire with zoning, watermelon tourmaline) are oriented to put the best color on the table and hide unattractive zones in the pavilion.
   - Dark stones (Thai/Australian basalt sapphire) may be cut shallower to lighten face-up color; light stones (pale Ceylon sapphire, pale amethyst) may be cut deeper to saturate color. This is not cheating — it is proper colored-stone cutting.
   - High-value per-carat stones (ruby, emerald, Padparadscha, alexandrite, Paraíba) carry enormous weight-retention pressure — cutters often leave extra weight on the pavilion/belly or in a "native cut" to preserve carat weight, even when it hurts appearance. Recutting an overheavy stone loses weight but gains appearance (a trade-off the owner must understand).
4. Face-up appearance: what to look for (~600w) — Gilbertson's framework.
   - **Color coverage:** does the stone look evenly colored face-up, or is there a "washed-out" center?
   - **Windowing (a "window" or "fish-eye"):** a transparent "see-through" area in the center where light passes straight through the pavilion instead of reflecting back to the viewer. Caused by pavilion angles that are too shallow — light leaks out. Some windowing is common in commercial colored stones cut for weight retention; a small window in a pale stone is acceptable; a large window in an expensive stone is a cut fault.
   - **Extinction:** dark, dead areas in the stone when viewed face-up, usually where the stone is cut too deep or where bodycolor is so dark the stone closes off. Thai/Australian dark blue sapphires often show heavy extinction. Some extinction is normal — colored stones are not expected to "return light" everywhere the way an ideal-cut diamond does.
   - **Over-dark / over-light stones:** a stone that closes up to near-black in daylight (over-dark — common in dark basalt sapphires, dark tourmalines, deep garnets) versus a stone that faces up pale (under-saturated from being cut too shallow or from pale rough).
   - **Symmetry:** is the table centered? Is the girdle even? Are facets meeting at points? Do the culet and table line up? Poor symmetry is visible to the eye and devalues the stone.
   - **Polish:** facet surfaces should be flat and mirror-like; bad polish shows scratches, "orange-peel" texture, or uneven facets across facet junctions.
   - **Brilliance (life):** does the stone "sparkle" from reflections even though it is not cut to diamond proportions? Fine colored stones show life (bright reflections across facets) without being judged by diamond AGS/GIA cut grades.
5. Shapes and cuts in colored stones (~300w). Standard shapes: oval (most popular colored-stone shape), round brilliant (less common for colored stones but seen especially in ruby/sapphire/tsavorite), cushion/antique cushion (classic ruby/sapphire shape, historically the dominant shape for colored stones), emerald cut (step cut — for emerald, tourmaline, aquamarine), pear, marquise, heart, octagonal/emerald cut, square cushion, princess (less common in colored stones because princess corners are vulnerable for softer gems). Cabochon cuts are used for phenomenal stones (star/cat's-eye/moonstone/opal), for translucent/opaque gems (jade, turquoise, lapis), and historically for all stones before faceting developed. The "sugarloaf" cabochon (higher-domed) appears in fine ruby/sapphire/emerald cabochons.
6. Specialty cuts: fantasy, precision, designer (~350w). Munsteiner fantasy cut (Bernd Munsteiner's 1960s–70s revolution in Idar-Oberstein — large sculptural reverse facets creating optical effects); precision/computer-cut faceting (Hsu 2013 high-precision; Hsu/Lucas 2016 robotic cutting); designer cutters (Dyber, Munsteiner heirs, John Dyer & Co., etc.). Precision and fantasy cuts command a premium above calibrated commercial cut because of labor; they are art pieces as much as gemstones. Pay (2016) "Cutting the Imperial Flame Topaz" and Grussing (2016) large opal cutting are extended examples of specialty cutting challenges.
7. Native cuts, recutting and the weight-retention trade-off (~350w). "Native cut" is a trade term (sometimes politically loaded) describing traditional commercial cuts from producing regions that prioritize weight retention over ideal proportions — often with thick girdles, uneven pavilions, windows, and off-center culets. Such stones may be dramatically improved by recutting, but recutting removes carat weight and cost per carat is not linear. Rule of thumb: recut only if the gain in appearance (and thus value) exceeds the value of the carat weight lost plus the labor cost. Opal and emerald recuts carry additional risk (cleavage, fracture, filler damage).
8. Calibrated sizes and commercial colored stones (~200w). Mountings are manufactured in calibrated sizes (6×4 mm, 7×5 mm, 8×6 mm ovals; 5 mm, 6 mm rounds, etc.). Commercial cutters cut to fit these sizes for the mass market. Calibrated stones carry a premium over non-calibrated because they drop into manufactured mountings without custom setting; fine stones over ~2 ct are rarely strictly calibrated because orientation for color trumps calibration.
