## Title
**Inclusions and the Micro-World: Reading Origin, Treatment, and History Under 10×**

## Scenario
An estate client brings in a 3 ct "ruby" set in an old mounting and asks you to make an offer. The stone is a slightly purplish red with moderate extinction and some visible irregularities under your 10× loupe. You could guess it's a Thai ruby based on color, or a synthetic based on the mounting date, or a garnet based on extinction — but a wrong guess costs you money. What do you look for, and when do you stop looking and send it to a lab?

## H2 outline
1. The estate ruby that could be five things (hook ~200w)
2. What is an inclusion? (~350w). Broad GIA definition: any feature inside the gem that differs from the ideal homogeneous crystal — solid crystals, liquid/gas inclusions, fractures/feathers, growth zoning, color zoning, silk, cleavage/parting, twin planes, surface features. Inclusions are the gem's "internal landscape" — they tell origin, treatment, natural/synthetic, and geological history. Gübelin/Koivula Photoatlas is the definitive reference; GIA's Micro-World column in G&G is the ongoing record.
3. Classifying inclusions: time and type (~400w). Primary (formed when the host grew — three-phase in Colombian emerald, rutile silk in corundum, zircon halos); secondary (formed after host growth — healed fractures, secondary fingerprints, epigenetic stress cracks); protogenetic (pre-existing host rock included before host grew — calcite/dolomite in marble-hosted ruby); syngenetic (grew at same time as host — rutile needles in corundum); epigenetic (formed after host — fractures, some fillings). Phase types: monophase (liquid or gas alone), two-phase (liquid + gas bubble), three-phase (liquid + gas + solid crystal, e.g., Colombian emerald jagged three-phase with halite cube, gas bubble, and liquid).
4. Famous diagnostic inclusions — what they look like and what they mean (~900w):
   - **Colombian emerald:** three-phase inclusions (gas bubble + liquid + halite/salt cube, jagged); yellow/reddish iron-oxide staining (in Muzo material); parisite crystals; pyrite.
   - **Schist-hosted emerald (Zambia/Brazil/Sandawana/Ethiopia):** biotite mica flakes, actinolite/tremolite needles, rectangular/elongate multi-phase inclusions, atoll structures. (Schist-hosted emeralds do NOT show the classic three-phase jagged inclusion — that's the key separation from Colombian.)
   - **Kashmir sapphire:** "velvet" from submicroscopic rutile/dust, "zircon halos" (zircon crystal with surrounding disk fracture from radioactive decay), coarse rutile and pargasite crystals.
   - **Mogok/Burmese ruby/sapphire:** calcite/dolomite crystals (marble host), apatite, rutile needles, short rutile silk, strong color zoning.
   - **Thai/Cambodian basalt ruby/sapphire:** "silk" absent or sparse (high iron), reddish-brown alteration rims around crystals, pyrochlore/niobite crystals, opaque metallic inclusions.
   - **Mong Hsu ruby (Burma):** dark blue/black core with white/pink rims, flux-healed fractures from common heat treatment.
   - **Sri Lankan sapphire:** long rutile needles, "silk," zircon halos, apatite, calcite, distinct fingerprint fluid inclusions.
   - **Montana sapphire:** metasomatic/lampprophyre inclusions, negative crystals, distinct color bands.
   - **Demantoid garnet (Russian):** "horsetail" inclusions — fibrous chrysotile/byssolite asbestos diverging from a central chromite crystal (the single most famous garnet inclusion; Russian material shows it strongly; Namibian/other demantoid does not always show classic horsetail).
   - **Opal:** matrix fragments, sand grains, in precious opal the play-of-color is structural (not an inclusion per se); treated opals show sugar/acid carbon or smoke/dye effects.
   - **Paraíba-type tourmaline:** fluid inclusions, trichites, copper mineral inclusions (but identification is spectroscopic, not visual-inclusion based).
   - **Spinel:** octahedral crystal inclusions, often "iron stains" and fingerprints.
5. Treatment inclusions — what treated stones look like under 10× (~500w)
   - Heated sapphire/ ruby: altered/dissolved silk; "snowflake" inclusions from altered crystals; circular "discoid" fractures around crystals; burst halos; pitted girdle from overheating; intact heat treatment leaves no filler, but can still be detected.
   - Beryllium-diffused sapphire: can look like natural but often shows very high color saturation, weak zoning, and often surface-concentrated color at facet junctions if cut; identification requires LIBS/LA-ICP-MS chemistry.
   - Lead-glass filled ruby: flash effect (bright blue/orange flashes at fractures when tilted in reflected light), large gas bubbles in filled cavities, flattened bubbles at glass/corundum interface, bluish/orange flash at facet junctions. (Tie to GIA-CS-06 video from M01.)
   - Oiled/resin-filled emerald: flattened gas bubbles in fissures, filler that may show a yellowish/blueish flash, dried filler that appears chalky, surface-reaching fissures with evidence of filler penetration.
   - Surface-diffusion sapphire (older Ti-diffused): color concentration at facet edges and girdle.
   - Coated stones: coated tanzanite, mystic topaz — bubbles in coating, coating concentrated on facet edges, color scrapes off with a knife on girdle (do not test on customer stones).
   - Dyed stones: dye concentrations in fractures (dyed lapis, dyed jadeite C-jade, dyed turquoise, dyed chalcedony).
6. Synthetic inclusion signatures (~400w) — tie to M08.
   - Verneuil (flame-fusion) corundum: curved growth striae (curved color bands) — single most important diagnostic; gas bubbles (spherical, often large and isolated); sharp "pin-point" Plato effect under certain lighting; no natural mineral inclusions.
   - Flux-grown synthetic ruby/emerald/sapphire: wispy/veil-like flux inclusions, often with a "chevron" or "fingerprint" appearance that looks too regular; platinum crystals from the crucible (in synthetics made in platinum crucibles); no natural mineral inclusions of the host-rock type.
   - Hydrothermal synthetic emerald/beryl/quartz: "chevron"/"zig-zag" growth patterns, nail-head spicules, sometimes phenakite crystals; metal platelets from autoclave; characteristic fluid inclusions (two-phase with tapered shape, "stepped" growth lines).
   - Synthetic opal (Gilson/Kyocera): "lizard skin" / columnar structure in play-of-color array viewed from the side (Schmetzer 1987), much more regular than natural sphere arrangement.
   - Synthetic quartz (hydrothermal): breadcrumb inclusions, seed plate.
7. How labs use inclusions for origin determination (~250w). Inclusions are the first line of evidence, but modern origin determination also uses trace-element chemistry (LA-ICP-MS) and spectroscopy (FTIR, Raman, UV-Vis-NIR). Inclusions + chemistry + spectroscopy together; no single feature is definitive in most cases. For example, three-phase inclusions suggest Colombia but some Zambian emeralds can also show multi-phase inclusions; a good lab does not call an origin from one inclusion alone. This is why origin reports are probabilistic assessments, not guarantees in every case.
8. Loupe discipline and when to refer to a lab (~250w). Hold loupe close to eye; bring stone to loupe; rock stone under overhead light; use dark-field illumination if available; look at girdle, table, pavilion, immersion if you can; do not guess at natural/synthetic or origin on the strength of a single visible inclusion. Correct referral triggers: any suspicious curved striae, excessive bubbles in fractures that look like glass fill, coating evidence, or a client asking for a written origin or natural/synthetic determination that goes beyond visual opinion.
