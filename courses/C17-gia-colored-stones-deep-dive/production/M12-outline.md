## Title
**Blue Sapphire: Velvet, Cornflower and the Ring Diana Chose**

## Scenario
A bridal client wants "a sapphire engagement ring like Kate Middleton's." She has read that Kate's ring is a Ceylon sapphire and she has also read about Kashmir sapphires selling for millions, and she would like the second thing at the price of the first. This is a genuinely easy conversation if you know two facts: Kate's stone is a 12-ct Ceylon (Sri Lankan) sapphire of good commercial-to-fine quality, and the Kashmir mines produced for a few decades in the late 19th century and are effectively exhausted. Both are sapphire. They are a hundred years and three orders of magnitude apart.

## H2 outline
1. **The Diana ring and the Kashmir question (hook ~250w).** Sets up the module: the trade's most famous sapphire is an accessible Ceylon stone; the trade's most prized sapphire is a deposit that stopped producing a century ago. Both facts are useful at the counter.

2. **Why sapphire is blue: intervalence charge transfer (~350w).** Corundum is colorless when pure. Blue requires **both iron and titanium**: Fe²⁺–Ti⁴⁺ intervalence charge transfer absorbs in the yellow-orange-red and transmits blue. Iron alone (Fe²⁺–Fe³⁺) gives a weaker, often greenish-blue or too-dark body color; too much iron is why basalt-derived Australian material trends dark. Consequence for treatment: heating in an oxidizing vs reducing atmosphere shifts the Fe/Ti ratio of paired ions and can develop or intensify blue. Constants: RI 1.762–1.770, SG ~4.00, Mohs 9, uniaxial (–), pleochroism blue/greenish-blue (or violet-blue in some).

3. **Sources, look by look (~900w)** — table plus short notes:
   - **Kashmir** — the benchmark; "velvet" or cornflower blue attributed to submicroscopic exsolved inclusions that scatter light and soften the color; mined from the 1880s, essentially exhausted by the 1930s with only small-scale recovery since; alpine, short season, tiny production. What exists is in collections and estates — which is why "Kashmir" on the secondary market is a report question, not a look question.
   - **Burma (Mogok; Baw Mar)** — rich, saturated royal blue, often with fine silk; marble-hosted.
   - **Sri Lanka (Ceylon — Ratnapura, Elahera)** — the workhorse of the modern market; brighter, lighter cornflower-to-blue; **geuda** (milky, low-value rough that transforms under heat) is a Sri Lankan specialty; also the historic source of padparadscha and fine star sapphires.
   - **Madagascar (Ilakaka and others)** — the 1998 Ilakaka rush brought enormous volume; quality ranges from commercial to genuinely fine and can overlap with Ceylon material.
   - **Montana (USA)** — **Yogo Gulch**: uniform cornflower blue in an igneous dike, famously needs no heat treatment, but small sizes (most cut stones under 1 ct) and limited production. **Rock Creek / Gem Mountain / Missouri River**: gravel deposits, typically pale or greenish in the rough and routinely heat treated; Gem Mountain sells untreated stones and offers heat-treating as a service.
   - **Australia (New England NSW; Queensland — Anakie, Rubyvale)** — basalt-hosted, high iron, characteristically dark blue to inky; usually heat treated to lighten.
   - **Thailand/Cambodia (Pailin, Chanthaburi)** — basalt-hosted, dark; Chanthaburi is also the global heat-treatment and trading capital.
   - **Nigeria, Ethiopia, China (Shandong), Colombia, Laos** — basalt-hosted commercial.
   - Note: the trade increasingly treats "Ceylon" as a color descriptor (bright cornflower) as well as a locality — be precise and say which you mean.

4. **Treatment: heat, diffusion, and cobalt-glass filling (~600w).** Heat is standard and permanent for sapphire, with Sri Lankan geuda as the classic example of material that is near-worthless rough and good blue after heating; disclose, care is normal. **Beryllium lattice diffusion** (M09) matters here because it produces orange/pink/padparadscha-like colors and requires chemistry to detect (LA-ICP-MS/LIBS) — never guess from a loupe. **Cobalt-glass filling** of sapphire: a filled fracture network where the filler is cobalt-doped glass, which both improves apparent clarity and can impart blue color. Detection: **flash effect** (often a vivid, unnatural blue/orange flash), **large and flattened gas bubbles**, unusually strong blue concentration along fractures, and — critically — a **cobalt absorption spectrum** in the glass and anomalous readings. Any stone with extensive surface-reaching fractures plus a "too good" blue should be referred. Disclosure is mandatory; care is composite care.
   > **[MEDIA: video | C17-M12-V1]** — Montana Gem Mountain (GIA-CS-07), 10:52: gravel washing, heat-treating and faceting service, untreated Montana sapphire.

5. **Synthetics and the diffusion trap (~300w).** Verneuil blue sapphire has been ubiquitous since the early 20th century (curved striae, gas bubbles, often an unnaturally uniform "electric" blue); flux and hydrothermal material exist too. Diffusion-treated and synthetic-overgrowth material are the two categories most likely to be presented as natural. Rule: an unusual color at an ordinary price gets a lab report before it gets a story.

6. **Value factors and honest framing (~450w).** Color first (hue/tone/saturation; the trade's "royal blue" and "cornflower blue" descriptors; GIA applies defined criteria to color terms). Then origin — Kashmir/Burma command large premiums, Ceylon and Madagascar are the commercial-to-fine mainstream, Montana carries a domestic-origin story. Then clarity (silk is normal; "sleepy" silk-heavy material is worth less than bright material), size (big steps past 1, 3, 5 ct), and treatment (unheated commands a large premium; order-of-magnitude for fine Kashmir/Burma). Give the price-band table and the "what your budget actually buys" line.

7. **On the counter: the bridal conversation (~250w).** Sapphire at Mohs 9 is genuinely a good everyday ring stone — the second-hardest natural gem, no cleavage, takes a good polish. Honest caveats: heated is normal and fine; avoid ultrasonics on filled or heavily included stones; sapphire does chip on a hard knock (hardness is not toughness); and for the Kate-comparison client, the right answer is "a beautiful Ceylon sapphire is exactly what hers is."
