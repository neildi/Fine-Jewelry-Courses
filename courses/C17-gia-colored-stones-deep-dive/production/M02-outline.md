## Scenario hook
A bridal client compares two blue sapphires side by side: a 2.10 ct dark inky blue from a Thai/Cambodian basalt source and a 2.05 ct velvety bright royal blue from Sri Lanka/Ceylon. Same carat weight, same "sapphire" label, same shape — and very different price. The client asks "why is this one three times more — are they not both sapphires?" This module is the answer.

## H2 outline
1. **The two-sapphire problem (scenario hook, ~200 words)**
2. **Corundum is corundum — the geochemistry of why sources differ (~500 words).** Ruby and sapphire are both Al₂O₃; trace elements (Cr, Fe, Ti, V, Ga) produce color. The key difference between sources is the *host rock* the corundum grew in — that determines which trace elements are present, whether rutile silk forms, and what inclusions look like. Basalt-hosted sapphires are high-iron, dark, often greenish/inky, and lack rutile silk (so they rarely star); metamorphic-marble-hosted rubies and sapphires are low-iron, bright, fluorescent, and carry the rutile silk that can produce stars and the "velvet" of Kashmir. This is why origin is a price factor.
3. **Environment 1: Alkali basalt — dark sapphire and basalt ruby (~400 words).** Based on Levinson & Cook (1994): corundum forms when Al-rich rocks (shales/laterites/bauxites) are subducted to ~50 km, metamorphosed into corundum, then carried to surface by alkali basalt eruptions. Key sources: Thailand/Cambodia (Chanthaburi-Trat, Pailin), Australia (New England, NSW/Queensland), Nigeria, China (Shandong, Penglai, Mingxi), Vietnam (southern basalt fields). Characteristics: high Fe, dark blue/green/yellow sapphires; blue often inky; fancy colors (green, gold, parti) common; heat treatment commonly lightens color; no rutile silk → rarely stars. Cite Cambodia Pailin video (GIA-CS-03).
4. **Environment 2: Pegmatites — tourmaline, beryl, topaz, kunzite, garnet (~400 words).** Based on Shigley & Kampf (1984) and Proctor (1985): late-stage granitic pegmatites form when water-rich residual magma crystallizes slowly, producing large gem crystals. Found in Minas Gerais (Brazil), San Diego County, Himalaya (Pakistan/Afghanistan), Madagascar, Maine/California, Nigeria/Mozambique. Gems: aquamarine, morganite, heliodor, goshenite (all beryl); tourmaline (including Paraíba-type cuprian from Batalha/paraíba and Mozambique); imperial topaz from Ouro Preto hydrothermal (cross to next section); kunzite (spodumene); spessartine garnet; quartz varieties; red beryl is a rhyolite-hosted exception (Shigley et al. 2003, Wah Wah Mountains Utah — vapor-phase in topaz rhyolite, a volcanic gas environment not a pegmatite).
5. **Environment 3: Hydrothermal deposits — emerald, imperial topaz, quartz (~400 words).** Hot, mineral-rich fluids move through fractures and react with surrounding rock to deposit gem minerals. Emerald forms this way in two main sub-types: Colombian black-shale hydrothermal (Muzo/Chivor/Coscuez — fluid interaction between limestone/shale and hydrothermal brine; vanadium+chromium yields the saturated slightly bluish-green) and schist-hosted/pegmatite-vein emerald (Zambia Kagem, Brazil, Sandawana Zimbabwe, Ethiopia, Panjshir Afghanistan — beryllium from pegmatite meets chromium/vanadium in mafic/ultramafic schist). Capão topaz (Keller 1983) is hydrothermal in itabirite (iron formation). Quartz (amethyst, citrine, ametrine from Anahi Bolivia) commonly deposits in hydrothermal veins.
6. **Environment 4: Metamorphic rocks — marble-hosted ruby, Kashmir sapphire, jadeite (~400 words).** Regional metamorphism — particularly the Pan-African orogeny (~550 mya, forming the Mozambique belt) and Himalayan orogeny (~45 mya, from India colliding with Asia) — produces the world's most valuable colored stones. Marble-hosted (marble is metamorphosed limestone) ruby and sapphire from Mogok (Myanmar), Mong Hsu, Luc Yen (Vietnam), Jegdalek (Afghanistan), Snezhnoe (Tajikistan), Nepal — low iron, bright reds/pinks/blues, rutile silk common. Kashmir sapphire is metamorphosed pegmatite/metasomatic in the Himalayas (metasomatic fluid alteration yielding the submicroscopic rutile that produces "velvet"). Jadeite forms in serpentinite mélange under high-pressure/low-temperature metamorphism along subduction zones (Myanmar Hpakant, Guatemala Motagua).
7. **Environment 5: Sedimentary and supergene deposits — opal, turquoise, malachite, sapphire gravels (~300 words).** Opal forms by weathering of silica-rich rock and deposition of silica gel in sedimentary host rock (Queensland boulder opal in Winton formation ironstone, per Wise 1993; Lightning Ridge black opal; Coober Pedy white opal; Ethiopian precious opal from volcanic-hosted nodules is Tertiary-age weathering). Turquoise and malachite are supergene (near-surface alteration of copper deposits). Alluvial gem gravels (Sri Lanka Ratnapura Ilakaka/Madagascar, Montana, Pailin/Cambodia river gravels) are sedimentary *placer* deposits — the gem has eroded from its primary rock and been concentrated in rivers/gravels. These are secondary deposits; the primary source rock may be far upstream.
8. **What this means for the counter (~250 words).** Why a basalt blue sapphire will never look like a Ceylon or Kashmir; why "Burma ruby" color requires marble-hosted low-iron chemistry; why Colombian emeralds form in a different geological environment than Zambian emeralds (which affects both the inclusions and the color); why Paraíba tourmaline is tied to granitic pegmatite; why opal requires different care from every crystalline silicate gem.
9. **On the floor this week (~150 words).** (1) Pull two blue sapphires from different sources and compare color; (2) learn to say "these two sapphires look different because they formed in different kinds of rock" rather than attributing every difference to "better quality"; (3) read the GIA Gem Encyclopedia entry on one species formation this week.
