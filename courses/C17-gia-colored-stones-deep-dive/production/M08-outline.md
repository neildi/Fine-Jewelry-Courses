## Title
**Synthetics, Simulants, and Assembled Stones: What Is (and Is Not) What It Looks Like**

## Scenario
A customer brings in a large "ruby" she bought at a vintage market for a low price. It has bright saturated color, looks clean under loupe, and she is convinced she found a bargain. Under 10× you see curved color bands and a couple of spherical gas bubbles. It's a Verneuil synthetic ruby — worth a few dollars a carat, not thousands. This module is the difference between paying $20 and $20,000 for the same-looking stone.

## H2 outline
1. The vintage-market ruby (hook ~200w)
2. Three categories: synthetic, simulant, assembled (~350w).
   - **Synthetic (lab-created/man-made):** same chemical composition, crystal structure and optical/physical properties as the natural gem — Verneuil/flux/hydrothermal ruby is real corundum (Al₂O₃); synthetic emerald is real beryl (Be₃Al₂Si₆O₁₈); they are not fake — they are grown in a lab instead of the ground and must be disclosed. FTC Guides require disclosure when a product is "not of natural origin."
   - **Simulant (imitation):** a different material that looks similar — glass imitating ruby, cubic zirconia imitating diamond (C16), cobalt glass imitating lapis, plastic imitating amber, goldstone imitating sunstone. A simulant is NOT the same material.
   - **Assembled stones (doublets/triplets):** two or three pieces bonded together — opal doublet (natural opal slice on potch/glass or ironstone backing; boulder opal is natural ironstone-hosted and not a doublet), opal triplet (opal + quartz cap + backing), garnet-topped doublet (GTD — garnet top fused to colored glass pavilion imitating ruby; historically common), emerald triplets (beryl top + green cement, or synthetic + natural composite), sapphire/spinel doublets.
   Terminology: "lab-created," "synthetic," "lab-grown," "cultured" (for pearls only, by convention). Avoid "fake," "real" — say "natural ruby" vs "synthetic (lab-created) ruby," and always disclose.
3. Major synthetic growth methods (~800w) — anchor: Stone-Sundberg 2013 Sapphire Series; Renfro 2010 decade review.
   - **Verneuil (flame-fusion)** — Auguste Verneuil 1902; drops aluminum oxide powder through an oxyhydrogen flame onto a rotating boule; fast and cheap; most commercial synthetic ruby/sapphire historically; synthetic star corundum also done this way with titanium doping. **Detection:** curved growth striae/curved color bands (curved arcs not present in natural corundum, which has straight/angular bands); spherical gas bubbles; sometimes clouds of tiny bubbles; no natural host-rock mineral inclusions (no calcite/biotite/actinolite). Used for ruby, sapphire (all colors), blue spinel, rutile, strontium titanate.
   - **Czochralski (pulled)** — pulling a seed crystal from a melt; used for ruby, sapphire, alexandrite (many commercial synthetic alexandrites), YAG, GGG, synthetic moissanite (SiC grown by a related method). **Detection:** sometimes curved striae; gas bubbles; generally very clean internally; higher clarity than Verneuil.
   - **Flux-melt** — crystals grown slowly from a molten flux (a solvent that dissolves the feed material at temperatures below melting point) over months; produces ruby, sapphire, emerald, alexandrite, spinel. Commercial brands: Chatham, Kashan, Ramaura, Knischka, Gilson. **Detection:** wispy/veil-like flux inclusions (often chevron or lace-like), platinum platelets from platinum crucibles, "feather" flux that looks too uniform or whitish/golden; no natural host-rock inclusions.
   - **Hydrothermal** — crystals grown in a high-pressure autoclave with a water-based solution (mirroring natural hydrothermal conditions); commercial emerald (Biron/Pool, Russian/Tairus, Chinese hydrothermals), synthetic quartz (almost all synthetic amethyst/citrine/prasiolite is hydrothermal), some hydrothermal ruby/sapphire, synthetic amethyst. **Detection:** chevron/zig-zag growth patterns, nail-head spicules (in hydrothermal emeralds), phenakite crystals, metal platelets (gold/copper) from autoclave walls, tapered two-phase fluid inclusions, a visible seed plate.
   - **Skull melt / cubic zirconia (CZ)** — covered in C16; CZ is a CZ simulant, not synthetic diamond.
   - **Synthetic opal** (Gilson/Kyocera and successors) — grown by sedimentation of silica spheres; **detection:** columnar/lizard-skin structure visible from the side (Schmetzer/Henn 1987); unusually regular play-of-color array; often no host-rock matrix; some synthetic opal is polymer-impregnated.
   - **Synthetic/amorphous** (glass, plastic) not synthetics in the gemological sense (they lack crystal structure); classify as simulants.
   - **Synthetic jadeite (GE, 1980s experimental)** — Nassau/Shigley 1987; never commercial jewelry threat but known to labs.
   - **Synthetic moissanite (SiC)** — C16; not a colored-stone synthetic in this course.
   - **Lechleitner overgrowth** (Kane 1985) and other overgrowth synthetics (Saeseaw 2015 synthetic ruby overgrowth) — thin synthetic layer on natural seed; detection: seed visible, growth features at boundary.
   - **Inamori/Kyocera synthetic star ruby and synthetic cat's-eye alexandrite** (Koivula/Kane papers).
4. Common simulants by species (~450w).
   - **For ruby/sapphire:** red/pink/blue glass (paste), synthetic spinel (common blue/red/red-pink Verneuil spinel), red garnet doublets (GTD), composite sapphire/spinel doublets (Anjomani 2016), pink topaz misrepresented as ruby, rubellite tourmaline.
   - **For emerald:** green glass, green CZ, green synthetic spinel, assembled beryl/glass triplets (Mayerson 2001; Singbamroong 2007 beryl triplets imitating Colombian), green plastic-coated beryl, green yttrium-aluminum-garnet (YAG), green tourmaline misrepresented as emerald, soude emerald (green-glass-layered emerald simulant).
   - **For alexandrite:** synthetic corundum doped with vanadium (color-change synthetic sapphire — not true alexandrite, which is chrysoberyl), color-change CZ, color-change glass.
   - **For lapis lazuli:** cobalt glass (Bosshart 1983), dyed howlite, dyed jasper, synthetic lapis (rare), sodalite.
   - **For turquoise:** dyed howlite, dyed magnesite, plastic/turquoise composites (block turquoise), glass, ceramic imitations, reconstructed turquoise.
   - **For pearl:** Majorica (glass bead with essence d'orient, Hanano 1990), shell pearls (calcite-bead coated with nacre dust), plastic beads, glass beads.
   - **For amber:** plastic (bakelite, celluloid, copal — young resin, not true amber), pressed amber (ambroid), glass.
   - **For opal:** plastic opalite (Koivula/Kammerling 1989 — "Opalite"), Gilson/Kyocera synthetic opal, Slocum Stone (a glass opal simulant with metallic flakes), opal doublets/triplets.
   - **For peridot:** peridot-colored glass (McClure/Reinitz 1999), green CZ, green tourmaline, green sapphire.
   - **For tanzanite:** violet-blue glass, synthetic forsterite/czochralski synthetics (rare), coated tanzanite/synthetic coated topaz.
   - Chelsea filter as a screening tool (not a verdict): emeralds typically show red/pink through the filter (Cobalt glass also shows red; some modern hydrothermal synthetics show red too). Chelsea is a triage tool only.
5. Assembled stones: doublets and triplets (~350w).
   - Opal doublet: thin precious opal layer glued to a backing (potch/common opal, black glass, ironstone epoxy, or basalt); adds durability and creates a dark background.
   - Opal triplet: same as doublet but with a clear quartz or glass cap over the opal (protects soft opal); domed cap magnifies play-of-color.
   - Garnet-topped doublet (GTD): thin garnet top fused to a colored glass pavilion — common 19th-century ruby simulant; detection: look at the girdle in profile for a join line; the red top is garnet (hard, luster) but the pavilion is soft glass.
   - Beryl-and-glass triplet imitating emerald (Mayerson 2001): near-colorless beryl top, green cement layer, near-colorless beryl bottom.
   - Soude emerald: two colorless layers (synthetic spinel or quartz, historically) joined by green glue.
   - Sapphire/synthetic-spinel doublets (Anjomani 2016) — recent detection reports.
   - Detection principles: look for a join line (usually at the girdle when immersed in liquid or viewed edge-on); gas bubbles in the glue layer; difference in luster between top and bottom; red ring (garnet top under the table edge in GTD); in opal triplets the top cap is transparent quartz with no play-of-color.
6. Counter discipline and disclosure (~300w)
   - FTC Guides (Overton 2004) require disclosure of treatments and of synthetic origin to a consumer before sale. Using "ruby" without qualifier means natural ruby. "Synthetic ruby," "lab-created ruby," or "lab-grown ruby" are acceptable for man-made corundum. Do not call synthetic corundum "Chatham ruby" without the word "synthetic" or "created" — Chatham is a brand name, not a species.
   - Never identify a stone as natural vs synthetic at the counter without a lab report unless you see a definitive feature (curved striae = Verneuil; obvious join line at girdle on a doublet). Even then, "this appears to be synthetic; let me confirm with the lab" is better than "this is a fake."
   - When in doubt, refer to a gemologist/lab. The cost of a GIA report is trivial compared with the cost of a wrong identification on a high-value stone.
