## Title
**Treatments: Heat, Diffusion, Filling, Oiling, Irradiation, Coating, Dyeing — and Disclosure**

## Scenario
A client brings back a "ruby" ring she bought from you a year ago. She cleaned it in jewelry cleaner (mild acid) at home and now the stone looks cloudy and orange, with white spots along some fractures. The stone is a lead-glass-filled ruby. The sale was made without disclosure, and she's asking why "her ruby" is damaged. If the treatment had been disclosed at sale — with the care rules — this would not be a surprise. This is the module that prevents that scenario.

## H2 outline
1. The lead-glass-filled ruby that came back damaged (hook ~200w)
2. Why treatments exist, and why disclosure is law (~350w). Treatments have been used since antiquity (Nassau 1984): Roman writers describe emerald oiling; Pliny describes heat treatment of agate/carnelian; dyeing of agate and turquoise is ancient. Modern treatments are far more sophisticated — heating corundum at 1800°C, lattice diffusion with beryllium, lead-glass filling of fractures. Treatment is not in itself deceptive — almost all commercial blue sapphire is heat-treated; almost all commercial tanzanite is heated; almost all blue topaz is irradiated. The deceptive act is failure to disclose. Overton (2004) is the legal anchor: FTC Jewelry Guides require disclosure when treatment is not permanent, affects value, or requires special care.
3. Stability categories: stable vs. unstable/damage-prone (~250w). Some treatments are permanent and undetectable without chemistry (Be-diffusion once penetrated; heat only — stable); some are stable under normal wear but damaged by heat/solvents (emerald oil/resin); some are fundamentally less durable than untreated material (lead-glass fill); some are coatings that can abrade; some are dyeing that can bleed. Stability determines disclosure and care.
4. Treatment by treatment: what each does, why it's done, detection, care, disclosure (~1600w — the main body of the module).
   - (a) **Heat treatment (corundum, tanzanite, aquamarine, morganite, amethyst → citrine, amber, zircon, some tourmaline).** Most common treatment in colored stones. Heat dissolves rutile silk (improving clarity), alters Fe/Ti oxidation to improve blue in sapphire, removes brown component in tanzanite (vanadium oxidation), removes green in aquamarine, changes amethyst to citrine/prasiolite, lightens dark zircon. Heat-only corundum is considered stable and is the industry standard; much commercial ruby/sapphire is heated without further treatment. Detection: altered/dissolved silk, snowflake crystals, discoid fractures around crystals, pitted girdle if overheated (Koivula 2013 useful clue; Abraham 1982 Chanthaburi scene; Nassau 1981 technical anchor). "H(BE)-heat" on GIA reports = heat only; no special care usually; ultrasonic/steam generally OK on heat-only material.
   - (b) **Low-temperature heat treatment of ruby** (Pardieu et al. 2015 low-temp Mozambique ruby heat) — milder heat to improve red; often associated with flux healing; classified with heat/flux.
   - (c) **Diffusion treatment** adds color-causing elements to the surface (or in beryllium case, into the lattice):
     - Surface (titanium) diffusion — classic early 1980s blue sapphire; color concentrated at facet edges/girdle (Kane 1990; McClure 1993).
     - Beryllium lattice diffusion (Emmett et al. 2003; McClure et al. 2002) — Be penetrates the entire lattice at high temperature, producing orange, pink, padparadscha-like, yellow and red colors in sapphire; requires LIBS/LA-ICP-MS for detection; stable once done (penetrates lattice), but disclosure is required; sometimes referred to as "lattice diffusion."
     - Cobalt diffusion on spinel (Saeseaw 2015) and other variants.
     - Diffusion-induced stars (Ti-diffusion producing synthetic rutile silk; stars can be unnaturally perfect).
     - Surface-diffusion color can be polished off on repolishing.
   - (d) **Lead-glass filling of ruby** (McClure et al. 2006 anchor) — fills surface-reaching fractures in heavily fractured Mong Hsu/Mozambique/Madagascar ruby with high-lead glass, improving apparent transparency dramatically. **Detection: flash effect (blue/orange flashes at fracture junctions in reflected light), large gas bubbles in glass, flattened bubbles at glass-corundum interface, blue/orange flash at facet joints, lower apparent hardness on filled fractures.** **Durability: glass is vulnerable to acid (household cleaners, lemon juice), ultrasonic, steam, jewelers' torches (melts), and even mild abrasion; recutting and repair work risks damaging the stone.** Disclosure is mandatory. GIA reports lead-glass fill as a Comment, not a "clarity enhancement" — it is considered a manufactured composite component. GIA-CS-06 is the video anchor.
   - (e) **Flux-assisted heat treatment / flux healing of ruby** — heat with a flux that melts into fractures, healing them with synthetic corundum grown from the flux; distinct from glass fill because the filler is precipitated corundum, not glass; common on Mong Hsu and some Mozambique rubies; detected by characteristic flux fingerprints. Classified as heat treatment.
   - (f) **Emerald clarity enhancement (oiling/resin filling)** (McClure et al. 1999 F0–F3 scale). Filling surface-reaching fractures with oil (cedarwood oil traditionally), resins (Opticon, other polymer resins), or "green oil" to diminish visibility of fractures. F0 = none; F1 = minor; F2 = moderate; F3 = significant. Oiling is ancient (Nassau 1984 references Pliny). Detection: flattened gas bubbles in fissures; colored flash in reflected light; dried filler appears chalky in older stones; IR spectroscopy detects resins. Stability: oils can sweat out with heat/UV, dry out over time; resins are more durable but can be damaged by strong solvents, steam, ultrasonic. Emeralds must avoid solvents and heat; clean with warm soapy water; re-oiling is routine maintenance.
   - (g) **Irradiation** (Nassau 1985; Rossman 1981; Crowningshield 1981). Gamma/electron/neutron irradiation creates color centers: all commercial blue topaz (London blue, Swiss blue, sky blue); many smoky quartz; some yellow/orange sapphire; "Maxixe-type" blue beryl; some pink/green quartz; diamond irradiation (C16). Neutron-irradiated topaz was historically a radiation-safety concern (NRC-regulated release levels now ensure safety); most commercial irradiated topaz is safe. Irradiated color can be stable (blue topaz) or fade (Maxixe beryl, some irradiated kunzite). Detection: spectroscopy; no visible inclusions from the irradiation itself. Disclosure expected for all irradiated colored stones whose color is not naturally occurring.
   - (h) **Coating** (McClure/Shen 2008 coated tanzanite; Gabasch 2008 mystic topaz). Thin-film deposition of metallic/oxide layer on the pavilion (or all surfaces) to create fancy colors (mystic topaz, "azotic" topaz, coated tanzanite imitating fancy sapphires, some diamond-like coatings C16). **Detection: bubbles in coating; iridescent film on the girdle; coating abrades at facet edges.** Durability risk; avoid ultrasonic/abrasion/repolishing.
   - (i) **Dyeing** — dyeing lapis (calcite portions), turquoise, jadeite C-jade, howlite "turquoise," chalcedony/agate, coral, pearls, coral imitations. **Detection: dye concentrations in fractures/grain boundaries; swab test (acetone) picks up dye on porous stones.** Dye can bleed with water/alcohol/acetone.
   - (j) **Bleaching and polymer impregnation of jade (B-jade)** (Fritsch et al. 1992). Acid bleaching removes brown stains from lower-quality jadeite, then polymer resin is impregnated to improve transparency and color. C-jade is dyed (often after bleaching). A-jade = natural untreated. B-jade is detectable by IR (polymer absorption); visually can look "too clean," pitted surface, loose texture. Durability: polymer can degrade with heat/strong chemicals; avoid ultrasonic/steam.
   - (k) **Sugar/acid treatment of opal** (Brown 1991 Andamooka matrix opal) — sugar solution then acid carbonizes sugar in pores, darkening bodycolor and intensifying play-of-color; typically for matrix opal.
   - (l) **Smoke treatment of opal** — smoke darkens the bodycolor of Ethiopian/Welo opal via carbon impregnation at surface.
   - (m) **Stabilization** — polymer/wax impregnation of porous stones (turquoise, lapis, opal sometimes) to improve hardness and seal porosity. Common and considered standard for some material; disclosure is expected.
   - (n) **Waxing/oiling of turquoise, serpentine, nephrite** — surface treatment to improve polish; generally stable; routine maintenance.
   - (o) **Amber treatments** (Wang 2014 heat treatment of Baltic amber; pressed amber ambroid, dyeing).
