# M14 Quiz — Emerald

**1. Why is Colombian emerald geologically unusual?**
a) It is the only emerald found in South America.
b) It is hosted by sedimentary rock — Cretaceous black shale — unlike almost every other emerald deposit, which is igneous or metamorphic.
c) It contains no beryllium.
d) It forms in basalt.

**2. What two elements cause the green in emerald?**
a) Iron and titanium.
b) Chromium and/or vanadium.
c) Copper and manganese.
d) Chromium and titanium.

**3. What do the GIA emerald grades F0, F1, F2 and F3 mean?**
a) Fluorite content.
b) None, minor, moderate and significant clarity enhancement.
c) Four color grades.
d) Four cutting grades.

**4. Why does an emerald need periodic re-oiling?**
a) Because the beryl dissolves.
b) Because traditional cedarwood-oil filler dries out over time.
c) Because the color fades.
d) Because the stone shrinks.

**5. Which pair of readings screens hydrothermal synthetic emerald from natural emerald?**
a) Mohs and streak.
b) RI ~1.560–1.570 and SG ~2.65 (synthetic) versus RI ~1.577–1.583 and SG ~2.72 (natural).
c) Pleochroism and luster.
d) Fluorescence and magnetism.

**6. What is the emerald cleaning rule?**
a) Ultrasonic is fine; avoid steam.
b) Steam is fine; avoid ultrasonic.
c) No ultrasonic and no steam, ever — warm soapy water and a soft brush only.
d) Any method is fine if the stone is F0.

**7. Which country is the largest emerald producer in the world by volume?**
a) Colombia.
b) Brazil.
c) Zambia.
d) Afghanistan.

**8. Why is Ethiopian emerald easy to misidentify?**
a) It is always synthetic.
b) It is chromium-dominant and low in iron, so it can look and fluoresce like Colombian material.
c) It is colorless.
d) It only occurs in tiny sizes.

**9. What is trapiche emerald?**
a) A synthetic emerald.
b) A rare six-rayed pattern of carbonaceous impurity between growth sectors, associated with Colombia.
c) A cutting style.
d) A type of assembled stone.

**10. Why is a loupe-clean emerald at an ordinary price suspicious?**
a) It is probably a fake setting.
b) Clean natural emerald is extraordinarily rare; such a stone is likely synthetic or assembled.
c) Because inclusions are required by law.
d) It is probably dyed quartz.

---

## Answer key

1. **b** — Nearly all other emerald forms in pegmatite or schist, where beryllium-bearing fluids met chromium-bearing rock. Colombia's host is Cretaceous black shale, with emerald in calcite–fluorite–pyrite–albite veins.
2. **b** — Chromium and/or vanadium in the aluminium site. Chromium gives red fluorescence; iron, when present, adds blue and quenches it.
3. **b** — F0 none, F1 minor, F2 moderate, F3 significant (McClure et al. 1999). F1 is normal and should not be presented as a defect; F0 and F1 carry real premiums.
4. **b** — Traditional cedarwood oil dries out. Polymer fillers last longer but can yellow or craze and are harder to re-treat.
5. **b** — Those two readings are the fastest bench screen. They are not conclusive alone, but a stone at 1.565/2.65 is not natural Colombian material.
6. **c** — Emerald's weakness is toughness, not hardness, and the filler is the most fragile part of the stone. Warm soapy water, soft brush, dry immediately.
7. **c** — Zambia, through Kagem and Musakashi. Colombia commands the price premium; Zambia leads on volume.
8. **b** — Low iron and chromium-dominance give it a Colombian look and Colombian fluorescence. Origin comes from the report, never from a resemblance.
9. **b** — A hexagonal growth phenomenon unique to a few Colombian localities (and rarely Brazil). It is an indicator, not a grade and not a certificate of origin.
10. **b** — Inclusions are the norm in emerald and are forgiven; what matters is transparency and liveliness. Unusual cleanliness at a normal price points to synthetic or assembled material.
