## Title
**Pearls: Nacre, Seven Value Factors, and the Word the FTC Requires**

## Scenario
A client brings in her mother's strand and asks two questions: "are these real?" and "are they worth anything?" Both questions are harder than they sound. "Real" splits into natural vs cultured vs imitation, and the answer changes the value by a large factor. "Worth anything" depends on luster, nacre, matching and condition. This module is the one that lets you answer both honestly in five minutes.

## H2 outline
1. **"Are they real?" (hook ~250w).** Sets up the module: the word "pearl" is legally reserved, and the honest answer to "are these real" is usually "they're cultured pearls — real pearls, grown with human help, and here's what that means."

2. **How a pearl forms (~350w).** A mollusk secretes **nacre** — aragonite platelets stacked like brickwork, bound by an organic protein called **conchiolin** — around an irritant that it cannot expel. Light interferes with and is diffracted by those stacked platelets, which is what produces **luster**, **orient** (the iridescent sheen) and **iridescence**. Nacreous vs non-nacreous: nacreous pearls are layered aragonite (Akoya, South Sea, Tahitian, freshwater, abalone); non-nacreous pearls are porcelaneous calcite/aragonite concretions with no platelet layering (conch, melo, clam).
   **Natural vs cultured:** a natural pearl forms by accident with no human intervention; a cultured pearl forms because a technician implanted a nucleus and/or a piece of mantle tissue. Both are real pearls. Under the FTC Jewelry Guides, the unqualified word **"pearl" means natural**, so cultured pearls must be called **cultured pearls** — this is a legal vocabulary point, not a snobbery point.
   Brief history: the modern cultured-pearl industry begins with Kokichi Mikimoto's work in Japan in the 1890s, with the first commercially successful spherical cultured pearls in the early 1900s.

3. **The types (~900w)** — table plus notes:
   - **Akoya** — *Pinctada fucata/martensii*; Japan, China, Vietnam, Australia; 2–10 mm; white/cream with rose, silver or green overtones; the classic round high-luster strand; bead-nucleated; nacre typically thin relative to South Sea (roughly 0.15–0.5 mm).
   - **South Sea** — *Pinctada maxima*; Australia, Indonesia, Philippines; 9–20 mm; white to silver, and **golden** (notably the Philippines); thick nacre (roughly 2–6 mm); bead-nucleated; the largest and generally most expensive cultured pearls.
   - **Tahitian** — *Pinctada margaritifera*; French Polynesia; 8–18 mm; naturally dark body color with **peacock green, aubergine, silver** overtones; bead-nucleated. Important: "black pearl" in the trade usually means dark, not literally black.
   - **Freshwater** — *Hyriopsis cumingii* and related mussels; overwhelmingly Chinese production; 2–15 mm; mostly **tissue-nucleated** (no bead), so mostly solid nacre — which is why freshwater pearls tolerate drilling and wear well; historically off-round and pastel, now produced round and in a wide color range, including large bead-nucleated "Edison" material.
   - **Mabé (blister pearl)** — grown against the inside of the shell; hemispherical; usually backed and capped; lower cost, good for earrings and rings.
   - **Keshi** — an all-nacre by-product of culturing, formed when a nucleus is rejected or a beadless sac forms; irregular shape, usually exceptional luster; sold as cultured pearls.
   - **Natural pearls** — wild mollusks, accidental, essentially antique and rare; the modern market is estate and auction (La Peregrina: 202.24 grains / 50.56 carats, sold at Christie's New York in December 2011 for $11,842,500).
   - **Conch** — *Lobatus (Strombus) gigas*; **non-nacreous**, porcelaneous, pink to white with a **flame structure** visible under magnification; cannot be commercially cultured; rare and collected.
   - **Abalone, melo, clam (Tridacna), cassis** — brief note; abalone is nacreous with intense iridescence, melo is non-nacreous orange.

4. **The GIA 7 Pearl Value Factors (~600w).** Size, shape, color, luster, surface, nacre, matching — with **luster the single most important**. Table of all seven with what good looks like. Note the trade's AAA/AA/A letter shorthand and the important caveat that **no single authority polices those letters** — they are a summary layered on top of the seven factors, so confirm any AAA claim against the factors. Also note: a moderate-size pearl with outstanding luster beats a big chalky one every time.

5. **Treatments (~500w).** Routine and largely undisclosed-but-accepted: washing, light tumbling, and traditional polishing/prepare (the Japanese *maeshori* process) which improve luster without adding color. Then the ones that must be disclosed: **bleaching** (near-universal), **dyeing** (black dye on freshwater; look for color concentration in drill holes, in surface irregularities and between pearls in a strand), **color treatments that do not add a colorant**, and **coating/irradiation** (less common).
   **The Ballerina case study:** "chocolate pearls" are **Tahitian cultured pearls** (*Pinctada margaritifera*) treated by Ballerina Pearl Co. to convert less-attractive dark material into a uniform brown — a bleaching process, not a dye, according to GIA's studies (Wang et al. 2006; Du Toit 2008 color-durability study; the 2016 "pistachio" study). Natural-color Tahitian pearls typically show a strong bluish fluorescence under DiamondView; treated stones are inert or weak. GIA's durability work found treated and untreated pearls behaved similarly to daylight, cosmetics and many chemicals, with more change under heat and reactive household cleaners.

6. **How a laboratory tells natural from cultured (~350w).** X-ray is the workhorse. **Real-time microradiography (RTX)** shows a bead nucleus in bead-nucleated cultured pearls and concentric growth structures in naturals; **X-ray computed microtomography (μ-CT)** maps the internal structure in three dimensions and is what separates a beadless natural from a beadless cultured (keshi, tissue-nucleated freshwater), where the diagnostic is the presence and shape of internal cavities and growth architecture rather than a bead. X-ray fluorescence also helps: freshwater pearls are generally manganese-rich and strontium-poor relative to saltwater pearls. Practical rule: **anything presented as a natural pearl gets a lab report** — the value difference is far too large for an opinion.

7. **Care (~250w).** **Last on, first off.** Wipe with a soft damp cloth after wearing. No ultrasonic, no steam, no soaking, no solvents, no heat. Avoid perfume, hairspray, cosmetics, chlorine. Restring periodically (silk stretches and soils) — annually for a frequently worn strand, and knot between pearls. Store flat in a soft pouch, not hanging; not in a sealed vault long-term (pearls contain a little water and organic material and do not like prolonged desiccation). Wear them: pearls dull from neglect more than from careful wear.
   Mohs ~2.5–4.5 — pearls are soft, and this is the reason for the "last on, first off" rule.
