## Title
**Jade: Jadeite vs Nephrite, Type A/B/C, and the Bangle That Must Fit**

## Scenario
A client brings in a green bangle she bought in a tourist market years ago. She paid a lot of money for it and she would like to know whether it is "real jade" and what it is worth. There are three separate questions hiding in that sentence: is it jade at all (it could be serpentine, quartz or glass); if it is jade, is it jadeite or nephrite; and if it is jadeite, is it Type A, B or C? Each answer changes the value substantially, and only the last one can be settled without a laboratory.

## H2 outline
1. **The bangle on the counter (hook ~250w).** Establishes the three-question structure and previews the module: jade is the one species where the counter's most common question ("is it real jade?") has a three-part answer, and where the cultural weight is heaviest.

2. **Two different rocks called jade (~500w).**
   - **Jadeite** — a pyroxene, NaAlSi₂O₆; Mohs ~6.5–7; SG ~3.30–3.38; RI ~1.66 (spot); granular, interlocking grain; capable of high translucency and vivid color. Chromium produces the finest green; iron contributes to darker and bluish greens; manganese is associated with lavender.
   - **Nephrite** — an amphibole in the tremolite–actinolite series, Ca₂(Mg,Fe)₅Si₈O₂₂(OH)₂; Mohs ~6–6.5; SG ~2.90–3.03; RI ~1.61; **fibrous, felted interlocking texture**; oily to waxy luster; colors run white through green to gray, brown and black.
   - The key contrast: **jadeite is harder, nephrite is tougher.** Nephrite's felted fibre structure makes it the toughest natural gem material — which is why it was the prehistoric tool stone across so many cultures.
   - Color causes table. Note that chromium gives jadeite's finest "imperial" green; the equivalent green is not available in nephrite.

3. **Where jade comes from (~500w).**
   - **Jadeite:** **Hpakant, Kachin State, Myanmar** — the world's premier source and effectively the only commercial source of top-quality imperial jadeite; **Guatemala** (Motagua Valley, rediscovered in the 1970s, blue-green and other colors); Japan, California, Russia, Kazakhstan as minor occurrences.
   - **Nephrite:** **Hetian (Hotan), Xinjiang, China** — historically the most prized white nephrite, recovered as river boulders and known as "mutton fat" at its best; **New Zealand pounamu** (greenstone), a taonga with specific cultural and legal standing; **British Columbia, Canada** (large deposits, much of it sold into the Chinese carving market); **Australia** (Cowell, South Australia); Russia (Lake Baikal), Taiwan (Fengtian), USA (Wyoming), Poland.

4. **Value factors for jadeite (~350w).** Color first — the most prized is **imperial green**: a pure, slightly bluish-to-emerald green, evenly distributed, with fine texture and good translucency. Then translucency, texture (fine and even grain), clarity (freedom from cracks, black spots and dull patches), carving quality, and size. Note: **transparency and texture matter more in jade than in any other species** because jade is a rock, not a single crystal — the grain is the beauty.

5. **Type A / B / C: the treatment language (~650w).**
   - **Type A** — natural jadeite with no chemical treatment; a **surface waxing** finish is traditional, accepted and generally considered within Type A.
   - **Type B** — **bleached and polymer-impregnated.** Acid treatment removes brown/gray iron staining, which leaves the stone porous and chalky; a polymer resin is then injected to restore translucency and hold the structure together. Detection: **infrared spectroscopy is the definitive test** (polymer absorption peaks); visually, a dull or waxy luster, a "pitted" or orange-peel surface under magnification, and resin that yellows, crazes and clouds over time. Type B degrades — this is the crucial client-facing fact.
   - **Type C** — **dyed.** Color added, often concentrated along grain boundaries and in cracks; visible with magnification and sometimes with a spectroscope or by dye concentration in surface pits.
   - **B+C** is common. Fritsch et al. (1992) is the anchor for bleached and polymer-impregnated jadeite. Disclosure is mandatory: Type B and C must be stated, and the price difference from Type A is enormous.

6. **Simulants and neighbours (~400w).** Table:
   - **Serpentine / bowenite** — "new jade," "Korean jade," often sold as jade; softer, lower SG, often with a slightly greasy feel and sometimes visible mottling.
   - **Maw-sit-sit** — a bright green rock from Myanmar containing kosmochlor (chromian jadeite), albite and jadeite, usually with black chromite spots; closely related to jadeite but a rock of its own.
   - **Aventurine quartz** — "Indian jade"; grainy green quartz with mica sparkle.
   - **Hydrogrossular garnet** — "Transvaal jade" / "African jade."
   - **Vesuvianite / californite** — "California jade."
   - **Dyed chalcedony / agate**, **glass**, **plastic**; dyed quartzite in the modern market.
   - Nephrite sold as jadeite (and vice versa) — a misnaming rather than a simulant, but the same disclosure problem.

7. **Bangles: sizing and the cultural weight (~450w).** How to size: the bangle must pass over the widest part of the hand with the thumb tucked across the palm; measure across the knuckles at the widest point (usually the hand flat, thumb pressed toward the little finger) and match that to the bangle's **inner diameter**. Offer a sizing set; do not force a bangle on. Note the shapes (round, flattened/"D", oval, donut) and that a snug bangle is traditional but should never require pain or lubricant that risks injury.
   Cultural context, handled respectfully: in Chinese tradition a bangle is a protective object, often given by an elder, and a bangle that breaks is widely said to have taken the blow meant for the wearer. Families often keep the broken pieces rather than discard them, and some bury them. **Do not mock this, and do not dismiss it** — the correct response is to take it seriously, explain whether the piece can be repaired or re-cut, and let the client decide. Note also that pounamu is a taonga with legal and cultural standing in New Zealand, that nephrite has deep Māori significance, and that jade carving traditions in China are thousands of years old.

8. **Care (~200w).** Type A: warm soapy water, soft cloth, no harsh chemicals, avoid prolonged heat. Type B/C: avoid solvents, heat and ultrasonics entirely — the polymer and dye are the fragile parts. Remove bangles for manual work; nephrite and jadeite are tough but a hard blow on a bangle can break it. Store separately so pieces do not abrade each other.
