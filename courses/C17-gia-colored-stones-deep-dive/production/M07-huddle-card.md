# M07 Huddle Card — Cut

**One headline:** Colored stones are cut for color, not light return like diamonds. Judge them face-up in daylight — not by diamond-style "ideal proportions."

**3 key points**
1. **The three trade-offs the cutter balances:** face-up color, weight retention, symmetry/polish. They often conflict. Dark stones are cut shallower to reduce extinction; pale stones are cut deeper to deepen color; fine stones are often cut heavy to retain carat weight.
2. **Face-up checklist (30 seconds):** color even across the table? Window (see-through center)? Extinction (dead dark patches)? Over-dark or over-light in daylight? Symmetry (centered table/culet, even girdle)? Polish (mirror-flat facets with no scratches)? Life (sparkle as it moves)?
3. **Recuts need a value estimate first.** Recutting always costs weight and risks cleavage/fracture/filler damage; refer to a gemologist for a before/after value analysis before committing. Fine and antique stones should be recut only when the improvement clearly outweighs lost weight and history.

**1 script to practice today**

When comparing two similar sapphires/rubies/emeralds:
"Let me show you these in daylight — that's the best way to judge a colored stone. Both are nice, but this one faces up brighter and more evenly because the cutter oriented it to bring out the blue — the proportions are slightly different, but what matters is how the stone performs when you're wearing it."

"Which of our best-selling colored stone lines are calibrated vs. non-calibrated? Let's agree on how to explain the calibrated premium ('it fits standard mountings with no custom work') vs. why fine stones aren't calibrated ('they're oriented for color first, hand-set')."
