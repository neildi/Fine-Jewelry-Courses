# M03 Huddle Card — Mining, Recovery, and Field Gemology

**One headline:** Colored stones come from a mix of large mechanized pits, small family tunnels, and river-gravel washing. No universal "ethical certification" exists the way the Kimberley Process does for diamonds — be honest about what you know and don't know.

**3 key points**
1. **Primary vs secondary mining.** Primary = the rock the gem formed in; secondary = gravels where eroded gems were concentrated by rivers. Most accessible colored-stone mining historically is secondary.
2. **Five recovery methods:** artisanal hand-mining/tunnels, alluvial washing (hoses + jigs/sluices), open-pit mechanized (Kagem, Belmont, Montepuez), underground (Chivor/Coscuez, Panjshir, Capão), and dredge/marine (rare for colored stones).
3. **The pipeline is long.** Miner → local buyer → treatment (often Chanthaburi) → cutting (mostly Jaipur) → dealer → manufacturer → retail. Traceability is exceptional rather than routine. Exceptions: vertically integrated producers (Belmont, Gemfields-branded auctions, some Montana sapphire, some Australian opal).

**1 script to practice today**

Client: "Is this emerald ethically sourced?"

Associate: "Colored stones don't have a universal certification like the Kimberley Process for diamonds, so I want to be straight with you. This emerald is [country of origin per report/supplier], and on this piece we [do / don't] have a mine-direct traceability program. I can show you pieces from [traceable line if available] if that's important to you, and I can walk you through the treatment and quality on this one regardless."

**1 question to ask the team**

"Which of our colored-stone lines have documented mine-to-market traceability today, and which don't? Let's agree as a team on exactly what we can and can't say about each line before we all get asked the same question on the floor this weekend."
