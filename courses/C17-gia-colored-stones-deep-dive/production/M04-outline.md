## Title
**Physical Properties: Hardness, Cleavage, Toughness, Optical Constants, and Durability in Design**

## Scenario
A client brings back her tanzanite ring three months after purchase with a chipped and cracked girdle. She wore it every day, cleaned it in an ultrasonic at home, and says "but the salesman told me it was precious." Nothing about the sale was dishonest, but no one told her that tanzanite sits at 6–6.5 Mohs, has perfect cleavage in one direction, and is dramatically softer and more sensitive than the sapphire it resembles. This is a durability conversation the associate should have had before the sale.

## H2 outline
1. The tanzanite that came back cracked (hook ~200w)
2. Durability has three parts, not one — hardness, toughness, stability (~500w). Define each with examples: diamond = high hardness + moderate toughness (can cleave), nephrite = moderate hardness (6–6.5) + exceptional toughness (fibrous), tanzanite = moderate hardness (6–6.5) + brittle + perfect cleavage, pearl = soft (2.5–4.5) + low chemical stability (acid-sensitive).
3. Hardness and the Mohs scale — what it measures and what it doesn't (~500w). Mohs scratch resistance, relative scale (not linear: diamond is 4 steps harder than corundum in absolute terms). Why dust (quartz, Mohs 7) scratches anything at or below 7. Table of species covered in this course by Mohs number with daily-wear implications. Why emerald (7.5–8) is less durable than the number suggests (fractures from inclusions). Cite Martin 1987 (Gemstone Durability).
4. Cleavage, fracture and toughness — why hardness alone misleads (~500w). Cleavage planes in diamond, topaz, spodumene (kunzite), tanzanite (zoisite), moonstone, feldspar. Conchoidal fracture in quartz and opal. Parting in corundum. Why emerald's jardin and internal fractures matter more than its 7.5–8 Mohs rating — a single knock can extend a feather even if the stone can't be scratched.
5. Optical constants: refractive index, birefringence, dispersion, SG — what they are and why they matter for ID (~600w). RI measured by refractometer (1.45–1.80 range on a standard refractometer); explain how a refractometer works (critical angle) without overcomplicating. Key RI/SG pairs: corundum RI ~1.762–1.770, SG ~4.0; beryl RI ~1.57–1.59, SG ~2.7; quartz RI ~1.544–1.553, SG ~2.66; topaz RI ~1.61–1.63, SG ~3.5; tanzanite RI ~1.69–1.70, SG ~3.35; opal RI ~1.45, SG ~2.1. Birefringence (double vision) as a quick filter for identifying doubly refractive stones (zircon, peridot, tourmaline show doubling of pavilion facets through a loupe). Pleochroism is introduced but deepened in M05. This section explains what you are seeing when you look at a stone under a loupe, not how to be a gemologist.
6. Stability — heat, light, chemicals, humidity (~350w). Per-species care: ultrasonic/steam safety (safe for corundum, most garnet, diamond — NOT for emerald, opal, tanzanite, pearl, peridot, amber, coral, malachite); light-fading (kunzite, amethyst prolonged strong light, some irradiated topaz); heat sensitivity (opal, tanzanite sudden temperature change, emerald fillers, pearl dehydration); chemical sensitivity (pearl/coral/malachite/amber to acid and household chemicals; lead-glass-filled ruby to even mild acid/abrasion; turquoise to cosmetics).
7. What this means for mounting and care — the floor conversation (~300w). How to set different stones (bezel vs prong for softer stones, protective settings for tanzanite/emerald/opal; avoid tension settings on tanzanite/opal; avoid ultrasonic exposure for treated stones; storage instructions).
8. On the floor this week
9. Objections/care mistakes
10. Self-check
11. Go deeper
