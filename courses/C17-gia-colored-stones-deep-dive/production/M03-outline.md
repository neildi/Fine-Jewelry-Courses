**Prerequisite:** M02 (formation environments)

## Title
**Finding Them: Mining, Recovery, and Field Gemology from Mogok to Montepuez**

## Scenario
A client looking at an emerald ring asks "where does this emerald actually come from — and is it from a mine I should feel good about buying from?" You know it was sold to you as "Colombian" by a dealer you trust, but you have no idea whether it came from a small family co-op, a large mechanized pit, or a parcel that changed hands four times. This module covers what you can say, what you should not say, and how mining actually works.

## H2 outline
1. The client who asks where it came from (hook, ~200 words)
2. Two kinds of mining, two kinds of deposit: primary vs secondary (~400 words). Primary = hard-rock mining at the geologic source (Kagem open-pit emerald, Mogok underground ruby, Wah Wah Mountain red beryl tunnels, Capão topaz underground, Hpakant jadeite). Secondary = alluvial/placer mining from eroded material in rivers or ancient gravels (Sri Lanka illam, Ilakaka sapphire, Pailin river mining, Montana sapphire gravels, most opal fields). Much colored stone on the market comes from secondary deposits because (a) gems are dense and concentrated by water, (b) secondary gravels are easier to mine without heavy equipment, and (c) the same river systems often have thousands of years of artisanal mining history.
3. How colored stones are mined: five methods (~600 words).
   - (a) Artisanal hand-mining (small-scale, hand tools, shafts and pits, river panning): most colored stones historically and much production today in Mogok, Jegdalek, parts of Madagascar and Kenya.
   - (b) Alluvial washing (high-pressure monitors + jigs/sluices, as in Pardieu's Pailin video): water separates dense corundum from lighter gravel. Describe Pardieu's video.
   - (c) Open-pit mechanized mining (Kagem is the largest colored-gemstone pit on Earth; Belmont Mine Brazil; Montepuez ruby; Winton boulder opal open-cut). Show scale (Kagem ~1 km across).
   - (d) Underground and tunnel mining (tunneling into primary rock — Chivor and Coscuez emeralds in Colombia, Wah Wah red beryl, Capão topaz, Panjshir emerald adits, Hpakant jadeite using both Bamar and Hpakant methods).
   - (e) Marine/river dredge and marine mining (less common for colored stones than for diamonds; alluvial sapphire dredging in some Australian and Montana deposits).
   Cross-reference videos (GIA-CS-03 Pailin, GIA-CS-04 Brazil emerald, GIA-CS-05 Afghanistan Panjshir, GIA-CS-07 Montana).
4. The cutting and trading pipeline: from mine to market (~500 words). Rough buying at the mine; sorting and grading; pre-forming (in Chanthaburi, Jaipur); heat treatment of corundum typically done in Chanthaburi; cutting (Jaipur for smaller commercial goods, Chanthaburi for Thai ruby/sapphire, Idar-Oberstein historically, China manufacturing in Panyu and Guangzhou per Hsu/Lucas); dealer parcels; Tucson/Hong Kong/Singapore/Jaipur shows and Gemfields auctions; bench jeweler; retail. Jaipur as the global emerald-cutting hub (Lucas 2016 Jaipur papers). Gemfields' auction model for Kagem and Montepuez.
5. Field gemology: why GIA sends scientists to mines (~400 words). Building reference collections of known-origin samples (direct-from-miner samples like the Pailin Pardieu video explains); supporting geographic origin determination; documenting new deposits (Ethiopian emerald, Madagascar demantoid, Winza ruby at the time of rush); documentary evidence from the mine that feeds laboratory separation criteria. GIA field teams (Pardieu, Vertriest, Palke, Lucas, etc.). Cite key expeditions: Mogok (2014–2015), Montepuez (2014), Zambia Kagem (2014/2015), Sri Lanka (2014), Brazil Belmont (2015), Luc Yen Vietnam (2015), Madagascar sapphire rushes (2012, 2015, 2016), Ethiopia (2017).
6. Traceability and responsible sourcing (~350 words). Kimberley Process does not apply to colored stones (color-stone supply chains are far more fragmented, smaller-scale, often artisanal). Archuleta (2016) "The Color of Responsibility" describes frameworks: mine-to-market programs (Gemfields' auction-plus-partner model, GIA's developing origin-and-traceability report seen in GIA-CS-01), ASM (artisanal small-scale mining) certification, OECD Due Diligence Guidance for responsible supply chains. This is not solved the way conflict-diamond rules are; an associate should know enough to describe the state of the question honestly.
7. The stories: five mines to know as counter stories (~250 words). Mogok (Burma, "Valley of Rubies," centuries of lore, restricted access), Montepuez (Mozambique, 2009 discovery, Gemfields, reshaped ruby market in a decade), Kagem (Zambia, world's largest emerald pit), Belmont (Brazil, a model of traceable emerald mining), Ilakaka (Madagascar, 1998 sapphire rush, still producing).
