## Title
**Fancy Sapphire and Phenomenal Corundum: Padparadscha, Stars, Color Change — and the Diffusion Scandal**

## Scenario
A client wants a padparadscha sapphire for an anniversary. She has found one online at a fraction of what you quoted, with a "certificate" from a lab you do not recognize. Meanwhile another client wants a white sapphire instead of a diamond to save money. Both conversations hinge on the same skill: knowing which fancy-corundum claims have to be verified in a laboratory and which are ordinary commercial facts.

## H2 outline
1. **Two clients, one skill (hook ~200w).** Sets up the module: fancy corundum is where the trade's treatment problems concentrate, because the colors that command the biggest premiums (padparadscha, vivid orange, intense pink) are exactly the colors beryllium diffusion can manufacture.

2. **What "fancy" means, and the color-cause table (~450w).** Fancy sapphire = any corundum color that is not red (ruby) and not blue. Table of color → chromophore:
   - **Pink** — Cr³⁺ at lower concentration than ruby; the ruby/pink-sapphire boundary is a tone-and-saturation judgment, not a chemistry change
   - **Orange / padparadscha** — Cr + Fe together (and, in some stones, color centers)
   - **Yellow** — Fe³⁺ and/or color centers; note stability concerns
   - **Green** — yellow (Fe³⁺) plus blue (Fe²⁺–Ti⁴⁺) in one stone
   - **Purple/violet** — Cr + Fe, sometimes with V
   - **Colorless (white)** — no chromophores at all
   - **Color-change** — chromium with vanadium
   - **Black** — abundance of dark inclusions (hematite/ilmenite), not a chromophore
   - **Parti / teal** — color zoning from changing chemistry during growth; a modern cutting fashion, not a species
   - Note: "fancy" is a trade convenience, not a mineralogical category.

3. **Padparadscha: the most argued-about name in corundum (~450w).** A pinkish-orange to orange-pink sapphire; the name comes from the Sinhalese for lotus blossom. Traditionally Sri Lankan; now also Madagascar, Vietnam and Tanzania. Why the name is contested: there is no single agreed boundary between padparadscha, "orange sapphire" and "pink sapphire," and different labs apply different criteria. Practical rule: **sell the stone, document the name.** If the report says padparadscha, say padparadscha; if it does not, describe the color and price accordingly. Never attach the name on your own judgment at a premium price.

4. **The beryllium diffusion scandal (~550w).** In the early 2000s vivid orange, pink and padparadscha-colored sapphires appeared in the market in volume, sold as natural untreated material. The color had been produced by **lattice diffusion of beryllium** at very high temperature: unlike older titanium surface diffusion, Be penetrates the *entire* stone, so there is no surface rim to polish through and no visual feature that is diagnostic at 10×. Detection requires trace-element chemistry — LA-ICP-MS or LIBS. Emmett, Scarratt, McClure et al. (2003) is the anchor paper, and the episode is the single best case study in the course for why a report matters more than a loupe. Disclosure is mandatory: "beryllium treated" or "lattice diffused."
   > **[MEDIA: video | C17-M13-V1]** — A Gemstone's Journey through the GIA Laboratory (GIA-CS-01), 57:21, linked at the report section: how origin and treatment are actually determined.

5. **Phenomenal corundum: stars (~600w).** Asterism is caused by densely packed, oriented needles of rutile ("silk") lying in three directions at 120° within the basal plane; a cabochon cut with its base parallel to that plane shows a six-ray star, and two overlapping sets can give twelve rays. How to judge a star: sharpness and completeness of the rays, centering on the dome, symmetry, body color, and translucency/silk balance. The trade's plain truth: **the finest stars are usually not the most transparent stones** — heavy silk makes the star but clouds the body, so a sharply starred stone often has a softer, less "gemmy" look. Cut matters enormously: the cabochon must be oriented on the c-axis, and a slightly high dome sharpens the star. Over-polishing or a flat dome weakens it. Synthetic star sapphire (Verneuil with titanium) is common and reads unnaturally perfect — razor-sharp rays, unnaturally even silk, a too-uniform body color. Diffusion-induced stars on natural material also exist and must be disclosed.

6. **Color-change sapphire (~300w).** Chromium plus vanadium produces a stone that shifts from bluish or violetish in daylight to reddish-purple under incandescent light — the same family of effect as alexandrite. Most commercial color-change sapphire is **synthetic** (Verneuil), produced as an alexandrite imitation; natural material exists but is not common. Refer before you call it natural.

7. **White sapphire vs diamond: the honest comparison (~350w).** Colorless corundum is a real, natural, Mohs-9 gem and a legitimate diamond alternative on price. It is also not a diamond: RI ~1.77 vs 2.42, dispersion 0.018 vs 0.044, and — the point clients actually notice over time — it does not hold the same sparkle and it dulls with wear in a way diamond does not. Table comparing the two honestly. Use it for accents, halos, and clients who want a large natural white stone at a modest price; be clear it will not look like the diamond it is standing in for.

8. **Value, treatment risk and buying rules (~350w).** Which fancy colors are ordinarily heated (nearly all of them), which carry diffusion risk (orange, pink, padparadscha, yellow — the vivid end), which are usually untreated (many pastel Sri Lankan and Madagascar goods, parti/teal material). Buying rule: anything vivid in the orange-pink-yellow range at a modest price gets a lab report before it gets a price.
