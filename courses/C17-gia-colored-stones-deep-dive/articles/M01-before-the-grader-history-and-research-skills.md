## The ring that isn't what grandma called it

A client hands you a 3.20 ct oval blue stone from her late mother's ring, in an old yellow-gold mounting with a small diamond on each shoulder. "The family always called it a sapphire," she says. "But it doesn't look like the royal-blue ones I see online, and I want to know what it is, what it's worth, and whether it's been treated." There is no lab report, no receipt, no other documentation. She expects a useful answer within the next 90 seconds.

If you are a C1 graduate, you already know four Cs and a colored-stone top ten. But none of that lets you look at a 3 ct blue stone on a loupe and confidently tell her what it is, whether it is heated, where it came from, or what it is worth. The stone could be a natural blue sapphire from Sri Lanka, from Madagascar, from Montana, from Cambodia, from Nigeria, or from Kashmir; it could be a flame-fusion synthetic sapphire made in the 1970s; it could be a diffusion-treated sapphire; it could be a blue spinel that an earlier generation sold as sapphire; it could be a heat-treated iolite; it could be a tanzanite with enough violet to read blue under certain lights; it could be a benitoite, a blue tourmaline, or a paste doublet. None of these distinctions are rude to raise out loud — they are what makes a careful answer different from a careless one.

This course exists to prepare you for that conversation. Where C1's M06 gave you the hardness/treatment/care table for the ten most-sold colored stones in a compact format for the new associate, C17 goes under that table and teaches you the science, the history, the treatment detection features, the locality differences, the synthetics, the market, and the four species that matter most (ruby, sapphire, emerald, and the pearl/jade capstones) the way GIA's own research literature teaches them — mechanism first, vocabulary second.

## Why colored stones are not "fancy diamonds" — the framing difference

If you sell diamonds, you already know the frame: color graded D–Z against masterstones; clarity graded on eleven grades from FL to I3 at 10×; cut graded on a seven-component scale; weight measured to the hundredth of a carat. Diamonds are one mineral species — carbon — with one dominant grading vocabulary that the world has used consistently since Shipley standardized the 4Cs in the 1940s. The vocabulary is portable. A VS1 G Excellent round from one vendor reads the same way as a VS1 G Excellent round from another.

Colored stones are not like that. Four structural differences make colored-stone grading and selling a fundamentally different job, and understanding them on day one will save you from the most common counter mistakes:

**1. Species diversity instead of one species.** The colored-stone category is not a single thing; it is dozens of mineral species (corundum, beryl, quartz, topaz, tourmaline, spodumene, olivine, opal, garnet, zircon, feldspar, zoisite, spinel, chrysoberyl, jadeite, nephrite, plus a handful of biological gems like pearl, coral, amber and shell). Each has its own physical properties, typical treatments, care requirements and value factors. There is no single colored-stone equivalent of the diamond grading report that works across all of them.

**2. Color is the primary value driver, not a demerit.** In diamonds, color is a defect (more yellow = less valuable). In colored stones, color *is* the point. Tone, saturation and hue — not letter grades — are how color is described. A fine Burmese ruby is not "A on some diamond-like scale"; it is a vivid, strongly saturated red to slightly purplish red in medium to medium-dark tone. A fine Kashmir sapphire is a velvety, saturated blue. A fine Colombian emerald is a saturated, slightly bluish green. The vocabulary is descriptive rather than lettered, and it differs per species.

**3. Treatments are the commercial norm, not a red flag.** Most rubies and sapphires on the market today are heat-treated. [VERIFY: GIA field reporting and trade literature consistently states the vast majority of commercial corundum is heat-treated; confirm exact percentage from McClure 2010 decade review during fact-check.] Most commercial emeralds are clarity-enhanced with oil or resin. Almost all blue topaz on the market is irradiated. These are not deceptive practices when properly disclosed — they are standard, stable, long-standing industry treatments that improve availability and affordability. The associate's job is not to apologize for them; it is to know which treatments are stable, which require special care, which affect value, and which must by law be disclosed.

**4. Origin is a price factor.** A 3 ct unheated Burmese ruby of fine color can sell for multiples of what a comparable-looking Mozambique ruby sells for, and multiples again over a Thai/Cambodian basalt ruby. Kashmir sapphires, Colombian emeralds with "gota de aceite" growth, and Burmese jadeite carry premiums that are not reflected in color/clarity alone. Diamond origin matters mostly for ethical/Branding reasons (CanadaMark, etc.); colored-stone origin materially changes value per carat. This is why GIA and other major labs offer origin reports for seven qualifying colored stones — ruby, blue sapphire, emerald, Paraíba-type tourmaline, red spinel, alexandrite and one further species confirmed in [VERIFY: GIA-CS-01 transcript captured six; verify the seventh against GIA's service page] — and why origin determination is now one of the lab's most important services.

Here is the difference in one table:

| Value factor | Diamonds (D-to-Z) | Colored stones |
|---|---|---|
| Species | Always diamond (C) | Dozens (corundum, beryl, tourmaline, quartz, opal, etc.) |
| Color | D–Z (letter grade against masterstones) | Hue / tone / saturation, described per species |
| Clarity | FL–I3 (11 grades at 10×) | Species-dependent; eye-clean expectations vary enormously (emeralds are *expected* to have inclusions; aquamarine is not) |
| Cut | Excellent–Poor (proportion-based) | Cut for color orientation first; windowing/extinction trade-offs; no universal cut grade |
| Weight | Carat to 0.01 ct | Carat measured, but price/ct swings wildly with color saturation and origin |
| Treatment | Laser drilling, fracture filling, HPHT, coating (exceptional) | Heat, oil/resin, diffusion, glass-filling, irradiation, dyeing, bleaching/polymer, coating — many are the commercial norm |
| Origin | Usually not a price tier (branded exceptions) | Major price tier for ruby, sapphire, emerald, jadeite, Paraíba-type tourmaline, red spinel |

The rest of the course teaches you, section by section, how to talk about each of those rows with the confidence of someone who has read the research.

## A short history of colored stones in the modern trade

You do not need to memorize dates to sell colored stones well. But a short mental timeline keeps you from repeating common myths (that "emerald oiling is a new scam," that "synthetic rubies only appeared in the last few years," that "lead-glass-filled rubies are somehow a third-world problem" rather than a global disclosure issue), and it gives you client-sayable stories for the stones in your case.

**The ancient and pre-modern trade (to c. 1800).** Colored stones are older than the diamond trade by thousands of years. Lapis lazuli from Badakhshan was traded to Egypt by the 4th millennium BCE. The Silk Road carried Sri Lankan sapphires, Burmese rubies from Mogok, and Afghan lapis to Europe; the Roman Empire imported emeralds from the Egyptian "Cleopatra's mines" at Wadi Sikait and from farther east. After the Spanish conquest of Colombia in the 1530s, Muzo and Chivor emeralds entered European jewelry in quantity — the Cheapside Hoard in London (buried c. 1640, excavated 1912) holds Colombian emeralds alongside Sri Lankan sapphires, Indian diamonds and Persian turquoises, a snapshot of the global colored-stone trade already in place four centuries ago. Mughal India carved Colombian emeralds into talismanic objects. Jadeite worked in China from Burmese (Hpakant) material was already a structured luxury market by the 18th century. Treatments are not new either: Nassau (1984) documents heat treatment of agate and carnelian going back to Pliny, oiling of emeralds known in Roman times, and dyeing recipes recorded in papyri. [VERIFY: specific Nassau historical claims will be cross-checked against the article during M09 production.]

**The 19th century synthetics shock (1880s–1910s).** Auguste Verneuil's flame-fusion process for synthetic ruby was presented publicly in 1902 [VERIFY: verify exact year against GIA/historical source; the standard date in gemological literature is 1902, with production reaching the market by 1904] and produced large, cheap, chemically real rubies that looked alarmingly like natural stones once cut. By 1910, the Geneva and other rubies were in the market and detection was a scramble. This is the origin of modern gemology as a discipline: the problem of telling natural from synthetic forced the trade to develop microscopy, refractive-index testing, specific-gravity testing and eventually spectroscopy.

**The GIA era and the treatment-detection decades (1931–1990).** Robert M. Shipley founded GIA in 1931 and standardized the 4Cs for diamonds in the 1940s. Colored-stone work proceeded in parallel but did not have the same clean standardization — Nassau's four-part series "The Early History of Gemstone Treatments" (1984) and the Fritsch–Rossman color-science trilogy (1987–1988, the anchor of this course's Module 5) and a stream of G&G articles built the modern identification toolkit. The Chatham flux-grown rubies and emeralds appeared mid-century; Russian hydrothermal synthetic emeralds and flux synthetic emeralds joined them; and the 1980s and 1990s saw a continuous stream of new synthetics and treatments that GIA documented in the Lab Notes and GNI sections of the journal. Glass-filled rubies existed in cavities as early as Kane (1984), and Opticon (resin) filling of emeralds was documented by Kammerling et al. in 1991.

**The beryllium scandal and the modern disclosure era (2001–2007).** In late 2001, a wave of unusually vivid orange and pink-orange sapphires entered the Thai and world markets, initially traded as natural untreated padparadscha-like stones. McClure et al. published the first special report in G&G Spring 2002; Emmett et al. published the definitive technical paper in Summer 2003 explaining the cause — bulk lattice diffusion of beryllium at temperatures above 1800°C, which alters color through the entire stone rather than on the surface like the earlier titanium diffusion. The incident is known in the trade as the "beryllium scandal" because the goods entered the market without disclosure, and it reset colored-stone disclosure norms: beryllium diffusion is today a legitimate treatment when disclosed, and every reputable lab tests for it.

**Lead-glass-filled rubies (2004–).** McClure et al. (2006) documented a new treatment of Mong Hsu and later low-quality Mozambique ruby with high-lead-content glass that effectively masked fractures and made opaque near-worthless rough transparent enough to sell. The flash effect (blue/orange flashes at facet joints), large gas bubbles, and durability vulnerability (household chemicals, acid, even routine ultrasonic cleaning can damage them) made this the most consumer-visible colored-stone treatment issue of the 2000s, and it remains a disclosure priority.

**The 2000s–2010s: new sources and origin science.** Shigley et al. (2010) and Shor & Weldon (2010) documented a decade of rapid supply change: Ilakaka, Madagascar sapphire (1998) added a vast new basalt-related sapphire source; Winza, Tanzania produced rubies and blue sapphires in a short-lived rush (2007–2009); Montepuez, Mozambique ruby (discovered 2009, industrialized by Gemfields from 2012 onward) became the dominant ruby source of the 2010s; Ethiopia added emeralds in 2016–2017. GIA and other labs developed the trace-element chemistry (LA-ICP-MS) and spectroscopy that underpin modern geographic origin determination, and responsible-sourcing frameworks (Archuleta 2016) began to address the fact that the Kimberley Process certification scheme does not apply to colored stones.

**Today.** A client walking into your store in 2026 is buying in a market where GIA issues colored-stone identification and origin reports (see the video at the top of this module), where Gemfields runs auction-based rough supply for Kagem emerald and Montepuez ruby, where Madagascar supplies more sapphire than any single historical source at its peak, and where heat treatment, oiling and irradiation are expected, disclosed, and priced into goods. Your job is to know this clearly enough that you never scare a client with "your stone is treated!" as though treatment is bad news, never mis-price a stone by ignoring origin, and never guess about origin or treatment when you do not know.

## How to read the literature: your four GIA doors

The entire rest of this course is built from GIA's free, public body of work. There are four doors in, and knowing which door to open saves you hours the first time a client asks you a question you cannot answer.

> **Gems & Gemology (G&G)** — https://www.gia.edu/gems-gemology
> GIA's quarterly peer-reviewed professional journal, published since 1934. Free to download, every article, every issue, back to volume one. **Use G&G when** you need the original research: identification of a new treatment, detection features, inclusion studies, trace-element chemistry, spectroscopic data, the peer-reviewed anchor for a claim. The articles are technical; they are written for gemologists. You do not need to read every one to sell jewelry, but when you need the definitive answer — "how do I recognize lead-glass filling?" "what is beryllium diffusion?" "why do Kashmir sapphires look velvety?" — G&G is where the answer was published first.

> **GIA Research & News** — https://www.gia.edu/gia-news-research
> Field expedition reports, Lab Notes (short case reports from the lab), industry analysis by Russ Shor and others, Knowledge Sessions webinars, video features from GIA's field gemology team, and the Micro-World photomicrography columns. **Use Research & News when** you want the most recent locality updates, photos and video from mines, and plain-language summaries of research. The field reports are the best way to learn what a mine actually looks like (and they include video the GIA channel doesn't always mirror). Industry analysis pieces are your source for market narratives (Gemfields auctions, China demand, price cycles).

> **GIA Gem Encyclopedia** — https://www.gia.edu/gem-encyclopedia
> A species-by-species reference with entries for every commercial gem species: overview, quality factors, buyer's guide, history and lore, care and cleaning, photos. **Use the Encyclopedia when** a client asks a basic species question and you need the answer in under two minutes: "what is tanzanite?" "how hard is peridot?" "what's the difference between jadeite and nephrite?" It is not the research literature — it is the first-stop reference, and it is excellent. Start here, go deeper if you need to.

> **GIA Library** — https://www.gia.edu/library
> The search portal for everything GIA has published, including the current QR-code lookup in the Further Reading PDF that ships with each GIA course. **Use the Library when** a direct article URL 404s (GIA has migrated its site repeatedly; legacy `gia.edu/doc/<season>_<year>.pdf` paths frequently break) or when you need to find an article from a partial citation. The Library is also where you retrieve the articles listed in the "For Further Reading" handout if their direct URLs fail.

Here is a thirty-second workflow you can use at the counter the next time a question stumps you:

1. **Identify the type of question.** Species ID? Start with the Encyclopedia. Treatment detection? Start with G&G (search for the treatment name). Locality or market update? Start with Research & News. Broken link? Start with the Library.
2. **If it's a client question and you're unsure of the answer**, say so. Don't guess at origin, treatment, identity or value. Use the script in the next section.
3. **When you find the answer**, bookmark it and add one fact to your next huddle so the whole team learns it.

A note on the Further Reading PDF this course is built from: GIA prints the line *"This reading is optional. You will not be tested on the content of these articles."* Treat that line as the opposite of true. The reading list is where the course content lives — it is GIA telling you, the student, where their own course designers went to learn the material the course summarizes. This course is an invitation to read it.

The "Big Three" colored stones of the global gem trade — ruby, sapphire and emerald — all belong to a small set of mineral species (ruby and sapphire are both corundum; emerald is the green chromium/vanadium variety of beryl). They are the Big Three not for mineralogical reasons but for commercial and historical ones: they are the three colored stones that have anchored royal jewelry, auction records, mining empires and consumer aspiration for centuries. The deep-dive modules give each of them origin-by-origin treatment, inclusion fingerprint, treatment history and value context.

Pearls and jade sit as capstones because neither fits the gemstone-mineral template. Pearls are biological (grown inside mollusks) and their entire grading vocabulary is unique (nacre thickness, luster, orient, surface, shape, color, overtone). Jade is a cultural gem in a way nothing else is — jadeite's value structure is dominated by the Chinese-speaking market, the Type A/B/C treatment vocabulary is specific to jadeite, and carving tradition adds dimensions that faceted-stone grading never discusses.

## Objections, mistakes and edge cases

| Situation | The trap | Better move |
|---|---|---|
| Client asks "Is this sapphire natural?" at the counter | Saying "yes" without qualification — almost all commercial sapphires are heat-treated, which is natural *species* but not untreated | Answer: "The stone is natural sapphire, and like nearly all sapphires in the market it is heat-treated, which is a standard, permanent, disclosed treatment. Let me confirm the specific report on this piece." |
| Client says "That emerald is VVS, right?" | Applying diamond clarity language to emeralds (emeralds are *expected* to contain inclusions; "eye-clean" is a better standard than diamond VS) | Answer: "We don't use the VVS scale the same way for emeralds — emeralds are expected to have inclusions, and this one is graded for clarity enhancement on the F1/F2/F3 scale rather than VVS/VS. Let me show you what that means." |
| Client asks "How much per carat?" the way they would for a diamond | Quoting a per-carat price on the assumption it scales linearly | Answer: "Colored-stone prices don't scale the way diamond prices do — two stones of the same carat weight can be worth very different amounts based on color saturation and origin. Let me walk you through this specific stone." |
| Client brings a family heirloom and says "Grandma said it's a Kashmir sapphire" | Confirming the family legend without evidence | Answer: "What a lovely family story. Origin is determined in the lab, not at the counter — Kashmir sapphires are rare enough that I'd want to see this one under the microscope before saying. I can recommend a lab report." |
| Client asks if a treated stone is "fake" | Treating "treated" as a moral category | Answer: "Treated doesn't mean fake — it means the color or clarity was improved by a process the trade has used for decades, and as long as it's disclosed and priced accordingly, it's a legitimate, valuable gem. The important thing is knowing which treatment it had and how to care for it." |
| Associate sees a colored stone online described as "VVS1" | Assuming that means the same as it means for a diamond | Recognize this as either sloppy marketing language or a seller who doesn't know colored stones; don't repeat it in your own selling. |
