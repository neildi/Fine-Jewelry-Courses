# The KPI Vocabulary of Jewelry Retail

## Why This Module Matters

A store manager who can't define conversion, ATV, or GMROI precisely can't diagnose why a month went wrong, and can't defend a budget request to a district manager who speaks fluently in these terms. This opening module of C12 builds the shared vocabulary the rest of the course depends on: eight core retail KPIs, each with its exact formula, so every later module in this course — building the weekly dashboard, diagnosing a miss, reporting upward — can be built on a foundation everyone in the room actually agrees on.

## Conversion Rate: The Percentage That Explains Everything Else

Conversion rate measures how many of the people who walked into the store actually bought something. The formula: number of transactions divided by foot traffic (or visits) for the period, multiplied by 100. A store with 400 visitors and 60 transactions in a week is converting at 15 percent. Conversion is the single number most directly affected by staffing, floor coverage, and product knowledge — exactly the disciplines covered in this course's companion course, C11, on daily and weekly store operations. A manager who only tracks total sales and not conversion can be fooled by a week where higher foot traffic masks declining sales skill on the floor.

## Average Ticket (ATV) and Units Per Transaction (UPT)

Average ticket value, often written ATV or AOV (average order value), measures how much a typical transaction is worth. One widely used formula: (gross sales minus discounts) divided by the number of orders. Units per transaction, or UPT, measures how many items a customer buys per visit — a store that sells one $2,000 ring per transaction has a very different growth lever available to it than a store that sells three $700 items per transaction, and UPT is the number that reveals which pattern is actually happening. Together, ATV and UPT decompose total sales into its two underlying drivers: are customers buying bigger pieces, or are they buying more pieces per visit — and each driver responds to a different coaching intervention.

## Sales Per Hour and Sales Per Square Foot

Sales per hour ties revenue directly to labor cost and staffing decisions, calculated by dividing total sales for a period by total hours worked in that period — the number Module 6 of this course will use directly when diagnosing whether a schedule is properly matched to traffic. Sales per square foot ties revenue to the physical footprint of the store, useful for comparing productivity across locations of different sizes, which becomes especially relevant in C14's multi-store leadership content.

## Sell-Through Rate: What Percentage of Stock Actually Moved

Sell-through rate measures what percentage of a specific item, category, or the full inventory has sold in a given period, relative to how much was available at the start of that period. The standard formula: units sold divided by beginning-of-period units on hand, multiplied by 100. A collection that arrived with 40 units and sold 10 in its first week has an 25 percent weekly sell-through rate. Sell-through is the number that tells a manager which categories need a markdown push and which need a reorder, well before the category shows up as a problem on a monthly financial statement.

## Inventory Turn: How Many Times Stock Cycles Through

Inventory turnover measures how many times average inventory is sold and replaced during a given period, calculated as cost of goods sold divided by average inventory (at cost). Turn and sell-through measure related but distinct things: sell-through looks at a specific batch or period, while turn looks at the ongoing cycling rate of the whole inventory investment over a longer horizon. A jewelry store, with high per-unit value and deliberately slower velocity than fast fashion, will run a much lower turn number than a typical apparel retailer — and that's expected, not a red flag, given the category.

## GMROI: Profitability Per Dollar of Inventory Invested

Gross margin return on investment, GMROI, measures how profitably a store's inventory dollars are working, calculated as gross margin dollars divided by average inventory cost. This is the number that answers a question sell-through and turn can't answer on their own: for every dollar tied up in inventory, how much gross profit is that dollar generating? A category with strong sell-through but thin margins can still produce a disappointing GMROI, which is why this metric belongs on the same dashboard as sell-through and turn rather than being treated as redundant with either.

## Shrinkage and Retention: The Two Numbers That Round Out the Set

Shrinkage rate — the percentage of inventory lost to theft, damage, or administrative error relative to recorded inventory value — is the direct output of the discipline C11's Module 4 (high-value verification protocol) and Module 7 (vendor and repair pipeline visibility) build. Customer retention rate, measuring what share of the store's client base makes a repeat purchase within a defined period, is the direct output of the clienteling cadence covered in C3 and the client-book review habit built in C11's Module 3. Both numbers depend on operational disciplines this course assumes the reader has already built, and both belong on the same weekly dashboard this course's Module 6 will construct.

## Putting the Vocabulary to Work

Each of these eight KPIs answers a different question, and a manager who only tracks one or two is working with an incomplete picture. Conversion and traffic explain whether the floor is converting visitors into buyers. ATV and UPT explain what's driving the size of each sale. Sell-through, turn, and GMROI explain whether the inventory investment itself is working. Shrinkage and retention explain whether the store is protecting what it has and keeping the clients it's already earned. This course's remaining modules build directly on this vocabulary: Module 2 goes deeper on conversion and traffic measurement specifically, Module 4 goes deeper on the three inventory KPIs, and Module 7 uses this full vocabulary to actually diagnose a sales miss.

## Practice Exercise

Using your store's most recent week of sales data, calculate conversion rate, ATV, and UPT by hand from the raw transaction count, foot-traffic count (or estimate), and total units sold. Compare your calculated numbers against whatever your POS system reports for the same metrics, and if they don't match, trace the discrepancy — it usually reveals which specific inputs (returns, gift-card redemptions, multi-location counting) your system is handling differently than a manual calculation would.

## Key Takeaways

- Conversion rate (transactions ÷ foot traffic × 100) is the number most directly shaped by staffing, floor coverage, and product knowledge, and should never be read in isolation from foot-traffic volume.
- ATV and UPT decompose total sales into two distinct drivers — bigger individual sales versus more items per visit — that respond to different coaching interventions.
- Sell-through rate (units sold ÷ beginning inventory × 100) flags markdown or reorder decisions early, while inventory turn (COGS ÷ average inventory) measures the ongoing cycling rate of the full inventory investment over a longer horizon.
- GMROI (gross margin dollars ÷ average inventory cost) answers a profitability question that sell-through and turn can't answer alone, and belongs alongside both on the same dashboard.
- Shrinkage rate and customer retention rate are the direct, measurable outputs of operational disciplines built in C11 and C3 respectively, closing the loop between this course's KPI vocabulary and the day-to-day habits that actually produce good numbers.
