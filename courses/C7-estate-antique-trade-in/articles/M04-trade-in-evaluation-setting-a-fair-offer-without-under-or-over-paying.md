# Trade-In Evaluation: Setting a Fair Offer Without Under- or Over-Paying

## Why This Module Matters

Setting a trade-in offer is where estate expertise meets real financial risk. Offer too little, and a knowledgeable client feels disrespected and walks; offer too much, and the store loses money the moment the piece is resold. This module covers the actual mechanics of valuing metal, diamonds, and colored stones for a trade-in offer, and how to communicate that offer honestly and confidently.

## The Core Reality: Retail, Wholesale, and Melt Are Three Different Numbers

Every piece of jewelry has multiple, simultaneously legitimate values, and a trade-in offer needs to be built from the right one — not from what the client originally paid at retail. New jewelry is typically marked up 100-300% over its wholesale/material cost to cover overhead, design, marketing, and margin; a trade-in evaluation works in the opposite direction, starting from wholesale and melt value, not retail. This is the single most important thing to communicate clearly to a client, since the gap between what they paid and what a piece is now worth in trade is almost always explained by this markup structure, not by the piece having somehow "lost value" through any fault of the client's.

## Valuing the Metal: The Melt Value Formula

For the metal component specifically, melt value is a precise, calculable number: **weight (in grams) × purity (as a decimal) × current spot price per gram.** Since gold spot price is quoted per troy ounce (31.1035 grams), the per-gram price is the spot price divided by 31.1035. A 10-gram, 14-karat (58.3% pure) piece, with gold spot at a given price per gram, has a melt value calculated directly from that formula — no guesswork required, since purity and weight are objectively measurable and spot price is public, real-time information. This is the floor value for the metal: a piece is never worth less than its melt value to a buyer willing to simply refine it, though a piece with genuine design, brand, or craftsmanship value is normally worth meaningfully more than melt alone.

## Valuing the Stones: Wholesale, Not Retail

Diamonds and colored stones follow the same wholesale-not-retail logic as the metal, but the gap between retail and resale is even starker for stones than for the piece as a whole. Multiple industry sources converge on a consistent range: a natural diamond typically recovers roughly 20-40% of its original retail price through realistic resale channels, with lab-grown diamonds recovering far less, often close to zero, given their comparatively low and continuously falling wholesale cost. This isn't a flaw in any specific stone — it's a structural feature of the retail-to-wholesale spread that exists the moment a diamond leaves a case, and it applies to every diamond, not just the one in front of you. When evaluating a stone for trade-in, the reference point should be current wholesale pricing (commonly benchmarked against Rapaport for diamonds) for a stone of comparable 4Cs, not the original retail receipt.

## Building the Actual Offer

A complete trade-in offer combines several components, each evaluated on its own terms:

1. **Metal melt value**, calculated directly as above, as the objective floor.
2. **Stone wholesale value**, based on current market pricing for comparable quality, not the original retail price.
3. **Condition adjustments**, which can move the offer in either direction — a piece needing significant repair, missing stones, or heavy damage should see the offer reduced to account for restoration cost; a piece in exceptional condition, a desirable brand, or genuine design/craftsmanship value beyond its raw materials can reasonably support an offer above pure melt-plus-wholesale.
4. **The store's own margin requirement**, since the store still needs to resell the piece (or its refined materials) at a profit — a common rule of thumb cited across sources is offering no more than roughly 50% of what the piece would realistically sell for as pre-owned retail, which itself is typically already discounted relative to new.

The resulting offer will, in the overwhelming majority of cases, be meaningfully lower than the client's original purchase price — and that's expected, not a sign the evaluation is wrong.

## Communicating the Offer Honestly and Confidently

This is where the technical valuation work meets the actual client conversation, and getting the communication right matters as much as getting the math right. The key move is explaining the *structure* behind the number, not just stating the number itself: walk the client through what melt value means, why stones resell below retail, and how the offer was actually built, rather than presenting a number with no explanation and hoping the client doesn't ask. A client who understands why an offer is what it is, even if it's lower than they hoped, is far more likely to accept it as fair than a client handed a number with no context. Being transparent about this process is also simply the honest, correct thing to do — it treats the client as someone capable of understanding real information, not someone to be managed with a vague, unexplained figure.

## A Worked Scenario: The Disappointed Client

A client brings in a ring purchased new for $3,000 two years ago, expecting a trade-in offer close to that figure. After evaluation, the metal and diamond wholesale value support an offer of $900. Rather than simply stating the number, walk through the structure: "I know $900 feels like a big gap from what you paid, so let me explain how we get there. When you bought this new, that $3,000 covered not just the materials but the design, the setting labor, the store's overhead, and their margin — that's normal, and it's true of virtually every piece of new jewelry. When we evaluate a trade-in, we're pricing the actual gold and diamond at today's wholesale value, since that's what we can actually recover. The diamond specifically resells for roughly 20-40% of its original retail price industry-wide, which is exactly the kind of gap you're seeing here. It's not that anything went wrong with your ring — this is just how the math works for every trade-in, on every piece." This turns a number that could feel arbitrary or insulting into one the client can actually understand and evaluate for themselves.

## Key Takeaways

- A trade-in offer is built from wholesale and melt value, not the client's original retail purchase price — new jewelry is typically marked up 100-300% over material cost, and that markup is not recoverable in a trade-in.
- Metal melt value is a precise, calculable number: weight (grams) × purity (decimal) × current spot price per gram — this is the objective floor value for the metal component.
- Diamonds typically resell for roughly 20-40% of original retail price through realistic channels, with lab-grown diamonds recovering far less; colored stones follow the same wholesale-benchmark logic.
- A complete offer combines metal melt value, stone wholesale value, condition adjustments (up or down), and the store's own resale margin requirement — commonly landing at roughly half of realistic pre-owned resale value.
- Explaining the structure behind a trade-in offer, not just stating the number, is both the more honest and the more persuasive approach — a client who understands why a number is what it is can accept it as fair even when it's lower than they hoped.
