# Legal and Compliance Considerations: AML, Stolen-Property Checks, and Buy-Sell Documentation

## Why This Module Matters

Buying jewelry from the public — whether for trade-in or straight purchase — triggers a distinct set of legal obligations that ordinary retail selling doesn't. These aren't abstract compliance boxes to check; they exist specifically to prevent a store from becoming, even unknowingly, a channel for stolen property or money laundering. Understanding these obligations well enough to apply them correctly protects the store, protects law enforcement's ability to recover stolen goods, and protects an innocent client from an unnecessarily suspicious transaction.

## AML Obligations Under the Patriot Act

Under the USA PATRIOT Act's implementing regulations (31 CFR 1027.210), jewelry businesses that meet a specific dollar threshold are legally defined as "dealers in jewels, precious metals, or precious stones" and must maintain a written anti-money laundering (AML) program. The threshold is precise: a business must establish an AML program if, in the prior calendar or tax year, it either (1) sold more than $50,000 in jewels, precious metals, and precious stones where those materials comprised 50% or more of the jewelry's selling price, or (2) purchased more than $50,000 in such materials from the general public under the same 50%-of-price condition. Both the sale-side and purchase-side thresholds matter specifically for estate and trade-in work, since a store actively buying from the public is exactly the scenario this regulation targets. A required AML program must include, at minimum, internal policies and procedures, a designated compliance officer, ongoing employee training, and independent periodic review — note specifically that the federal rule requires the program itself but does not separately require filing Suspicious Activity Reports with FinCEN, a nuance worth knowing precisely rather than over- or under-stating the requirement.

## State and Local Secondhand Dealer Laws: Holding Periods and Police Reporting

Separately from federal AML law, most states and many municipalities have their own secondhand dealer or pawnbroker statutes that apply directly to a store buying jewelry from the public, and these vary considerably by jurisdiction — what follows illustrates the general pattern, not a universal rule, and local law must always be confirmed directly.

- **Transaction reporting to police**, often within 24 hours of purchase, is a common requirement (California's statute specifically requires this, and many jurisdictions use an electronic reporting platform for it).
- **Holding periods** — a mandatory window during which a purchased item cannot be resold, altered, or melted down — are nearly universal in the jurisdictions reviewed, though the specific length varies meaningfully: Florida requires 30 days specifically for precious metals, gemstones, jewelry, and antiques (15 days for other secondhand goods); California requires 30 days; a Wisconsin municipal example specifically requires just 48 hours before gold or silver bullion can be melted, on top of its general 15-day holding period. This variance means a store operating across jurisdictions, or simply operating in a single jurisdiction, needs to know its own specific local requirement precisely, not assume a number from a different state applies.
- **Police hold orders**: even after a standard holding period expires, a law enforcement officer with probable cause that an item is stolen can place an additional hold order on it (commonly up to 90 days, sometimes extendable further by a court), during which the dealer must produce the item for law enforcement and cannot dispose of it regardless of the standard holding period's status.
- **Record retention**: multi-year retention of transaction records (three years is specifically cited in at least one state's statute) is a common requirement, separate from the holding period itself.

## Stolen-Property Databases: LeadsOnline and Similar Systems

**LeadsOnline** (formally styled L.E.A.D.S. Online) is the dominant electronic reporting platform used across the secondhand-goods, pawn, and precious-metals-buying industry specifically to satisfy the police-reporting requirements described above. Transaction details — the seller's identification, item descriptions, photographs — are uploaded to the platform, which law enforcement agencies across all 50 states can search against active stolen-property reports and the National Crime Information Center's (NCIC) nightly stolen-property feed. For a store's compliance purposes, this means the reporting obligation isn't just a bureaucratic formality — it's the actual mechanism by which a genuinely stolen piece gets identified and recovered, sometimes well after the original purchase transaction. Consistent, accurate, and prompt use of whatever reporting system a jurisdiction requires is a genuinely important line of defense, both for the store's legal standing and for actually helping recover stolen property when it does pass through.

## Buy-Sell Documentation: What a Complete Record Should Include

Beyond the specific regulatory reporting requirements above, a complete internal purchase record for any piece bought from the public should include: the seller's verified government-issued ID (name, address, ID number, and, where required, a photograph or thumbprint); a detailed, specific description of the item (not a generic category, but the actual identifying details — exactly the kind of documentation Module 3 already established as good attribution practice); photographs taken before any cleaning, repair, or alteration; the purchase price and payment method; and the date and time of the transaction. This record serves double duty: it satisfies the legal reporting and retention obligations described above, and it protects the store's own position if a piece's provenance is ever later questioned.

## Cash Transaction Reporting: IRS Form 8300

Separately from AML and secondhand-dealer law, any business (including a jewelry store) that receives more than $10,000 in cash in a single transaction, or in related transactions, must file IRS Form 8300 reporting that transaction. This applies to a store both when selling to a client and, relevant to this module, potentially when making a large cash payout for a purchased or traded-in piece, depending on the specific payment structure used. This is a distinct federal requirement from the AML program obligation described above and should not be confused with it — they serve different regulatory purposes and have different triggering thresholds.

## A Worked Scenario: The New Client with an Unusual Piece

A client the store has never seen before brings in a high-value, unbranded diamond piece for an outright cash sale, offers no explanation of its provenance, and seems eager to complete the transaction quickly. None of this alone proves anything wrong, but it's exactly the kind of situation where following every procedural step matters most: verify and record the seller's ID precisely, take detailed photographs and a specific written description before any further handling, complete whatever local police-reporting requirement applies (through LeadsOnline or the jurisdiction's specified platform) within the required timeframe, and observe the full mandatory holding period before any resale or alteration — without shortcuts, regardless of how routine the transaction otherwise feels. If anything about the piece or the circumstances raises genuine concern beyond ordinary caution, escalating to a manager before completing the purchase is the appropriate response, consistent with Module 6's documentation-first, non-accusatory discipline.

## Practice Exercise

Practice walking through the complete documentation checklist (ID verification, item description, photographs, price/payment record, date/time) for a hypothetical purchase, then research your own jurisdiction's specific secondhand-dealer holding period and police-reporting requirement, since this module's ranges illustrate the general pattern but cannot substitute for confirming your store's actual local obligation.

## Key Takeaways

- Federal AML law (31 CFR 1027.210) requires a written anti-money laundering program for jewelry businesses that buy or sell more than $50,000 annually in precious metals/gems where those materials comprise 50%+ of the transaction price — this applies specifically to active purchasing from the public, exactly this module's context.
- State and local secondhand-dealer laws typically require prompt police reporting (often within 24 hours) and a mandatory holding period before resale or alteration (commonly 15-30 days, though this varies meaningfully by jurisdiction and must be confirmed locally).
- LeadsOnline and similar platforms are the standard mechanism for satisfying police-reporting requirements, connecting a store's purchase records to nationwide stolen-property databases including the NCIC feed.
- A complete buy-sell record includes verified seller ID, a specific item description, pre-alteration photographs, price/payment details, and transaction date/time — this satisfies legal obligations and protects the store if provenance is later questioned.
- IRS Form 8300 (required for cash transactions over $10,000) is a distinct federal requirement from AML program obligations, with its own separate trigger and purpose — don't conflate the two.
