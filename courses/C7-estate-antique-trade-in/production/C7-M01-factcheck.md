# C7-M01 Fact-Check: The Secondary Market

## Verification Status: Strong cross-source agreement on core definitions and trade-in mechanics; genuine variance in market-size figures across research firms

## Claims Reviewed

**1. Estate jewelry defined as ownership history (previously owned, any age), attributed to GIA's own definition.**
Status: CONFIRMED CONSISTENT across Charles Schwartz & Son (which directly attributes this definition to GIA: "estate jewelry encompasses any previously owned piece, regardless of provenance, age, or pedigree"), Estate Diamond Jewelry, and Hausers Jewelers, all independently describing estate as an ownership-history concept, not an age-based one.

**2. Antique jewelry defined as 100+ years old.**
Status: CONFIRMED CONSISTENT AND UNANIMOUS across every source reviewed (Charles Schwartz & Son, Argo & Lehne, Estate Diamond Jewelry, Hausers Jewelers) — this is the single most consistently agreed-upon specific figure in this module.

**3. Vintage jewelry defined as roughly 20-100 years old, with some variance in the lower boundary (20 years vs. 50 years).**
Status: MODERATE VARIANCE across sources — Charles Schwartz & Son states 20-100 years "with most professionals preferring 50 years" as a practical cutoff; Argo & Lehne states "over 20 years"; Hausers Jewelers notes "many experts use 50 years as a rule of thumb... however, some define vintage as anything over about 20-30 years old." This module presents the range rather than a single figure, consistent with this genuine variance.

**4. Vintage-style defined as newly made, older-aesthetic-inspired pieces, not actually old or previously owned.**
Status: CONFIRMED CONSISTENT — Estate Diamond Jewelry explicitly defines this term and distinguishes it from actual vintage; consistent with general industry usage across other sources reviewed.

**5. Trade-in program mechanics (full original-purchase-price credit toward a higher-value upgrade, with a minimum threshold).**
Status: CONFIRMED CONSISTENT across four major chain retailers' own program pages: Jared ("trade-in amount at the full value of the original jewelry purchase price," new item "at least double the trade-in value"), Zales ("full value of the original purchase price"), Kay (matching Jared's language, same corporate parent), and Shane Co. ("full credit of your original items... as long as it's at least $1 more"). Specific upgrade-threshold requirements vary slightly by retailer (Jared requires double the value; Shane Co. requires only $1 more), which this module does not present as a universal figure.

**6. Secondary/resale jewelry market size and growth rate.**
Status: SUBSTANTIAL VARIANCE across market-research sources. Verified Market Reports cites $32.1B (2025) growing to $66.8B (2033) at 9.7% CAGR; Business Research Insights cites a much smaller $2.7B (2026) to $6.76B (2035) at 10.5% CAGR (with a separate figure in the same report of $2.21B in 2024 to $5.53B by 2033); Markwide Research cites $31.4B (2026) to $72.24B (2035) at 9.70% CAGR. The wide variance between the ~$2-7B range and the ~$31-72B range suggests these firms are scoping "used jewelry resale service market" differently (e.g., platform/service revenue vs. total transaction value). This module presents the general growth trend and approximate CAGR range (9-10.5%) rather than a single precise dollar figure, given this apparent methodological inconsistency across sources.

**7. Fine jewelry as a fast-growing category within broader secondhand luxury resale.**
Status: Sourced from Designer Signals' 2026 US Luxury Resale Market Signals report (fine jewelry references alongside watches and iconic handbags as strong-demand categories) and 25hours' secondhand jewelry statistics report (projecting the secondhand market broadly to reach $350B by 2028, citing fine jewelry as resale's fastest-growing category). These are industry-analysis sources rather than primary government/regulatory data, and are presented as directional market commentary rather than precise verified figures.
