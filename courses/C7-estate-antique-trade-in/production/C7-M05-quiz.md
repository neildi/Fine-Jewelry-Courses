# C7-M05 Quiz: Appraisals for Estate and Trade-In

**Instructions:** Answer all questions. This module's quiz emphasizes correctly matching appraisal type to purpose, not memorizing exact percentage ranges.

---

**1. What is the correct ordering of the three value types by typical magnitude, from highest to lowest?**
A) Fair market value > liquidation value > retail replacement value
B) Retail replacement value > fair market value > liquidation value
C) Liquidation value > retail replacement value > fair market value
D) All three values are always identical

**Correct answer: B**
Explanation: RRV includes retail markup not recoverable elsewhere; FMV reflects an orderly secondary-market sale; liquidation value reflects a forced, urgent sale — each step down reflects a specific, explainable discount.

---

**2. What is the IRS's specific legal definition of fair market value?**
A) The cost to replace an item new at a retail store
B) The price at which property would change hands between a willing buyer and a willing seller, neither being under any compulsion to buy or sell, with both having reasonable knowledge of relevant facts
C) Whatever the original purchase price was
D) The lowest possible price an item could sell for in an emergency

**Correct answer: B**
Explanation: This is a specific, legally defined standard, not a general description — it requires an orderly transaction and reasonably informed parties on both sides, distinguishing it clearly from both retail replacement value and liquidation value.

---

**3. Which appraisal type is legally required for estate settlement, probate, and IRS Form 706 estate tax reporting?**
A) Retail replacement value
B) Fair market value
C) Liquidation value
D) Any appraisal type is acceptable for this purpose

**Correct answer: B**
Explanation: Probate courts and the IRS specifically require fair market value for these purposes, since it reflects the estate's actual secondary-market worth rather than an inflated new-replacement cost.

---

**4. Why would using retail replacement value instead of fair market value for an estate tax filing be a problem?**
A) It wouldn't be a problem; the two values are interchangeable
B) RRV is intentionally the highest of the three values, so using it would overstate the estate's actual value and could unfairly inflate the estate's tax liability
C) RRV is always lower than FMV
D) Estate tax filings never require any specific value type

**Correct answer: B**
Explanation: Since RRV includes retail markup that doesn't reflect the estate's actual realizable value, using it for tax purposes would create an artificially high taxable estate value.

---

**5. In what context is liquidation value the appropriate appraisal type?**
A) Standard homeowners insurance coverage
B) Genuinely urgent, forced-sale contexts such as bankruptcy proceedings or court-ordered rapid sales
C) A routine, unhurried resale to a private individual
D) Liquidation value is never actually used for any real purpose

**Correct answer: B**
Explanation: Liquidation value specifically accounts for the price discount that urgency and limited marketing time create, making it the correct standard for genuinely forced or time-pressured sale scenarios.

---

**6. How do divorce and equitable distribution appraisals typically work, per this module?**
A) They always use retail replacement value with no exceptions
B) They vary by jurisdiction and circumstance — many use fair market value, some specifically request a "marketable cash value" appraisal, and a lender or trustee may separately require forced liquidation value
C) Divorce proceedings never require any jewelry appraisal
D) Only liquidation value is ever acceptable in divorce cases

**Correct answer: B**
Explanation: Unlike insurance (always RRV) or estate tax (always FMV), divorce appraisal type shows genuine variation depending on jurisdiction and the specific parties' needs.

---

**7. Why is a trade-in offer (from Module 4) conceptually related to, but not a substitute for, a formal liquidation-value appraisal?**
A) They are functionally identical documents that can always be used interchangeably
B) A trade-in offer is typically an internal buying calculation, while a formal liquidation appraisal is a written, credentialed document appropriate for specific legal or financial purposes — the two share similar underlying logic but serve different functions
C) Trade-in offers are always higher than liquidation appraisals
D) There is no meaningful relationship between the two concepts at all

**Correct answer: B**
Explanation: Recognizing this overlap without conflating the two prevents a client from mistakenly relying on an informal trade-in quote for a purpose that actually requires a formal, credentialed appraisal.

---

**8. In the worked scenario, why does the associate ask the executor a clarifying question before producing any appraisal?**
A) Because the appraisal type doesn't actually matter for this client
B) Because using the wrong appraisal type (e.g., retail replacement value instead of fair market value) for an estate filing could create real tax or legal complications for the client
C) Because the associate wants to delay helping the client unnecessarily
D) Because estate filings never require a specific appraisal type

**Correct answer: B**
Explanation: Proactively confirming the appraisal's actual purpose protects the client from a document that might not hold up for their specific legal filing, which is a genuinely important professional safeguard.
