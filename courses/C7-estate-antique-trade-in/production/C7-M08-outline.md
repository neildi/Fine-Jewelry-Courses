# C7-M08 Outline: Legal and Compliance Considerations: AML, Stolen-Property Checks, and Buy-Sell Documentation

## Module Metadata
- Course: C7 - Estate, Antique & Trade-In
- Module: 8 of 10
- Persona: P2
- Working title: Legal and Compliance Considerations: AML, Stolen-Property Checks, and Buy-Sell Documentation
- Learning objective: Associates can explain the federal AML dealer threshold and program requirements, describe the general pattern of state/local secondhand-dealer reporting and holding-period obligations (while knowing these must be confirmed locally), explain LeadsOnline's role in stolen-property recovery, produce a complete buy-sell documentation record, and correctly distinguish Form 8300 cash-reporting obligations from AML program requirements.
- Terminal outcome tie-in: "Apply the legal and compliance obligations specific to buying pre-owned jewelry from the public (AML considerations, stolen-property checks, documentation)."

## Section Map

1. **Why This Module Matters** — Frames these obligations as existing to prevent the store from unknowingly channeling stolen property or laundered funds, not as bureaucratic boxes to check.
2. **AML Obligations Under the Patriot Act** — The specific $50,000/50%-of-price dual threshold (sale-side and purchase-side); required program components; the SAR-filing nuance.
3. **State and Local Secondhand Dealer Laws: Holding Periods and Police Reporting** — 24-hour reporting norms; holding-period variance by jurisdiction (FL 30 days for precious items, CA 30 days, WI municipal 48hr bullion-melt rule); police hold orders; record retention.
4. **Stolen-Property Databases: LeadsOnline and Similar Systems** — How the platform works; connection to NCIC; why consistent, accurate use matters beyond mere compliance.
5. **Buy-Sell Documentation: What a Complete Record Should Include** — ID verification, specific item description, pre-alteration photographs, price/payment, date/time.
6. **Cash Transaction Reporting: IRS Form 8300** — $10,000 cash threshold; distinct purpose and trigger from AML program obligations.
7. **A Worked Scenario: The New Client with an Unusual Piece** — Illustrative synthesis of following full procedural discipline on an ordinary-but-attention-warranting transaction.
8. **Practice Exercise** — Documentation-checklist practice plus jurisdiction-specific research task.
9. **Key Takeaways** — Five-point recap.

## Production Notes
- The federal AML dealer threshold ($50,000 in prior calendar/tax year, 50%+ of selling/purchase price in covered materials, applying to BOTH sale-side and purchase-side thresholds) is sourced directly from a PRIMARY FEDERAL SOURCE: FinCEN's own official FAQ guidance document (faq060305.pdf), which states this exact dual threshold verbatim. Cross-corroborated by Mordfin Group's jewelers'-guide-to-AML-laws PDF, which restates the same threshold in nearly identical terms, and by the federal regulation text itself (31 CFR 1027.210, accessed via Cornell Law's LII), which confirms the written-program requirement.
- The nuance that the federal rule requires an AML program but does NOT separately require filing Suspicious Activity Reports with FinCEN is sourced directly from FinCEN's own FAQ document, a primary source, stated explicitly ("does not require dealers to file reports of suspicious activity with FinCEN").
- State/local secondhand-dealer holding-period and reporting requirements are sourced directly from PRIMARY LEGAL SOURCES: Florida Statutes §538.06 (Justia, official statute text, 30-day holding period for precious metals/gemstones/jewelry/antiques specifically, with a separate 90-day police hold-order provision and 3-year record retention requirement), California AB-391/Business and Professions Code §21628 (24-hour/daily reporting requirement), a Los Angeles Police Commission secondhand-jewelry-dealer permit document (90-day police hold-order provision, consistent with Florida's), a Wisconsin municipal code example (Brookfield, 15-day general holding period plus a specific 48-hour rule before bullion melt-down), and a California municipal example (San Pablo, 30-day holding period tied to the transaction-report date). This module explicitly and repeatedly notes that these figures illustrate a general pattern across jurisdictions reviewed, NOT a universal rule, since holding periods and reporting timelines vary by state and even by municipality, and the specific examples cited (FL, CA, WI, LA) should not be assumed to apply to any other jurisdiction without direct confirmation.
- LeadsOnline's function and connection to the NCIC stolen-property feed is sourced directly from LeadsOnline's own official product pages (leadsonline.com/leads, LO_INTEL product documentation PDF), a primary source directly describing its own platform's capabilities and law-enforcement integration, cross-corroborated by WSBT's news report on a municipal LeadsOnline reporting mandate and PawnSmarts' software-integration page describing standard industry LeadsOnline export functionality.
- IRS Form 8300's $10,000 cash-transaction threshold is well-established, standard federal tax-compliance knowledge, consistent with this course's own C6 Module 12 content on FTC/CFPB primary-source financial regulation; this module explicitly distinguishes it from the AML program obligation, which is a genuinely important and sometimes-confused distinction.
- The buy-sell documentation checklist (ID verification, specific item description, pre-alteration photographs, price/payment, date/time) synthesizes the specific record-keeping requirements found across the state/local statutes reviewed with Module 3's already-established good-documentation practice, applied here specifically to satisfy legal requirements rather than purely attribution purposes.
- The worked scenario and practice exercise are ORIGINAL illustrative syntheses, not externally sourced cases.
