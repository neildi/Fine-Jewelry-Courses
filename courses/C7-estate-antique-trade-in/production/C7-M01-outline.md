# C7-M01 Outline: The Secondary Market: Why Estate, Antique, and Trade-In Are Distinct Categories

## Module Metadata
- Course: C7 - Estate, Antique & Trade-In
- Module: 1 of 10
- Persona: P1
- Working title: The Secondary Market: Why Estate, Antique, and Trade-In Are Distinct Categories
- Learning objective: Associates can precisely define and distinguish estate, vintage, antique, and vintage-style jewelry, explain trade-in as a distinct transaction structure, and articulate why the secondary jewelry market represents a genuine, growing business opportunity rather than a niche side business.

## Section Map

1. **Why This Module Matters** — Frames precise terminology as foundational to pricing, marketing, and client trust.
2. **The Four Terms, Defined Precisely** — Estate (ownership history), vintage (~20-100 years), antique (100+ years), vintage-style (newly made, older aesthetic).
3. **Trade-In: A Different Transaction Entirely** — Trade-in as a transaction structure, not a classification; major-retailer program mechanics (full-value credit toward an upgrade).
4. **The Secondary Market Is Real, and It's Growing** — Market-size and growth-rate data; fine jewelry as a fast-growing resale category.
5. **Why This Distinction Matters for the Rest of This Course** — Explicit forward references to Modules 2, 5, and 9.
6. **A Worked Scenario: The Misused Word** — Illustrative synthesis of precise, respectful terminology correction.
7. **Practice Exercise** — Sorting exercise across five hypothetical pieces, including a red-flag scenario foreshadowing Module 6 (authentication).
8. **Key Takeaways** — Five-point recap.

## Production Notes
- The four-term definitional framework (estate/vintage/antique/vintage-style) is STRONGLY CROSS-CORROBORATED across six independent sources (Charles Schwartz & Son, Argo & Lehne, Estate Diamond Jewelry, Hausers Jewelers, and consistent with GIA's own definition of estate jewelry as cited by Charles Schwartz). All sources agree on the antique=100+ years threshold precisely; vintage's lower age boundary shows minor variance (20 years cited by some, 50 years preferred by others) which this module presents as a range rather than a single figure.
- GIA's own definition of "estate jewelry" (any previously owned piece regardless of age/pedigree) is cited via Charles Schwartz & Son's article, which directly attributes this definition to GIA.
- Trade-in program mechanics (full original-purchase-price credit toward a higher-value upgrade) are corroborated across four major chain retailers' own program pages: Jared, Zales, Kay, and Shane Co., all describing highly similar structures (full value credit, minimum upgrade threshold).
- Secondary market size/growth figures show some variance across sources (used jewelry resale market estimates range from ~$2.2B to ~$32B depending on market-research firm and methodology, with CAGR estimates clustering around 9-10.5%) — this module presents the general growth trend and CAGR range rather than a single precise dollar figure, given this variance likely reflects different market-scope definitions across research firms.
- The broader secondhand-luxury market context (fine jewelry as a fast-growing resale category alongside watches/handbags) is sourced from Designer Signals' 2026 US Luxury Resale Market report and 25hours' secondhand jewelry statistics report.
- The worked scenario and practice exercise are ORIGINAL illustrative syntheses, not externally sourced cases.
